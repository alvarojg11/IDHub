# Enterovirus A71 Rhombencephalitis in Adults: Evidence-Based Clinical Review with Southeast Asian Context

---

## 1. Clinical Presentation in Adults

### 1.1 Overview and Pathophysiology

EV-A71 is a single-stranded positive-sense RNA virus of the *Picornaviridae* family, species *Enterovirus A*. It is one of the major causative agents of hand, foot, and mouth disease (HFMD), and the most neurovirulent enterovirus since poliovirus eradication. The virus uses scavenger receptor class B member 2 (SCARB2) as its primary cellular receptor, which is expressed on neurons and epithelial cells — explaining its neurotropism (Kobayashi & Koike, *J Biomed Sci*, 2020; PMID: 31924205).

In vitro and animal models suggest that CNS damage results not from classical viral cytopathic effect but from an **activated cerebral immune response**: elevated IL-6 and catecholamines (adrenaline/norepinephrine) mediate brainstem dysfunction rather than direct viral replication (Feng et al., *Lab Invest*, 2020; PMID: 31857694).

### 1.2 Classic Triad: Myoclonus, Ataxia, and Cranial Nerve Palsies

The sentinel description of EV-A71 rhombencephalitis comes from the 1998 Taiwan epidemic. Huang et al. (*NEJM*, 1999; PMID: 10498488) described a three-tier clinical grading system in 41 children:

| Grade | Features | Prognosis |
|-------|----------|-----------|
| **Grade I** | Myoclonic jerks, tremor, ataxia, or both | Good — self-limited |
| **Grade II** | Myoclonus + cranial nerve involvement (CN VI, VII, IX, X, XII palsies) | Intermediate — requires ICU |
| **Grade III** | Rapid onset: respiratory distress, cyanosis, poor perfusion, shock, coma, loss of doll's eye reflex, apnea | Poor — 71% mortality within hours |

**Key neurologic findings across grades:**
- **Myoclonus**: The most common initial neurologic sign, present in >50% of cases. Typically manifests as generalized or multifocal myoclonic jerks, often sleep-related initially (Huang et al., 1999; Yang et al., *BMC Infect Dis*, 2017; PMID: 28212620).
- **Ataxia**: Truncal and gait ataxia, present in ~39% of cases (Lee et al., *J Korean Med Sci*, 2016; PMID: 27051240).
- **Tremor**: Intention tremor, present in ~36% (Lee et al., 2016).
- **Cranial nerve palsies**: Facial weakness (CN VII), ophthalmoplegia (CN III, IV, VI), dysphagia (CN IX, X), tongue deviation (CN XII). Ptosis is a frequent finding.
- **Opsoclonus-myoclonus**: Rare but reported; resolves with IVIG (Sahly et al., *Child Neurol Open*, 2017; PMID: 28975137).

### 1.3 Differences in Adults vs. Children

Adult EV-A71 CNS infection is **rare but increasingly recognized**, particularly in immunocompromised hosts. Key differences:

1. **Immunocompromised predilection**: The majority of reported adult cases occur in patients with impaired humoral immunity — particularly those on **rituximab** (B-cell depletion) or post-hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT).
   - Kapadia et al. (*J Neuroimmunol*, 2021; PMID: 34214953): 25-year-old woman on rituximab for rheumatoid arthritis who developed meningoencephalitis and acute flaccid myelitis. CSF and stool RT-PCR confirmed EV-A71. Treated with IVIG and fluoxetine → remarkable recovery.
   - Ueda et al. (*J Infect Chemother*, 2025; PMID: 39870240): 63-year-old woman post-allo-HSCT for MDS who developed EV-A71 meningoencephalitis. Detected by BioFire FilmArray ME panel. Despite IVIG, she did not fully recover; required tracheotomy. Fatal outcome.

2. **Preceding or concurrent HFMD may be absent**: Adults may not display the characteristic vesicular rash of HFMD, making diagnosis more challenging. Close contact with an infected child is often the only epidemiological clue.

3. **Phenotype may mimic other conditions**: Adult presentations can include:
   - Disturbance of consciousness
   - Ptosis and ophthalmoplegia
   - Limb weakness (acute flaccid paralysis/myelitis)
   - Meningoencephalitis pattern rather than isolated brainstem syndrome

4. **CSF pleocytosis may be absent**: In the immunocompromised adult (Ueda et al., 2025), CSF showed **no pleocytosis** despite confirmed EV-A71 in CSF, highlighting the blunted inflammatory response.

5. **Adult seroprevalence provides partial protection**: Pre-epidemic seroprevalence studies in Taiwan showed EV-A71 neutralizing antibody rates of 60–70% in adults and children >6 years (Chang, *Pediatr Neonatol*, 2008; PMID: 19054914). This explains why adults are largely protected during epidemics — but waning immunity or immunosuppression can permit severe disease.

### 1.4 Autonomic Nervous System Dysregulation

A critical feature of severe EV-A71 rhombencephalitis is **autonomic nervous system (ANS) dysregulation** preceding cardiopulmonary collapse:

- Tachycardia (universal)
- Systemic hypertension (followed by hypotensive shock)
- Profuse sweating
- Cold extremities with poor perfusion
- "Catecholamine storm": markedly elevated plasma norepinephrine and epinephrine

(Fu et al., *Arch Dis Child*, 2004; PMID: 15033850; Wang & Liu, *Expert Rev Anti Infect Ther*, 2009; PMID: 19681701)

### 1.5 Neurogenic Pulmonary Edema

The leading cause of death in severe EV-A71 infection. Mechanism:
1. Brainstem lesions → autonomic dysregulation
2. Catecholamine surge → increased pulmonary vascular permeability
3. Pulmonary hemorrhage/edema
4. Rapidly progressive respiratory failure

(Finsterer, *Eur Neurol*, 2019; PMID: 31117074; Xu et al., *PLoS One*, 2015; PMID: 25767882)

## 2. MRI Findings

### 2.1 Characteristic MRI Pattern

MRI is the neuroimaging gold standard for EV-A71 rhombencephalitis. The hallmark finding is **T2/FLAIR hyperintensity** in the **posterior (dorsal) brainstem**.

**Core MRI features** (from the largest series):

| Location | Frequency | Signal Characteristics |
|----------|-----------|----------------------|
| **Posterior pons (pontine tegmentum)** | Most common (~70%) | T2/FLAIR hyperintense, T1 hypointense |
| **Dorsal medulla oblongata** | ~76% (35/46 in Liu et al.) | T2/FLAIR hyperintense — **strongest predictor of poor outcome** |
| **Dentate nuclei (bilateral cerebellar)** | ~15–37% | T2 hyperintense, may occur without cerebellar symptoms |
| **Midbrain** | ~22% | T2 hyperintense |
| **Cervical spinal cord (anterior horns)** | Variable, with AFP | T2 hyperintense, often with root enhancement |

(Huang et al., *NEJM*, 1999; PMID: 10498488; Zeng et al., *PLoS One*, 2016; PMID: 27798639; Liu et al., *Pediatr Infect Dis J*, 2019; PMID: 29624567; Chen et al., *Int J Clin Exp Med*, 2014; PMID: 25356127)

### 2.2 Pathognomonic vs. Suggestive Patterns

**Highly suggestive (near-pathognomonic for EV-A71):**
- Bilateral, symmetric T2/FLAIR hyperintensity in the **dorsal pontine tegmentum** extending to the **dorsal medulla**
- Concurrent involvement of the **bilateral dentate nuclei**
- **Segmental distribution** along the brainstem, consistent with retrograde axonal transport from the pharynx via the vagus nerve (Li et al., *J Neurovirol*, 2019; PMID: 30734196)

**Suggestive but non-specific:**
- Isolated pontine lesions (can occur in Listeria, HSV, ADEM)
- Midbrain involvement without brainstem-predominant pattern
- Spinal cord anterior horn involvement (can mimic poliovirus, EV-D68)

**Classification by disease stage** (Zeng et al., *PLoS One*, 2016; PMID: 27798639):
- **Acute stage**: Foci of prolonged T1/T2 signal, with or without contrast enhancement. Novel pattern: T2 hypointensity with enhancement in some cases.
- **Convalescent stage**: Persistent T1/T2 prolongation without enhancement
- **Long-term follow-up** (31 patients): 26 normalized, 3 had persistent FLAIR hyperintensity, 2 showed encephalomalacia

### 2.3 Outcome Prediction by MRI

Liu et al. (*Pediatr Infect Dis J*, 2019; PMID: 29624567) demonstrated:
- **Dorsal medulla oblongata involvement** was the single most powerful predictor of poor outcome (90.5% accuracy)
- **Multiple area involvement** (brainstem + cerebellum + midbrain) correlated with worse prognosis
- Patients with isolated pontine abnormalities recovered faster than those with multi-focal brainstem disease
- Glasgow Coma Scale score combined with MRI improved prognostic accuracy

## 3. Diagnostic Testing

### 3.1 Cerebrospinal Fluid (CSF) Analysis

| Parameter | Typical Finding |
|-----------|----------------|
| **WBC** | Mild lymphocytic pleocytosis (10–200 cells/μL). May be **absent in immunocompromised adults**. |
| **Protein** | Normal to mildly elevated (typically <100 mg/dL) |
| **Glucose** | Normal (distinguishes from bacterial etiologies) |

- 86.7% of pediatric cases show CSF pleocytosis (Lee et al., *J Korean Med Sci*, 2016; PMID: 27051240)
- **Important caveat**: In adults on rituximab or post-HSCT, CSF pleocytosis may be absent despite active CNS infection (Ueda et al., 2025; PMID: 39870240)

### 3.2 Molecular Detection (PCR)

**RT-PCR is the diagnostic gold standard.** Multiple specimen sites should be tested:

| Specimen | Sensitivity | Notes |
|----------|-------------|-------|
| **Throat swab** | Highest in early disease | Viral shedding for 1–2 weeks |
| **Rectal swab / stool** | High — often positive even when CSF negative | Prolonged shedding (weeks) |
| **CSF** | Variable — lower than respiratory/GI specimens | Most specific for CNS invasion |
| **Vesicle fluid** | Moderate | Useful when HFMD rash present |
| **Blood** | Low | Limited utility |
| **Nasopharyngeal swab** | Moderate | Important in adults without skin lesions |

**Key recommendations**:
- Test **multiple sites** simultaneously (CSF + throat + rectal swab) to maximize yield (Kapadia et al., 2021; PMID: 34214953)
- **BioFire FilmArray Meningitis/Encephalitis panel** can detect Enterovirus genus from CSF rapidly; speciation requires referral laboratory sequencing (Ueda et al., 2025)
- VP1 gene sequencing is used for genotyping (subgenogroup identification — most commonly C4, B5 in Southeast Asia)

### 3.3 Serology

| Test | Utility | Limitations |
|------|---------|-------------|
| **EV-A71 IgM** | Suggests recent infection | Cross-reactivity with other enteroviruses |
| **Paired IgG (acute + convalescent)** | ≥4-fold rise confirms infection | Requires 2–4 weeks for convalescent sample |
| **Neutralizing antibody assay** | Gold standard serological test | Labor-intensive; not routinely available |

- Seroprevalence studies show ~60–70% of adults in endemic areas have neutralizing antibodies (Chang, 2008; PMID: 19054914)
- In Vietnam, adults 25–34 years had highest baseline GMT of EV-A71 neutralizing antibodies (Hoang et al., *Biomed Res Int*, 2020; PMID: 33274234)

### 3.4 Brain Biopsy

Brain biopsy is **almost never performed** for EV-A71 encephalitis specifically. It may be considered when:
- Diagnosis remains elusive despite extensive non-invasive workup
- Alternative diagnoses (lymphoma, autoimmune encephalitis) cannot be excluded
- The patient is deteriorating despite empiric therapy

Autopsy series from fatal cases show: **neuronal necrosis, softening, perivascular cuffing, neuronophagia** in the brainstem (Yang et al., 2017; PMID: 28212620).

### 3.5 Differential Diagnosis

| Condition | Key Distinguishing Features |
|-----------|---------------------------|
| **HSV encephalitis** | Temporal lobe predominance; CSF PCR positive for HSV; ~50% have supratentorial involvement when brainstem affected; acyclovir-responsive. Mortality 22% with acyclovir vs 75% without (Jubelt et al., *Curr Neurol Neurosci Rep*, 2011; PMID: 21956758) |
| **Listeria monocytogenes brainstem encephalitis** | Most common cause of infectious rhombencephalitis in adults; biphasic illness (flu-like → brainstem); CSF/blood cultures positive; rhombencephalitis in ~9% of CNS listeriosis. Ampicillin is treatment. (Jubelt et al., 2011) |
| **ADEM** | Multifocal demyelination with white matter predominance; typically follows infection/vaccination by 1–4 weeks; children > adults; MRI shows larger, asymmetric lesions with demyelination pattern |
| **Bickerstaff brainstem encephalitis** | Autoimmune; preceded by infection; characterized by external ophthalmoplegia, ataxia, and impaired consciousness; anti-GQ1b antibodies positive; overlaps with Miller Fisher syndrome |
| **Japanese encephalitis** | Endemic in Southeast Asia; thalamic involvement on MRI (bateral thalamic T2 hyperintensity); basal ganglia and substantia nigra involvement; CSF JEV IgM; seasonal (monsoon) |
| **West Nile virus** | Flavivirus; acute flaccid paralysis + meningitis/encephalitis; anterior horn cell involvement on MRI; IgM in CSF; no specific treatment |
| **Poliomyelitis / EV-D68** | Anterior horn cell involvement causing AFP; EV-D68 associated with acute flaccid myelitis; distinguish by PCR and VP1 sequencing |
| **Neuro-Behçet disease** | Recurrent; brainstem involvement common; oral/genital ulcers; uveitis; elevated CSF protein without infection |

**Empiric treatment recommendation** (Jubelt et al., 2011; PMID: 21956758):
> Start **ampicillin + acyclovir** empirically for all rhombencephalitis cases after obtaining CSF/blood for cultures and PCR. Adjust based on MRI, culture, PCR, and antibody results.

## 4. Treatment

### 4.1 Supportive Care (Mainstay)

No FDA-approved specific antiviral exists for EV-A71. **Supportive care remains the foundation** of management:

- **ICU monitoring** for all Grade II/III patients (autonomic instability can progress within hours)
- **Early intubation** for cranial nerve involvement or respiratory compromise (Chen et al., *Medicina*, 2020; PMID: 32344662): Grade II patients intubated early had favorable outcomes
- **Hemodynamic support**: Vasopressors for shock; careful fluid management to avoid exacerbating pulmonary edema
- **Mechanical ventilation**: For neurogenic pulmonary edema, apnea, or coma

### 4.2 Intravenous Immunoglobulin (IVIG)

**Evidence and rationale:**
- IVIG may neutralize viremia, modulate cytokine storm, and provide passive immunity
- **Adult case evidence**:
  - Pham et al. (*Diagn Microbiol Infect Dis*, 2025; PMID: 40209324): 21-year-old Vietnamese woman with EV-A71 encephalomyelitis → **single dose IVIG → full recovery**
  - Kapadia et al. (2021; PMID: 34214953): 25-year-old on rituximab → IVIG + fluoxetine → remarkable improvement
  - Ueda et al. (2025; PMID: 39870240): 63-year-old post-HSCT → transient improvement after IVIG but incomplete recovery
- **Pediatric data**: Zhang et al. (*Pediatr Infect Dis J*, 2014; PMID: 24732390) — Shanghai outbreak; early IVIG associated with better survival in severe cases (n=220 enrolled)
- **Mechanism**: Increases regulatory T cell populations, increases cAMP, modulates IL-6/IL-8/IL-10 levels (Wang et al., *PLoS One*, 2014; PMID: 25010330)

**Dosing:**
- Typically **1 g/kg/day × 1–2 days** or **2 g/kg as single dose** (following pediatric meningitis/encephalitis protocols)
- No adult-specific RCT data exists — dosing is extrapolated from pediatric experience

### 4.3 Milrinone

**Strongest evidence for severe disease (Grade III / cardiopulmonary failure).**

**Mechanism**: Phosphodiesterase-3 inhibitor → increases cAMP → attenuates sympathetic overactivity → reduces catecholamine storm → improves cardiac output and reduces pulmonary edema.

**Key RCT** — Chi et al. (*Crit Care Med*, 2013; PMID: 23685637):
- **First and only RCT** of milrinone in EV-A71
- **Design**: Prospective, randomized, controlled, open-label, single-center (Ho Chi Minh City, Vietnam)
- **Population**: Children ≤18 years with EV-A71-induced pulmonary edema and/or neurogenic shock (Stage 3B)
- **Intervention**: IV milrinone 0.5 μg/kg/min (n=22) vs. conventional management (n=19). Both groups received dopamine/dobutamine and IVIG.
- **Results**:
  - 1-week mortality: **18.2% (milrinone) vs. 57.9% (control)** (RR=0.314, 95% CI 0.12–0.83, **p=0.01**)
  - More ventilator-free days in milrinone group (p=0.01)
  - No drug-related adverse events
  - No apparent neurologic sequelae in survivors

**Additional mechanistic data**:
- Lin et al. (*Pediatr Res*, 2022; PMID: 35121850): Rat model showed milrinone protects cardiac mitochondria from norepinephrine-induced damage, maintains mitochondrial DNA copy number, and improves survival vs. esmolol
- Wang et al. (*PLoS One*, 2014; PMID: 25010330): Milrinone increased regulatory T cell populations and cAMP levels while reducing IL-6, IL-8, IL-10

**Adult data**: **No adult-specific data exists.** However, the pathophysiology (catecholamine storm → neurogenic cardiac stunning → pulmonary edema) is expected to be similar. Milrinone is widely used in adult cardiology (heart failure, pulmonary hypertension) with established dosing:
- **Adult dosing**: Loading dose 50 μg/kg over 10 minutes (often omitted in critical illness), then **0.25–0.75 μg/kg/min** infusion
- **Monitoring**: Blood pressure, heart rate, platelet count (thrombocytopenia risk), arrhythmias

### 4.4 Magnesium Sulfate

**Rationale**: MgSO4 is effective in controlling autonomic instability in tetanus and has been used empirically in severe EV-A71 with ANS dysregulation.

- Qui et al. (*Trials*, 2016; PMID: 26896318): Protocol for RCT of IV MgSO4 in Vietnamese children with HFMD + ANS dysregulation. Target Mg levels 2–3× normal. Primary endpoint: composite of disease progression (need for milrinone, ventilation, shock, death). Trial registered as NCT01940250.
- Results of this trial have not been published in the searched literature.

### 4.5 Pleconaril and Other Antivirals

**Pleconaril**: Capsid-binding inhibitor that prevents viral uncoating by binding the hydrophobic pocket in VP1.

- **FDA status**: NOT approved. Failed FDA approval for rhinovirus common cold due to adverse effects and resistance concerns.
- **Enterovirus spectrum**: Active against many enteroviruses including EV-A71 in vitro, but resistance develops rapidly with single-agent use.
- **Recent data**: Ianevski et al. (*Antiviral Res*, 2024; PMID: 38417531): Triple combination of **pleconaril + rupintrivir + remdesivir** showed remarkable efficacy against EV-A71 in human intestinal organoids and protected cells from enterovirus-induced cytotoxicity across multiple passages.
- **Clinical use in EV-A71**: No published clinical trials in EV-A71 rhombencephalitis. Not commercially available in most countries.

**Other investigational agents**:
- **Rupintrivir** (3C protease inhibitor): Active in vitro; no clinical data in EV-A71 encephalitis
- **Remdesivir**: Broad antiviral; synergistic in combination with pleconaril + rupintrivir against EV-A71 in organoids (Ianevski et al., 2024)
- **Fluoxetine**: Some reports of use in enteroviral CNS infections (Kapadia et al., 2021 used fluoxetine alongside IVIG); mechanism unclear, possibly anti-inflammatory
- **Vemurafenib**: Combination therapy with pleconaril and AG7404 showed efficacy against enteroviruses in vitro and in vivo (Ravlo et al., *NAR Mol Med*, 2025; PMID: 41522881)
- **Imidazolidinones**: Novel class of EV-A71-specific antivirals in development (Chang, 2008; PMID: 19054914)

### 4.6 Corticosteroids

- **No RCT data** specific to EV-A71 rhombencephalitis
- Theoretically **harmful** in viral encephalitis due to immunosuppression
- May be considered for **immune-mediated complications** (e.g., if ADEM overlap is suspected)
- Not recommended as standard therapy
- Dexamethasone has been used empirically in some case series, but evidence does not support routine use

### 4.7 ICU Management: Neurogenic Pulmonary Edema and Autonomic Instability

**Neurogenic pulmonary edema** (Finsterer, *Eur Neurol*, 2019; PMID: 31117074):

| Parameter | Management |
|-----------|-----------|
| **Diagnosis** | Pink frothy sputum, bilateral opacities on CXR, PaO₂:FiO₂ <200, acute CNS event, rapid resolution within 48–72h, exclusion of other causes |
| **Ventilation** | Early intubation; lung-protective ventilation; PEEP titration |
| **Hemodynamics** | Milrinone for cardiac dysfunction and pulmonary edema; vasopressors for shock |
| **Advanced support** | ECMO if refractory; thermodilution monitoring |
| **Simultaneous CNS treatment** | Treat underlying cause; manage ICP; neurocritical care |

**Autonomic instability**:
- Monitor for: tachycardia → hypertension → sudden hypotension/shock
- Milrinone is the agent of choice for catecholamine storm (reduces sympathetic overactivity)
- Avoid pure beta-blockers (may worsen outcomes — esmolol worsened survival in rat models; Lin et al., 2022)
- Tight glycemic control (hyperglycemia is a risk factor for severe disease; Zhang et al., 2014)

**Stage-based management** (adapted from Taiwan/Vietnam protocols):

| Stage | Features | Management |
|-------|----------|-----------|
| **Stage 1** | HFMD/herpangina, fever | Symptomatic; monitor for CNS signs |
| **Stage 2** | CNS involvement (myoclonus, ataxia, CN palsies) | Hospitalize; IVIG; consider ICU |
| **Stage 3A** | ANS dysregulation (tachycardia, hypertension, sweating) | ICU; milrinone; IVIG; magnesium sulfate (investigational) |
| **Stage 3B** | Pulmonary edema and/or cardiogenic shock | Intubation; milrinone; vasopressors; IVIG; ECMO if refractory |

## 5. Epidemiology in Southeast Asia

### 5.1 Taiwan

- **1998 epidemic**: 405 severe cases, 78 deaths. Landmark description of EV-A71 neurologic complications (Huang et al., *NEJM*, 1999). Pre-epidemic seroprevalence ~60–70% in those >6 years (Chang, 2008; PMID: 19054914).
- Ongoing epidemics 2000–2002 with dozens of fatal cases.
- Stage-based management developed to reduce fatality.
- **Adult data**: Rare during epidemics due to high baseline seroprevalence. Most adult infections are subclinical or mild HFMD.

### 5.2 Vietnam

- **2011–2012**: Largest outbreak — >200,000 hospitalizations, 207 fatalities (Donato et al., *Virology*, 2016; PMID: 27148893). Predominantly subgenogroup C4, with B5 and C5 co-circulating.
- EV-A71 accounts for **50% of species A enteroviruses** in HFMD, **54.8%** of meningitis cases, and **51.5%** of AFP cases in southern Vietnam (Romanenkova et al., *Vaccines*, 2023; PMID: 37243035).
- **Adult data**: Household contact studies show adults are frequently infected (47.6% EV-A71 infection rate among household contacts of index cases) and contribute to transmission (Hoang et al., 2020; PMID: 33274234).
- **Adult clinical case**: Pham et al. (2025; PMID: 40209324) — 21-year-old Vietnamese woman with EV-A71 encephalomyelitis confirmed by CSF/throat/skin/anal RT-PCR. Treated with single-dose IVIG → full recovery.
- Genotyping: C4a predominant, B5 also common (Le et al., *J Med Virol*, 2019; PMID: 30132913).

### 5.3 China

- Recurrent large epidemics since 2007. In Shanghai region (2010–2012): 26,829 children with suspected EV-A71, 767 admitted, 224 with severe disease, 7 deaths (3%). Majority of severe cases and all deaths from rural areas (Zhang et al., 2014; PMID: 24732390).
- Guangzhou (2012): 288 patients studied prospectively; 9.7% developed poor perfusion, 8.0% pulmonary edema, 2.4% died. MRI abnormalities in 37.7% of survivors (Yang et al., 2017; PMID: 28212620).
- **Three Chinese vaccines** completed Phase III clinical trials (inactivated whole-virus vaccines from Vigoo, Sinovac, and CAMS). Efficacy >90% against EV-A71-related HFMD; not yet widely available outside China (Lee, *Korean J Pediatr*, 2016; PMID: 27826325).

### 5.4 Cambodia

- **April 2012**: Fatal outbreak of undiagnosed disease at Kantha Bopha Children's Hospital, Phnom Penh. Most children died within 24 hours. EV-A71 C4a subgenotype confirmed as etiology (Duong et al., *Emerg Microbes Infect*, 2016; PMID: 27651091).

### 5.5 Thailand

- EV-A71 identified as the most prevalent cause of HFMD in 2008–2009; 2 patients developed brainstem encephalitis, 1 fatal (Chatproedprai et al., *Jpn J Infect Dis*, 2010; PMID: 20657060).

### 5.6 Korea

- Epidemics 2009–2012; C4a subgenotype dominant. 585 EV-A71 cases identified, 81.4% in children <5 years, 0.9% mortality (Kim et al., *BMC Infect Dis*, 2016; PMID: 27538397).

## 6. Prognosis and Outcomes in Adults

### 6.1 Mortality

**Pediatric data (Grade III)**:
- 71% mortality within hours without treatment (Huang et al., 1999)
- 57.9% 1-week mortality reduced to **18.2%** with milrinone (Chi et al., 2013)

**Adult data** (limited — case reports/series):
- Immunocompromised adults have **poorer outcomes**:
  - Post-HSCT: Fatal outcome reported (Ueda et al., 2025)
  - Rituximab-associated: Recovery with IVIG ± fluoxetine (Kapadia et al., 2021)
- Immunocompetent adults:
  - Vietnamese 21-year-old: Full recovery with IVIG (Pham et al., 2025)

### 6.2 Neurologic Sequelae

**Pediatric data** (follow-up from 1998 Taiwan epidemic):
- Persistent myoclonus, cranial nerve deficits, ventilator-dependent apnea in survivors of Grade III
- Most survivors of brainstem encephalitis with cardiopulmonary failure had **neurologic sequelae and impaired cognition** (Chang, 2008)
- Acute flaccid paralysis: 60% had persistent motor deficits (Classes III–V) at follow-up; IVIG did not predict long-term motor outcomes (Lee & Chi, *J Child Neurol*, 2014; PMID: 24453152)

**Adult data**: Insufficient data for definitive statements. Available cases suggest:
- Immunocompetent adults may recover fully with prompt treatment
- Immunocompromised adults have high risk of persistent deficits (muscle weakness, pulmonary impairment requiring tracheotomy)

### 6.3 Recovery Timeline

- **Grade I**: Full recovery within days to 2 weeks
- **Grade II**: Recovery over weeks; most achieve full neurologic recovery with supportive care ± IVIG
- **Grade III**: Survivors require weeks to months of rehabilitation; many have permanent sequelae
- **MRI normalization**: Most MRI lesions resolve within months; 84% of followed patients showed normal MRI at long-term follow-up (Zeng et al., 2016)
