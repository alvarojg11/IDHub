# Coccidioides / coccidioidomycosis etymology & discovery HistorID source notes

## Core narrative

In 1892, Alejandro Posadas (1870-1902), a 22-year-old Argentine medical student at the Hospital de Clínicas in Buenos Aires, examined tissue from a soldier named Domingo Escurra who had a fungating facial lesion. Escurra had been stationed in the Gran Chaco region (northern Argentina/Paraguay/Bolivia). Posadas saw large round bodies filled with endospores. He interpreted them as protozoan parasites from the group Coccidia and named the organism *Coccidioides* — Greek *kokkos* (berry/seed) + *-oides* (resembling), meaning "resembles Coccidia."

In 1900, William Ophüls (1871-1933), a pathologist at Stanford University's Cooper Medical College in San Francisco, proved it was a dimorphic fungus — mold in soil producing arthroconidia, converting to spherules in tissue. He named the species *Coccidioides immitis* (Latin *immitis* = harsh, cruel, severe). The protozoan hypothesis was dead; the genus name was permanent.

## Etymology

- Greek *kokkos* = berry, grain, seed (the spherule's appearance)
- *-oides* = resembling, like
- *Coccidioides* = resembling Coccidia (protozoan parasites) — **incorrect etymology based on a misidentification**
- Latin *immitis* = harsh, cruel, severe — clinically accurate
- *posadasii* = named for Alejandro Posadas (second species, recognized 2002)

## Discovery timeline

| Year | Person(s) | Event |
|------|-----------|-------|
| 1892 | Alejandro Posadas | First clinical description. Patient: Domingo Escurra, Argentine soldier. Published in Anales del Círculo Médico Argentino. Thought it was a protozoan. |
| 1894-96 | Emmet Rixford & T.C. Gilchrist | Described two cases in California agricultural workers. Also thought protozoan. Published in Johns Hopkins Hospital Reports. |
| 1900 | William Ophüls | Proved organism was a dimorphic fungus. Demonstrated mold-to-spherule conversion. Published definitively in JAMA 1905. |
| 1930s | Myrnie Gifford | San Francisco public health physician. Linked erythema nodosum ("Valley bumps") to acute coccidioidomycosis. First to show the disease was usually self-limited. |
| 1940s | Charles E. Smith | UC Berkeley epidemiologist. Developed coccidioidin skin test. Population surveys showed ~60% asymptomatic infections. Outbreaks at WWII military bases accelerated research. |
| 2002 | Fisher et al. | Molecular phylogenetics identified C. posadasii as a second species distinct from C. immitis. |

## Sources

1. Posadas A. Un nuevo caso de micosis fungoidea con psorospermias. An Círculo Méd Argent. 1892;15:585-597.
   Key details: The original description. Posadas was a 22-year-old medical student. Patient: Domingo Escurra. "Psorospermiae" was the term used for what we now know as spherules. Posadas classified it as a protozoan.

2. Rixford E, Gilchrist TC. Two cases of protozoan (coccidioidal) infection of the skin and other organs. Johns Hopkins Hosp Rep. 1896;1:209-268.
   Key details: Two California cases in agricultural workers. First US reports. Both fatal. Authors also thought protozoan. Use of the term "coccidioidal" in the title shows the protozoan hypothesis was the consensus.

3. Ophüls W. Coccidioidal granuloma. J Am Med Assoc. 1905;45(17):1291-1296.
   Key details: The definitive proof of fungal etiology. Ophüls cultured the organism on fungal media, observed mold-to-spherule conversion in animal models, and named the species C. immitis. This is the paper that corrected the protozoan hypothesis.

4. Smith CE. Epidemiology of acute coccidioidomycosis with erythema nodosum ("San Joaquin" or "Valley Fever"). Am J Public Health Nations Health. 1940;30(6):600-611.
   Key details: Seminal epidemiological work. Linked Valley Fever to coccidioidin reactivity. Showed ~60% asymptomatic infection rate in endemic areas. Named "Valley Fever."

5. Galgiani JN, Ampel NM, Blair JE, et al. Coccidioidomycosis. Clin Infect Dis. 2005;41(9):1217-1223.
   DOI: 10.1086/496991
   Key details: IDSA clinical practice guidelines. Covers epidemiology, diagnosis, treatment. Fluconazole for mild-moderate pulmonary, itraconazole for skeletal, amphotericin B for severe/disseminated. Risk factors for dissemination: Filipino and African ancestry, pregnancy (third trimester), immunosuppression.

6. Wikimedia Commons: Coccidioides fungal spherule. NIAID/NIH, public domain.
   URL: https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Coccidioides_Fungal_Spherule.jpg
   Key details: Large spherule (~60-80 microns) in tissue with endospores visible. NIAID image, US government work, public domain. This is the structure Posadas saw in 1892 and called a protozoan.

## Clinical context for drafting

### Coccidioidomycosis key facts
- Dimorphic fungus: mold in soil (arthroconidia), spherule in tissue
- Arthroconidia aerosolize from disturbed soil → inhaled → spherulate in lung
- ~60% asymptomatic, ~40% flu-like illness (<1% disseminate)
- Classic presentation: Valley Fever — cough, fever, chest pain, erythema nodosum
- Skin test (coccidioidin/spherulin): detects prior exposure, not useful for acute diagnosis
- Serology: immunodiffusion (IDCF) and complement fixation (CF). CF titer correlates with disease severity
- Culture: white-to-tan mold in 3-5 days on standard fungal media. **Biosafety level 3 practice required** for mold form — arthroconidia are highly infectious. Spherule form in tissue is not infectious.
- Treatment: fluconazole (mild-moderate), itraconazole (skeletal), lipid amphotericin B (severe/disseminated, especially meningitis)
- Lifelong azole suppression for meningitis
- No vaccine available despite decades of effort (a live attenuated vaccine candidate showed promise but was shelved)
- Endemic zone expanding with climate change: now reported in Washington state

### Risk factors for severe disease
- Filipino ancestry (highest risk — 10-175x relative risk for dissemination)
- African ancestry
- Pregnancy (especially third trimester)
- Immunosuppression: HIV (CD4 <250), transplant, TNF-alpha inhibitors
- Diabetes mellitus
- Older age

### Distinctive from histoplasmosis
- Histoplasma: small intracellular yeasts (2-4 µm), Ohio/Mississippi River Valleys, histoplasmin skin test
- Coccidioides: large spherules (30-80 µm), Southwest US, coccidioidin skin test
- Histoplasma grows faster in culture (1-3 weeks) and is less hazardous to handle
- Coccidioides mold form is a biosafety level 3 organism due to arthroconidia infectivity

## Drafting notes
- The central irony: a medical student named a fungus after a parasite, and the name stuck for 133 years
- Posadas died at 28, never knowing what he'd actually discovered
- Ophüls gets less name recognition than Posadas, but his work was more scientifically significant — he proved the dimorphic life cycle
- The WWII connection (military bases, internment camps) gives the story an uncomfortable gravity
- The spherule is the diagnostic thread: Posadas saw it, Ophüls explained it, every pathologist looks for it today
- No em dashes. No AI vocabulary. Natural conversational tone.
- Ending: Posadas died young, Escurra's name survives because Posadas wrote it down, the name is wrong but permanent
