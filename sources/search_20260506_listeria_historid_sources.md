# HistorID Source Notes: It Was Almost Listerella

Date: 2026-05-06

## Historical naming sources

Source: Burn CG. Characteristics of a new species of the genus `Listerella` obtained from human sources. *J Bacteriol*. 1935;30(6):573-591.

DOI: https://doi.org/10.1128/jb.30.6.573-591.1935

Key evidence: Burn used the genus name `Listerella` for human isolates and explicitly tied the organism to the Lister honorific period of the story.

Source: Burn CG. Clinical and pathological features of an infection caused by a new pathogen of the genus `Listerella`. *Am J Pathol*. 1936;12(3):341-348.1.

PMC: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1911083/

Key evidence: Confirms that `Listerella` was in active clinical-pathologic use in the mid-1930s and gives the naming story a real historical scene rather than a purely taxonomic note.

Source: Gray ML, Killinger AH. `Listeria monocytogenes` and listeric infections. *Bacteriol Rev*. 1966;30(2):309-382.

DOI: https://doi.org/10.1128/br.30.2.309-382.1966

Key evidence: Broad historical review used to support the early naming history, the transition from older taxonomic language, and the organism's long clinical arc.

Source: Seeliger HP. [Nonpathogenic listeriae: `L. innocua` sp. n. (Seeliger et Schoofs, 1977) (author's transl)]. *Zentralbl Bakteriol Mikrobiol Hyg A*. 1981;249(4):487-493.

PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/6171948/

Key evidence: The English abstract explicitly references `Listeria monocytogenes` as `(Murray et al., Pirie, 1940)`, which is useful for anchoring the Pirie rename in a citable database entry when direct access to the original 1940 paper is limited.

## Name meaning used in the draft

Verified framing used: `monocytogenes` should be described as an old blood-count observation from the early rabbit work, reflecting marked monocytosis or mononuclear leukocytosis.

Important guardrail: the draft should not imply that the species name was coined from modern mechanistic knowledge about intracellular pathogenesis. The name is older and more observational than that.

## Modern clinical relevance

Source: Charlier C, Disson O, Lecuit M. Maternal-neonatal listeriosis. *Virulence*. 2020;11(1):391-397.

PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32363991/

Key evidence: Supports the pregnancy and placental-infection ending. Useful points include mild or nonspecific maternal illness, placental tropism, fetal loss, and neonatal infection.

Source: Disson O, Lecuit M. Targeting of the central nervous system by `Listeria monocytogenes`. *Virulence*. 2012;3(2):213-221.

PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22460636/

Key evidence: Supports the rhombencephalitis angle, including brainstem tropism and the way CNS disease can differ from routine acute bacterial meningitis.

Source: Hof H, Nichterlein T, Kretschmar M. Management of listeriosis. *Clin Microbiol Rev*. 1997;10(2):345-357.

DOI: https://doi.org/10.1128/CMR.10.2.345

Key evidence used: listeriae are uniformly susceptible to many antibiotics in vitro except cephalosporins and fosfomycin; cephalosporins should not be framed as reliable treatment.

Source: Bento D, Tobin EH. `Listeria monocytogenes` Infection (Listeriosis). *StatPearls*. Updated 2026 Apr 12.

Bookshelf: https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK534838/

Key evidence: concise modern summary for pregnancy risk, CNS disease, brainstem predilection, and intrinsic cephalosporin resistance.

## Verified details used in the draft

- The draft should say the organism was first formally described in rabbit disease work before the `Listerella` / `Listeria` naming arc settled.
- `monocytogenes` should be explained as a preserved hematologic observation, not as a modern molecular insight.
- `Listerella` should be presented as the near-miss name attached to Joseph Lister.
- The most useful modern close is not food safety alone. It is pregnancy plus CNS disease, especially rhombencephalitis and the cephalosporin coverage gap.
