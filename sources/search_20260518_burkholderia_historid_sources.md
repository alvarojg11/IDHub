# Burkholderia HistorID source notes

## Sources checked

1. Burkholder WH. Sour skin, a bacterial rot of onion bulbs.
   URL: https://www.cabidigitallibrary.org/doi/abs/10.5555/19501101340
   Key details: Primary discovery paper from 1950 in Phytopathology. Burkholder described Pseudomonas cepacia as the cause of sour skin rot in onion bulbs. The species epithet cepacia derives from Allium cepa, the common onion.

2. Yabuuchi E et al. Proposal of Burkholderia gen. nov. (1992).
   URL: https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1348-0421.1992.tb02129.x
   Key details: The paper that created the genus Burkholderia. Seven species transferred from Pseudomonas homology group II on the basis of 16S rRNA, cellular fatty acids, ubiquinone systems, and phenotypic characteristics. Type species: Burkholderia cepacia. Named after Walter H. Burkholder. Published in Microbiology and Immunology, validated 1993.

3. Wikipedia: Burkholderia
   URL: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burkholderia
   Key details: Confirms genus named after Walter H. Burkholder. Notes that first species were transfers from Pseudomonas. Documents the 2014 split into Burkholderia sensu stricto (pathogens) and Paraburkholderia (environmental).

4. Wikipedia: Walter H. Burkholder
   URL: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_H._Burkholder
   Key details: Biographical information. Born 1891, died 1983. PhD Cornell 1917, professor of plant pathology at Cornell. First described Pseudomonas cepacia in 1950. Member of the Society of American Bacteriologists. Also co-authored Diseases and Insect and other Pests of the Field Bean in New York.

5. Mahenthiralingam E et al. The multifarious, multireplicon Burkholderia cepacia complex.
   URL: https://doi.org/10.1038/nrmicro1085
   Key details: Nature Reviews Microbiology review of the BCC. Documents the clinical impact in CF, person-to-person transmission, intrinsic multidrug resistance, and 35% mortality in outbreaks. Describes the large multireplicon genome (~8 Mb).

6. Wikimedia Commons: Burkholderia pseudomallei colonies
   URL: https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Burkholderia_pseudomallei_01.jpg
   Key details: CDC PHIL #1926. B. pseudomallei on blood agar. Credit: CDC/Courtesy of Larry Stauffer, Oregon State Public Health Laboratory. Public domain.

## Drafting notes

- The strongest hook is the gap between agricultural origin and clinical terror: a plant pathologist's onion bacterium became a CF killer and bioweapon.
- The name itself tells the story: Burkholder + -eria = Burkholderia, cepacia = of the onion.
- Burkholder was not a physician. This matters for the story. The organism crossed into human medicine later.
- The 1992 reclassification is the hinge: molecular methods showed it wasn't even in the same bacterial class as Pseudomonas.
- The Yabuuchi paper is the nomenclatural birth of the genus. Seven species transferred.
- Clinical stakes: 35% mortality in CF outbreaks, transplant contraindication, CDC Tier 1 select agents.
- The ending should circle back to the onion. The name preserves the agricultural origin. Every clinician saying "Burkholderia cepacia" is naming a vegetable from 1949.
- Keep the tone conversational but precise. Match the tone of "The Capsule That Wasn't There" and "Before It Was E. coli."
