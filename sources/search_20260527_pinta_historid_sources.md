# Pinta (Treponema carateum) etymology, discovery, and clinical significance — HistorID source notes

## Core narrative

Pinta is a non-venereal treponematosis caused by *Treponema carateum*, a spirochete morphologically and serologically indistinguishable from *T. pallidum* (syphilis). The name comes from the Spanish word *pinta* meaning "painted" or "spotted," describing the characteristic depigmented skin patches. The indigenous name *carate* is preserved in the species epithet. Known since pre-Columbian times, the spirochete was first visualized in 1938 by Arístides Herrera and named by Émile Brumpt in 1939. Unlike syphilis, pinta affects only the skin. Pinta may be the first human treponematosis eliminated.

## Etymology

- Spanish *pinta* = spot, mark, painted (from *pintar* = to paint)
- *carate* = indigenous term used in Colombia, Venezuela, and Panama
- *mal del pinto* = Spanish "disease of the painted one"
- *azul* = Spanish "blue" (some depigmented patches had a blue-gray hue)
- *empeines*, *lota*, *tina* = other regional names

### Scientific etymology
- *Treponema*: Greek *trepein* (to turn, twist) + *nema* (thread) — "turning thread," describing corkscrew motility
- *carateum*: from *carate*, the indigenous name
- Full meaning: "the turning thread of carate"

## Discovery timeline

| Year | Person(s) | Event |
|------|-----------|-------|
| Pre-Columbian | Indigenous populations | Recognized as distinct skin disease. Had local names (carate, mal del pinto, etc.) |
| 1500s | Spanish chroniclers (Gonzalo Fernández de Oviedo) | First European descriptions of what was likely pinta |
| 1869 | Édouard André & Édouard Riou | First visual documentation — "El carate" illustration published in *Le Tour du Monde* |
| 1938 | Arístides Herrera (Cuba) | First visualization of spirochetes in lesion exudate using dark-field microscopy |
| 1939 | Émile Brumpt (France) | Formal species description and naming: *Treponema carateum* |
| 1950s-60s | WHO/UNICEF | Mass penicillin treatment campaigns. Incidence collapsed. |
| 2020 | Vighi da Rosa et al. | Last autochthonous case reported from Brazil |

## Sources

1. Antal GM, Lukehart SA, Meheus AZ. The endemic treponematoses. Microbes Infect. 2002;4(1):83-94.
   DOI: 10.1016/S1286-4579(01)01513-1
   Key details: Comprehensive review of yaws, bejel, and pinta. Covers epidemiology, clinical presentation, diagnosis, treatment. The three endemic treponematoses are clinically distinct but caused by organisms that are genetically and antigenically nearly identical.

2. Giacani L, Lukehart SA. The endemic treponematoses. Clin Microbiol Rev. 2014;27(1):89-115.
   DOI: 10.1128/CMR.00070-13
   Key details: Detailed review including genomic comparisons. Confirms that the treponemes are morphologically identical and serologically cross-reactive. Genomic differences are small (<0.2% difference in the ~1.14 Mb genome). The molecular basis for differences in tissue tropism and clinical severity is not understood.

3. Brumpt E. Précis de Parasitologie. 6th ed. Paris: Masson et Cie; 1949. pp. 497-499.
   Key details: Brumpt named *Treponema carateum* in 1939. His parasitology textbook included the formal species description. Brumpt was a major figure in parasitology who also named *Trypanosoma cruzi* (after Carlos Chagas' mentor Oswaldo Cruz).

4. World Health Organization. Endemic treponematoses. Wkly Epidemiol Rec. 1981;56(31):241-244.
   Key details: WHO documentation of the mass treatment campaigns. The yaws and pinta control programs of the 1950s-1970s were among the largest disease control operations ever undertaken. Coverage in some endemic areas exceeded 90%.

5. Vighi da Rosa R, Damares Rodrigues de Souza D, Cartell A, Ricardo Martins Souza P. Mal de Pinta, first autochthonous case from South of Brazil. Int J Dermatol. 2021;60(1):112-114.
   DOI: 10.1111/ijd.15264
   Key details: The most recent case report of autochthonous pinta in the literature. From southern Brazil. Describes a 54-year-old man with depigmented patches, positive treponemal serology, and epidemiological link to an endemic region. No other autochthonous cases have been reported since.

6. Wikimedia Commons: El carate (1869). Illustration by Édouard André and Édouard Riou. Public domain.
   URL: https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:El_carate.jpg
   Key details: Engraving from Le Tour du Monde (1869). Shows a pinta patient being examined in the San Martín Territory, United States of Colombia. The earliest known visual documentation of pinta. Édouard André (1840-1911) was a French botanist and explorer. Édouard Riou (1833-1900) was a French illustrator who also illustrated Jules Verne's novels.

## Clinical context for drafting

### Pinta key facts
- Causative organism: *Treponema carateum*
- Motile spirochete, 6-20 µm long, 0.1-0.2 µm diameter
- Cannot be cultured in vitro (same limitation as *T. pallidum*)
- Morphologically indistinguishable from *T. pallidum*
- Serologically cross-reactive: VDRL, RPR, FTA-ABS, TPHA all positive
- No reliable serological test distinguishes pinta from syphilis, yaws, or bejel

### Clinical stages
- **Primary** (2-3 weeks after contact): small pruritic papule on exposed skin (arms, legs, face). Regional lymphadenopathy. Spirochetes abundant — dark-field microscopy diagnostic.
- **Secondary** (3-9 months): pintids — disseminated flat, hyperpigmented or erythematous plaques. May be scaly. Spirochetes present but sparse.
- **Late** (years later): depigmentation. Melanocyte destruction. Achromic patches resembling vitiligo. Permanent. Spirochetes rare or absent.

### Distinguishing from syphilis
- Transmission: skin-to-skin (non-venereal), usually in childhood
- No primary chancre (unlike syphilis)
- No congenital transmission
- No neurological involvement
- No cardiovascular involvement
- No gummatous lesions
- No organ damage beyond skin
- Essentially a cosmetic disease with zero mortality

### Treatment
- Benzathine penicillin G, 1.2 million units IM, single dose — curative
- Alternatives: tetracycline, doxycycline, azithromycin
- No known penicillin resistance
- Contacts should also be treated

### Epidemiology
- Endemic belt: southern Mexico through Central America to Colombia, Venezuela, Ecuador, Peru, Brazil
- Rural, impoverished communities. Warm, humid climates.
- Peak incidence: pre-1950s (hundreds of thousands of cases in some countries)
- Post-WHO campaigns: dramatic decline
- Current status: possibly eliminated. No confirmed autochthonous cases since 2020 (Brazil).
- WHO no longer maintains separate surveillance for pinta (folded into general treponematosis surveillance)

### Differential for positive treponemal serology with skin depigmentation
- Pinta (endemic region, childhood onset, no systemic symptoms)
- Late syphilis (sexual history, may have other organ involvement)
- Yaws (tropical Africa, Asia, Pacific — different endemic zone)
- Bejel (arid regions of Africa and Middle East)
- Biologic false-positive (rule out with treponemal-specific test)

## The treponematosis spectrum

| Disease | Organism | Transmission | Organs affected | Mortality |
|---------|----------|-------------|----------------|-----------|
| Venereal syphilis | *T. pallidum* subsp. *pallidum* | Sexual, congenital | Any organ, CNS, CV, placenta | Yes |
| Bejel (endemic syphilis) | *T. pallidum* subsp. *endemicum* | Skin-to-skin, oral, fomites | Skin, bone, cartilage | Rare |
| Yaws | *T. pallidum* subsp. *pertenue* | Skin-to-skin, childhood | Skin, bone, cartilage | Rare |
| Pinta | *T. carateum* | Skin-to-skin, childhood | Skin only | None |
