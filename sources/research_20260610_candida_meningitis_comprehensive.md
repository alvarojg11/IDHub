# Candida Meningitis in Immunocompromised Hosts: Comprehensive Research Summary
## Compiled for IDHub Clinical Case Development — June 10, 2026

---

## 1. CLINICAL PRESENTATION

### High-Risk Patient Populations

**Neonates** (highest-risk group):
- Extremely low birth weight (ELBW, <1000g) and very low birth weight (VLBW, <1500g) infants
- Prior antibiotic exposure, central venous lines, endotracheal intubation, fungal colonization
- Increased permeability of the blood-brain barrier in neonates predisposes to hematogenous Candida meningoencephalitis
- C. albicans and C. parapsilosis are the predominant species in neonatal units
- Crude mortality ~30% even with treatment (Chapman RL, Curr Opin Pediatr 2003; PMID: 12544279)
- Tripathi N et al, Semin Perinatol 2012; PMID: 23177800

**Post-neurosurgical patients**:
- Ventricular shunts, external ventricular drains (EVDs), CSF diversion devices
- Direct inoculation during neurosurgical procedures
- C. parapsilosis particularly associated with foreign body/device-related infections
- Shunt removal is essential for cure (IDSA guidelines)

**HIV/AIDS**:
- Candida meningitis is rare even in advanced HIV; cryptococcal meningitis is far more common
- Candida CNS infection typically occurs in the setting of prolonged candidemia or disseminated disease

**Hematologic malignancy / neutropenia**:
- Prolonged neutropenia is a major risk factor
- Candida CNS involvement in setting of disseminated candidiasis
- Granulocytopenic patients at particular risk (Meunier F, Eur J Clin Microbiol Infect Dis 1989; PMID: 2502408)

**Transplant recipients** (solid organ, HSCT):
- Immunosuppressive therapy (calcineurin inhibitors, high-dose corticosteroids)
- Candida CNS infection typically from hematogenous dissemination

**Prolonged candidemia**:
- Failure to clear candidemia increases risk of hematogenous dissemination to CNS
- Candida spp. are common commensal organisms that cause invasive disease when barriers are disrupted (Pappas PG et al, Nat Rev Dis Primers 2018; PMID: 29749387)

### Typical Symptoms and Signs
- Fever (most common, though may be absent in severely immunocompromised)
- Headache
- Nuchal rigidity (meningismus) — may be subtle or absent
- Altered mental status / lethargy
- Seizures
- Focal neurological deficits (cranial nerve palsies, hemiparesis)
- Signs of raised intracranial pressure (papilledema, vomiting)
- In neonates: temperature instability, apnea, bradycardia, feeding intolerance, lethargy, bulging fontanelle, seizures
- May present as subacute to chronic meningitis, brain abscess, or meningoencephalitis

### CSF Findings
- **WBC**: Elevated, typically lymphocytic or neutrophilic pleocytosis (variable); may be normal in profoundly neutropenic patients
- **Protein**: Elevated (often 50–500 mg/dL)
- **Glucose**: Low to low-normal (hypoglycorrhachia)
- **Culture sensitivity**: CSF culture is positive in approximately 40-60% of cases; sensitivity is lower in early infection and in patients already on antifungal therapy
- Multiple large-volume CSF samples increase yield
- **Gram stain**: May show yeast forms; Candida appears as Gram-positive budding yeast with or without pseudohyphae

### Time Course and Progression
- Typically subacute onset, evolving over days to weeks
- In neonates: may develop rapidly in setting of candidemia
- In post-neurosurgical: may be insidious with low-grade fever and progressive neurological decline
- Untreated, progresses to hydrocephalus, cerebral abscess formation, vasculitis, stroke, and death

## 2. DIAGNOSTIC APPROACH

### CSF Culture
- **Gold standard** but sensitivity is limited (~40-60% for Candida meningitis)
- Large-volume CSF collection (≥3-5 mL) improves yield
- Repeated lumbar punctures may be necessary
- Positive culture with compatible CSF findings is diagnostic

### CSF (1,3)-Beta-D-Glucan (BDG)
- Useful as a nonspecific marker of fungal meningitis
- Systematic review (Davis C et al, J Clin Microbiol 2020; PMID: 31996446):
  - >95% sensitivity in Exserohilum outbreak
  - 53% sensitivity and 87% specificity for Histoplasma meningitis
  - 89% sensitivity and 85% specificity for Cryptococcus meningitis
- For Candida: expected to be positive since Candida has abundant (1,3)-beta-D-glucan in cell wall
- FDA black box warning states Cryptococcus does not produce BDG, but evidence shows it is detectable in cryptococcal meningitis CSF
- Should be used as adjunct with organism-specific testing

### Serum Beta-D-Glucan
- Screening test for invasive fungal infection (including invasive candidiasis)
- Positive in most invasive Candida infections (sensitivity ~70-80% for invasive candidiasis)
- Does not distinguish Candida from other fungal pathogens
- Negative result does not exclude CNS candidiasis

### Serum Candida Mannan / Antimannan
- Mannan antigen and anti-mannan antibody assays (e.g., Platelia Candida)
- Useful adjunct for invasive candidiasis diagnosis
- Combined mannan/antimannan sensitivity ~80-85% for invasive candidiasis
- Limited data specifically for CNS candidiasis

### CSF India Ink
- Traditionally used for Cryptococcus (encapsulated yeast)
- **Candida does NOT typically produce a capsule**, so India ink is usually negative in Candida meningitis
- However, Candida may occasionally appear as refractile budding yeast on wet prep, potentially mimicking Cryptococcus
- Cryptococcal antigen (CrAg) test is specific for Cryptococcus and should NOT cross-react with Candida
- **Key point**: If India ink shows encapsulated yeast with narrow-based budding → Cryptococcus; if non-encapsulated budding yeast with pseudohyphae → Candida

### MRI Findings
- **Leptomeningeal enhancement**: Pachymeningeal and/or leptomeningeal gadolinium enhancement
- **Hydrocephalus**: Communicating or obstructive, often at the basal cisterns
- **Cerebral microabscesses**: Multiple small ring-enhancing lesions (typically <1 cm)
- **Ependymitis/ventriculitis**: Enhancement of ventricular lining
- **Vasculitic infarctions**: May see lacunar or territorial infarcts from fungal vasculitis
- **Granulomas**: Fungal granulomas in chronic infection
- MRI is more sensitive than CT for detecting these findings
- (Góralska K et al, Infection 2018; PMID: 29785613)

### Distinguishing Candida from Other Meningitides

| Feature | Candida Meningitis | Cryptococcal Meningitis | TB Meningitis | Bacterial Meningitis |
|---------|-------------------|------------------------|---------------|---------------------|
| **Onset** | Subacute | Subacute-chronic | Subacute-chronic | Acute |
| **CSF WBC** | Variable (neutro or lymph) | Lymphocytic | Lymphocytic (early neutro) | Neutrophilic |
| **CSF Glucose** | Low | Low | Very low | Very low |
| **CSF Protein** | Elevated | Elevated | Very elevated | Elevated |
| **India Ink** | Negative (no capsule) | Positive (encapsulated) | Negative | Negative |
| **CrAg** | Negative | Positive | Negative | Negative |
| **BDG** | Positive | Usually positive* | Negative | Negative |
| **CSF Culture** | Candida spp. | C. neoformans/gattii | M. tuberculosis (low yield) | Bacteria |
| **MRI** | Microabscesses, meningeal enhancement, hydrocephalus | Basal meningitis, cryptococcomas, dilated VR spaces | Basal meningitis, tuberculomas, hydrocephalus | Diffuse meningeal enhancement, empyema |
| **Host** | Immunocompromised, neonates, post-neurosurgical | HIV/AIDS (CD4<100), immunosuppressed | Endemic areas, immunocompromised | Any host |

*Note: FDA black box warning says Cryptococcus doesn't produce BDG, but clinical evidence shows detectable levels in CSF in cryptococcal meningitis.

## 3. TREATMENT

### Initial Therapy (IDSA 2016 Guidelines — Pappas PG et al, Clin Infect Dis 2016; PMID: 26679628)

**Recommended**: Liposomal amphotericin B (L-AmB) 3–5 mg/kg/day IV **PLUS** flucytosine (5-FC) 25 mg/kg PO/IV QID

- **Liposomal AmB**: Preferred over AmB deoxycholate due to better safety profile; achieves detectable therapeutic concentrations in CNS despite relatively limited CSF distribution
- **Flucytosine**: Excellent CSF penetration (water-soluble); synergistic with amphotericin B against Candida
  - Combination reduces mortality of Candida meningitis (Sigera LSM & Denning DW, Ther Adv Infect Dis 2023; PMID: 37051439)
  - Should be used especially in neonatal candidiasis due to high frequency of CNS involvement
  - Risk of bone marrow suppression and hepatotoxicity requires monitoring
  - Resistance can emerge on monotherapy (Bennet JE, Ann Intern Med 1977; PMID: 320931)

### Step-Down Therapy
**Fluconazole** 400–800 mg/day (6–12 mg/kg/day) PO/IV
- Step-down after clinical response and CSF sterilization on initial therapy
- Good CSF penetration (~50-80% of serum levels)
- Duration guided by species and susceptibility
- Dose adjusted for renal function

### Duration of Treatment
- **Minimum 4–6 weeks** after CSF sterilization
- Extended duration may be needed for:
  - Persistent CSF abnormalities
  - Cerebral abscesses (may require longer, up to several months)
  - Immunocompromised hosts with slow response
  - Neonatal meningoencephalitis
- Clinical and CSF parameters should guide duration
- Follow-up CSF cultures should be obtained to document sterilization

### Shunt Removal in Post-Neurosurgical Cases
- **Critical**: Infected ventricular shunts or EVDs must be removed
- Biofilm formation on devices renders antifungal therapy alone ineffective
- Shunt removal + antifungal therapy is the standard of care
- New shunt can be placed after CSF sterilization is confirmed
- (IDSA 2016 Guidelines; Whitney LC & Bicanic T, Cold Spring Harb Perspect Med 2014; PMID: 25384767)

### IDSA Guidelines Summary (2016, PMID 26679628)
- CNS candidiasis is treated as disseminated candidiasis with CNS involvement
- L-AmB + flucytosine for initial therapy
- Transition to fluconazole after clinical improvement
- Minimum 4-6 weeks total duration
- Removal of any infected prosthetic material (shunts, drains)
- Ophthalmologic examination to assess for Candida endophthalmitis (concurrent disseminated disease)
- Echocardiography to assess for Candida endocarditis

## 4. KEY SPECIES CONSIDERATIONS

### C. albicans
- Most common cause of invasive candidiasis (~40% of candidemia)
- Generally susceptible to fluconazole, amphotericin B, echinocandins
- Most commonly isolated in neonatal CNS candidiasis

### C. tropicalis
- Associated with neutropenic patients and hematologic malignancy
- Higher propensity for tissue invasion and dissemination
- Generally susceptible to fluconazole, but resistance emerging

### C. parapsilosis
- Second most common in neonatal ICUs
- Strong association with foreign bodies (catheters, shunts, prosthetic devices)
- Biofilm production is a key virulence factor
- Higher MICs to echinocandins compared to other species
- Fluconazole usually effective

### C. glabrata (Nakaseomyces glabrata)
- Increasing fluconazole resistance (dose-dependent susceptibility or resistant)
- May require voriconazole or higher-dose fluconazole
- Echinocandin resistance emerging via FKS mutations
- Higher mortality than C. albicans infections
- (Arendrup MC & Patterson TF, J Infect Dis 2017; PMID: 28911043)

### C. krusei
- **Intrinsically resistant to fluconazole**
- Must use amphotericin B or echinocandin (with limitations for CNS)
- Voriconazole has activity and better CNS penetration

### C. auris
- **Emerging multidrug-resistant pathogen** — global threat
- >90% resistant to fluconazole
- Many strains also resistant to amphotericin B
- Echinocandins are first-line therapy (but limited CNS penetration)
- Meningitis due to C. auris reported (García CS et al, Enferm Infecc Microbiol Clin 2020; PMID: 32111364 — described 1 case of meningitis among 30 invasive C. auris infections)
- (Long B et al, Am J Emerg Med 2024; PMID: 39137491)
- MALDI-TOF MS required for accurate identification

### Fluconazole Resistance Patterns (Czajka KM et al, Cells 2023; PMID: 37998390)
- Resistance mechanisms: ERG11 mutations, UPC2 gain-of-function, efflux pump overexpression (CDR1, CDR2, MDR1)
- N. glabrata, P. kudriavzevii (C. krusei), and C. auris demonstrate fluconazole resistance
- Echinocandin resistance via FKS1/FKS2 mutations

### Echinocandin Use for CNS Candidiasis
- **Major limitation**: Echinocandins (caspofungin, anidulafungin, micafungin) have poor CNS penetration
- Low or undetectable CSF concentrations (Góralska K et al, Infection 2018; PMID: 29785613)
- Do NOT play a meaningful role as primary therapy for CNS candidiasis
- May be used as initial empiric therapy for candidemia before CNS involvement is confirmed
- If Candida meningitis develops on echinocandin therapy, must switch to L-AmB-based regimen
- Novel agent ibrexafungerp (triterpenoid, same target as echinocandins) has shown some promise but limited CNS data (El Ayoubi LW et al, Curr Res Microb Sci 2024; PMID: 38873590)

## 5. PROGNOSIS AND COMPLICATIONS

### Mortality Rates
- Overall mortality of invasive candidiasis: ~63.6% (995,000 of 1,565,000 annual cases) (Denning DW, Lancet Infect Dis 2024; PMID: 38224705)
- Neonatal systemic candidiasis: crude mortality ~30% (Chapman RL 2003; PMID: 12544279)
- CNS candidiasis mortality is higher than candidemia alone
- Mortality is increased in:
  - Non-albicans species (C. glabrata, C. krusei, C. auris)
  - Delayed diagnosis
  - Persistent neutropenia
  - Non-removal of infected devices (shunts, catheters)

### Hydrocephalus
- Common complication of Candida meningitis, especially with basal meningitis
- May be communicating or obstructive
- Requires CSF diversion (external ventricular drain or shunt)
- Shunt should be placed only after CSF sterilization
- May be a permanent sequela requiring lifelong shunting

### Neurologic Sequelae
- Neurodevelopmental impairment in neonatal survivors
- Seizure disorders
- Cognitive deficits
- Cranial nerve palsies (especially involving basal meninges)
- Sensorineural hearing loss
- Focal motor deficits
- Visual impairment (from concurrent endophthalmitis or optic nerve involvement)
- Even successfully treated neonates may have significant long-term neurodevelopmental impairment (Tripathi N et al, Semin Perinatol 2012; PMID: 23177800)

## APPENDIX: Antifungal CNS Penetration Summary

| Drug | CSF Penetration | Role in CNS Candidiasis |
|------|----------------|------------------------|
| Liposomal AmB | Limited but detectable therapeutic levels | **First-line** (with 5-FC) |
| AmB deoxycholate | Limited CSF levels | Alternative (more nephrotoxic) |
| Flucytosine (5-FC) | Excellent (~70-80% of serum) | **First-line** adjunct |
| Fluconazole | Good (~50-80% of serum) | **Step-down therapy** |
| Voriconazole | Good (moderately lipophilic) | Alternative, especially C. krusei |
| Posaconazole | Negligible CSF | Not recommended for CNS |
| Itraconazole | Negligible CSF | Not recommended for CNS |
| Isavuconazole | Considerable efficacy reported | Emerging option |
| Echinocandins (caspofungin, anidulafungin, micafungin) | Low/undetectable CSF | **Not recommended** for CNS |

*Source: Góralska K et al, Infection 2018; PMID: 29785613*
