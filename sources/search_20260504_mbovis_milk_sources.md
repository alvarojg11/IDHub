# HistorID Source Notes: When Tuberculosis Came from the Cow

Date: 2026-05-04

## Primary Historical Source

Source: Smith T. A comparative study of bovine tubercle bacilli and of human bacilli from sputum. Journal of Experimental Medicine. 1898;3(4-5):451-511.

DOI: https://doi.org/10.1084/jem.3.4-5.451

Key evidence: The 1898 paper is the clearest primary source for the story hinge. Smith compared bovine tubercle bacilli with human bacilli from sputum and argued they were not interchangeable in a simplistic way. That did not finish the public-health argument overnight, but it gave bacteriology a firmer basis for treating bovine tuberculosis as a distinct human-relevant problem.

## Bovine Tuberculosis Control History

Source: Good M, Bakker D, Duignan A, Collins DM. The History of In Vivo Tuberculin Testing in Bovines: Tuberculosis, a "One Health" Issue. Front Vet Sci. 2018;5:59.

DOI: https://doi.org/10.3389/fvets.2018.00059

Key evidence: Useful for the transition from laboratory distinction to herd-level control. Confirms that bovine TB control became a practical animal-health and public-health project rather than a purely taxonomic curiosity.

## Zoonotic and Milk-Transmission Context

Source: O'Reilly LM, Daborn CJ. The epidemiology of Mycobacterium bovis infections in animals and man: a review. Tuberc Lung Dis. 1995;76 Suppl 1:1-46.

DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/0962-8479(95)90591-X

Key evidence: States that `M. bovis` became a major public-health problem when transmitted to humans via milk from infected cows, and that pasteurization helped eliminate much of that route in many settings.

Local metadata file: `sources/pubmed_20260504_bovine_tb_pasteurization.json`.

Source: de la Rua-Domenech R. Human Mycobacterium bovis infection in the United Kingdom: Incidence, risks, control measures and review of the zoonotic aspects of bovine tuberculosis. Tuberculosis (Edinb). 2006;86(2):77-109.

DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tube.2005.05.002

Key evidence: Strong review for the public-health consequences of raw milk transmission, pasteurization, cattle testing, and the continuing difficulty of distinguishing `M. bovis` from `M. tuberculosis` without species-level work.

Local metadata files: `sources/pubmed_20260504_mbovis_review.json` and `sources/pubmed_20260504_bovine_tb_pasteurization.json`.

Source: Macedo Couto R, Ranzani OT, Waldman EA. Zoonotic Tuberculosis in Humans: Control, Surveillance, and the One Health Approach. Epidemiol Rev. 2019;41(1):130-144.

DOI: https://doi.org/10.1093/epirev/mxz002

Key evidence: Useful modern framing for the One Health angle and the observation that pasteurization substantially changed the old milk-borne story without making zoonotic TB vanish globally.

## Modern Clinical Relevance

Source: Centers for Disease Control and Prevention. About Bovine Tuberculosis in Humans. Updated May 20, 2024.

URL: https://www.cdc.gov/tb/about/m-bovis.html

Key evidence used:

- `M. bovis` is most commonly found in cattle and can cause TB disease in humans.
- People are most commonly infected through contaminated, unpasteurized dairy products.
- Over a century of cattle control and routine pasteurization sharply reduced cattle-to-human transmission in the United States.
- `M. bovis` usually has pyrazinamide resistance, which matters when the species is identified.
- CDC notes that `M. bovis` causes less than 2% of TB disease cases in the United States.

## BCG Coda

## Verified Details Used

- The story hinge should be Theobald Smith's 1898 comparative study, not a vague claim that `M. bovis` was simply "discovered" in one dramatic instant.
- The public-health payoff is milk. The important transition is from cattle disease to human risk through dairy supply chains.
- Pasteurization and herd-control measures should be described as anti-TB interventions, not as background food-policy trivia.
- Modern relevance should land on unpasteurized dairy exposure, zoonotic TB surveillance, and pyrazinamide resistance.
- BCG should remain a brief coda in this piece, not the main narrative.
