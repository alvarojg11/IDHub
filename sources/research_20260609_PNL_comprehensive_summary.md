# Pure Neural Leprosy (PNL) — Comprehensive Research Summary for Clinical Case Building
## Date: 2026-06-09

---

## 1. CLINICAL PRESENTATION

### Demographics
- **Mean age at presentation**: ~43 years (SD 15.3) (Sarkar et al. 2026, n=95)
- **Male predominance**: 77% male (Sarkar et al. 2026); similar ~70% in Bhoi et al. 2024 (n=49)
- **Endemic regions**: India (highest burden), Brazil, Nepal, Indonesia, Myanmar, Bangladesh, sub-Saharan Africa. India contributes >50% of global leprosy cases; PNL is reported from Asia, Africa, South America, and Europe (Rao & Suneetha 2016).
- **Travel history matters**: PNL should be considered in non-endemic countries in patients with peripheral neuropathy and a history of travel to leprosy-endemic areas (Payne et al. 2015).

### Symptom Timeline
- **Delayed presentation**: Over half (55%) of patients present 1–5 years after symptom onset. This delay contributes to irreversible nerve damage (Sarkar et al. 2026).
- **Silent neuritis**: Progressive nerve damage occurs without symptoms, contributing to late presentation with established disability.

### Nerve Involvement Patterns
**Most commonly affected nerves** (across multiple studies):
1. **Ulnar nerve** — most frequently affected; ulnar claw (38%) (Sarkar et al. 2026)
2. **Common peroneal nerve** — foot drop is the presenting complaint in ~51% (Sarkar et al. 2026)
3. **Median nerve** — particularly at carpal tunnel
4. **Posterior tibial nerve**
5. **Sural nerve** — preferred for biopsy (sensory nerve)
6. **Great auricular nerve** — bilateral thickening is a significant finding
7. **Radial nerve** (cutaneous branch)
8. **Cranial nerves** — facial nerve (VII) involvement reported; also trigeminal (V)

**Nerve involvement patterns**:
- **Mononeuritis multiplex** (multiple mononeuropathies): 46.9–52.6% of cases (most common pattern)
- **Mononeuropathy**: 18% of cases
- **Polyneuropathy** (asymmetric): 7–22.2% of cases
(Shukla et al. 2020; Sarkar et al. 2026; Bhoi et al. 2024)

### Sensory vs Motor Findings
- **Sensory deficits**: Most common initial finding, present in ~80% (Sarkar et al. 2026)
- **Motor deficits**: Foot drop (51%), ulnar claw (38%), decreased muscle strength (91%)
- **Typical course**: Sensory loss precedes motor loss (M. leprae first damages Schwann cells → sensory fibers affected → then motor)
- **Trophic ulcers**: Present in 25% at diagnosis (Sarkar et al. 2026)

### Skin Lesions
- **By definition, skin lesions are ABSENT** in PNL — this is the diagnostic hallmark
- However, sub-clinical skin involvement can be demonstrated: when skin biopsies are taken from skin along the distribution of the affected nerve, a proportion of patients show leprosy pathology (Rao & Suneetha 2016)
- **Up to 20% of PNL patients develop skin lesions on follow-up**, indicating progression to cutaneous disease (Rao & Suneetha 2016)

### Pain Characteristics
- **Neuropathic pain**: Can be severe — described as "painful small-fiber neuropathy" (Nascimento 2013)
- **Nerve pain/tenderness**: Swelling and tenderness of affected nerves (Shrestha et al. 2019 — painful swelling near elbow)
- **Lepra reactions**: Can cause acute neuropathic pain (Type 1 reversal reactions, Type 2 ENL)
- Pain may also be present as nerve abscess formation (caseous necrosis within nerve)

## 2. DIAGNOSTIC APPROACH

### Diagnosis Without Skin Lesions
PNL is a **diagnosis of exclusion with supporting evidence**. The diagnostic pathway includes:

1. **Clinical suspicion**: Mononeuritis multiplex or mononeuropathy in endemic areas with thickened nerves
2. **Electrophysiology** (NCS/EMG)
3. **Nerve ultrasound (HRUS)**
4. **Slit-skin smear** (usually negative — because bacillary load is in nerves, not skin)
5. **Nerve biopsy** (gold standard)
6. **PCR** from nerve tissue or blood
7. **Serology** (PGL-1 antibodies — limited sensitivity)

### Nerve Ultrasound (HRUS)
**Key paper**: Roy et al. 2025 (J Clin Ultrasound, PMID 40457577) — HRUS in PNL diagnosis
**Key paper**: Shukla et al. 2020 (J Peripher Nerv Syst, PMID 32250546)

**Ultrasound findings in PNL**:
- **Nerve enlargement**: Cross-sectional area (CSA) increased at typical sites (cubital tunnel for ulnar, fibular head for peroneal)
- **Asymmetric and segmental thickening**: 100% of Hansen disease patients show peripheral nerve asymmetry and segmental thickening on US (Chen et al. 2024)
- **Echotexture changes**: Loss of normal honeycomb fascicular pattern; increased hypoechogenicity
- **Fascicular architecture disruption**: Loss of normal fascicular pattern
- **Increased vascularity**: On Doppler (indicating active inflammation)
- **US is superior to clinical examination** for detecting nerve thickening (Shukla et al. 2020)
- **Positive correlation** between illness duration and US-detected nerve involvement severity (Roy et al. 2025)

**US-guided FNAC**: Roy et al. 2025 described US-guided fine needle aspiration cytology of affected nerves:
- 82.8% of inconclusive skin biopsy cases had features suggestive of PNL on US-guided FNAC
- Combined cytology + AFB staining + PCR improved diagnostic yield

### Nerve Conduction Studies / EMG
**Key paper**: Bhoi et al. 2024 (Acta Neurol Belg, PMID 38438636)
**Key paper**: Sarkar et al. 2026 (PLoS Negl Trop Dis, PMID 41961892)

**NCS findings**:
- **Mononeuritis multiplex pattern**: Most common (~47–53%)
- **Axonal polyneuropathy**: 22% of cases
- **Sensory nerves more affected than motor**: Frequency of absent sensory nerve responses (51.2%) significantly higher than motor (24.7%) (Chen et al. 2024, P=0.0183)
- **Demyelination**: Present in 100% of biopsied cases; axonal damage in 60% (Chen et al. 2024)
- **Mixed axonal-demyelinating pattern**: Common
- **No response (absent potentials)**: In severely affected nerves

### Nerve Biopsy (Gold Standard)
**Key paper**: Sarkar et al. 2026 — 95 biopsy-confirmed PNL cases

**Biopsy findings**:
- **Borderline tuberculoid (BT) pathology**: 78% of cases (most common)
- **Borderline lepromatous (BL)**: 15.7%
- **Lepromatous (LL)**: Found even in clinically mononeuropathy cases — emphasizing need for biopsy
- **Granulomas**: Epithelioid cell granulomas with lymphocytes (in tuberculoid/borderline forms)
- **Acid-fast bacilli (AFB)**: Demonstrable in 75% of biopsy-confirmed cases (Shukla et al. 2020); may be negative in paucibacillary forms
- **Necrotizing granulomatous inflammation**: Can be AFB-negative but still consistent with tuberculoid leprosy (Shrestha et al. 2019)
- **Demyelination and axonal damage**: Universal findings

**Preferred nerve for biopsy**: Sural nerve (sensory, pure sensory nerve — no motor deficit risk)

**Important caveat**: Some patients have multibacillary leprosy in nerves but paucibacillary in skin, underscoring the usefulness of nerve biopsy (Grimaud & Vallat 2003).

### Slit-Skin Smear in PNL
- **Usually NEGATIVE** in PNL — because M. leprae is confined to peripheral nerves with no skin involvement
- The bacillary load is in the nerves, not the skin
- This is a key distinction from multibacillary cutaneous leprosy where skin smears are positive
- Even when skin biopsies are taken from along affected nerve distributions, they may be non-diagnostic (Roy et al. 2025: skin biopsy was diagnostic in only 23/52 patients = 44%)

### PCR from Nerve Tissue / Blood
**Key paper**: Razdan et al. 2024 (Diagn Microbiol Infect Dis, PMID 39278136)

- **Multiplex PCR (M-PCR)**: Can detect M. leprae DNA from nerve tissue
- **PCR from nerve biopsy**: Higher yield than from blood
- **Sensitivity remains limited**: PCR was positive in 1/3 tested cases in Roy et al. 2025 (33.3%)
- **Molecular technology was positive in 100% (11/11) of confirmed Hansen disease patients** using combined approach (Chen et al. 2024)
- **Blood PCR**: Lower sensitivity; may detect bacillary DNA in multibacillary PNL
- **Important**: Need for improving PCR sensitivity and research on specificity for ruling out mimics

### WHO Classification Criteria for PNL
- PNL is recognized as a distinct subgroup by the **Indian Association of Leprologists (IAL) classification**
- The **WHO classification** does not formally recognize PNL as a separate category — it classifies leprosy as paucibacillary (PB) or multibacillary (MB) based on skin smear results and number of skin lesions
- Since PNL patients have no skin lesions and negative skin smears, they are typically classified as **paucibacillary** by WHO criteria
- However, nerve biopsy may reveal multibacillary pathology (borderline lepromatous or lepromatous), creating a classification dilemma
- **WHO currently recommends**: PNL should be treated with the **multibacillary MDT regimen** (12 months) due to uncertainty about bacillary load
- The WHO 2018 guidelines and 2023 updated guidelines note that PNL patients should receive MB-MDT

## 3. DIFFERENTIAL DIAGNOSIS

### Key paper: Santos et al. 2021 (Rev Assoc Med Bras, PMID 34161469) — PNL vs amyloid neuropathy
### Key paper: Nascimento 2013 (Arq Neuropsiquiatr, PMID 24141500)
### Key paper: Dalave et al. 2024 (Cureus, PMID 39192940) — PNL mimicking carpal tunnel syndrome

### Conditions Mimicking PNL

| Condition | Distinguishing Features |
|---|---|
| **CIDP** | Symmetric proximal + distal weakness; CSF protein elevated; demyelinating on NCS (prolonged distal latencies, conduction block, temporal dispersion); responds to IVIG/steroids; nerve US shows diffuse uniform enlargement (vs segmental/asymmetric in PNL) |
| **Mononeuritis multiplex (vasculitic)** | Multiple nerve infarcts; systemic vasculitis features (weight loss, fever, rash); NCS shows asymmetric axonal neuropathy; nerve biopsy shows vasculitis (fibrinoid necrosis of vessel walls); ANCA+, ESR elevated; pain is typically acute and severe |
| **HNPP** | Autosomal dominant; PMP22 deletion; recurrent pressure palsies; demyelinating on NCS; nerve US shows focal enlargements at compression sites; family history positive; genetic testing diagnostic |
| **CMT** | Family history; symmetric slowly progressive; "inverted champagne bottle" legs; pes cavus; uniform demyelination on NCS; nerve US shows diffuse enlargement; genetic testing available |
| **Diabetic mononeuropathy** | Diabetes history; typically affects CN III, VI, VII, or femoral; usually acute onset; self-limiting; asymmetric; HbA1c elevated |
| **Sarcoidosis** | Granulomas on biopsy (non-caseating, vs caseating in tuberculoid leprosy); AFB negative; elevated ACE; bilateral hilar lymphadenopathy on CXR; can affect any nerve; responds to steroids |
| **Amyloidosis** | Congo red positive (apple-green birefringence); can mimic PNL with neuropathy; autonomic involvement prominent; carpal tunnel common; monoclonal protein in serum/urine; fat pad or nerve biopsy for diagnosis |

### Key Distinguishing Clinical Clues for PNL
1. **Asymmetric nerve involvement** with palpable nerve thickening
2. **Endemic area exposure** or travel history
3. **Skin lesions absent** but nerve damage present
4. **Sensory loss preceding motor loss**
5. **Trophic changes** (ulcers, hair loss, anhidrosis) in affected distribution
6. **Nerve US**: Asymmetric, segmental thickening with altered echotexture (vs uniform in CIDP/HNPP)
7. **Nerve biopsy**: Granulomatous inflammation ± AFB (pathognomonic)

## 4. TREATMENT

### MDT Regimen for PNL

**Current WHO recommendation**: PNL should be treated with **multibacillary (MB) MDT regimen**:
- **Rifampicin**: 600 mg once monthly (supervised)
- **Dapsone**: 100 mg daily (self-administered)
- **Clofazimine**: 300 mg once monthly + 50 mg daily

### Duration of Treatment
- **WHO MB-MDT duration**: 12 months (standard)
- Many experts recommend longer treatment given the uncertainty about bacillary load in nerves
- Some centers extend to 24 months, especially if biopsy shows multibacillary pathology
- Sarkar et al. 2026: All patients received MDT; 41/95 (43%) experienced lepra reactions

### WHO Guidelines for PNL Treatment
- WHO 2018 Guidelines: PNL patients should receive MB-MDT
- WHO 2023 updated guidelines: Continue to recommend MB-MDT for PNL
- Rationale: Since skin smears are negative and there are no skin lesions for bacillary counting, the true bacillary load is unknown. Treating as MB ensures adequate coverage.

### Corticosteroids for Neuritis in PNL
**Key paper**: Rao & Suneetha 2016

- **Systemic corticosteroids** are recommended for both:
  - **Acute neuritis** (Type 1 reversal reactions)
  - **Silent neuritis** (progressive nerve damage without symptoms)
- **Prednisolone**: Standard starting dose 40–60 mg/day, tapering over 12–24 weeks
- WHO recommends corticosteroids for managing leprosy reactions to prevent further nerve damage
- Early recognition and prompt treatment with corticosteroids of leprous reactions and "silent neuropathies" is critical to prevent disability (Grimaud & Vallat 2003)
- Bhoi et al. 2021: Patients treated with MB-MDT + steroids in tapering dose showed clinical improvement

### Additional Management
- **Pain management**: Neuropathic pain medications (gabapentin, pregabalin, amitriptyline)
- **Physiotherapy**: To prevent contractures and maintain function
- **Self-care of limbs**: Injury prevention in anesthetic areas
- **Trophic ulcer management**: Wound care, protective footwear
- **Surgical decompression**: Considered for nerve abscesses or entrapment (Shrestha et al. 2019)

## 5. KEY EPIDEMIOLOGICAL DATA

### Percentage of Leprosy Cases That Are Pure Neural
- PNL constitutes a **significant but variable percentage** of leprosy cases:
  - **India**: ~5–10% of all leprosy cases; in some referral centers, PNL represents a higher proportion
  - **Brazil**: Reported in endemic areas; exact percentage varies by region
  - Rao & Suneetha 2016: "It constitutes a good percentage of leprosy cases reported from India, which contributes to more than half of global leprosy numbers"
- Global estimate: PNL represents approximately **2–10%** of all new leprosy cases depending on the reporting country and diagnostic capability

### Countries with Highest PNL Prevalence
1. **India** — highest absolute number of PNL cases globally
2. **Brazil** — second highest leprosy burden
3. **Indonesia**
4. **Nepal** (Shrestha et al. 2019)
5. **Bangladesh**
6. **Myanmar**
7. **Mozambique**, **Democratic Republic of Congo**
8. **Nigeria**
9. Cases reported from **China** (Chen et al. 2024) and **United States** in immigrants from endemic areas (Payne et al. 2015)

### Recent Studies on PNL Diagnostic Criteria
1. **Sarkar et al. 2026** (PLoS Negl Trop Dis): Largest biopsy-confirmed PNL cohort (n=95); emphasizes need for nerve biopsy in all suspected cases; identified that clinically mononeuropathy patients may have lepromatous pathology
2. **Roy et al. 2025** (J Clin Ultrasound): US-guided FNAC as a less invasive alternative to open nerve biopsy
3. **de Athaide et al. 2025** (Front Immunol): Gene expression profiling in PNL nerves — identified inflammasome activation and autophagy impairment as key pathways
4. **Razdan et al. 2024** (Diagn Microbiol Infect Dis): Review of non-invasive diagnostic modalities — multiplex PCR, serological findings, immunological profiling
5. **Bhoi et al. 2024** (Acta Neurol Belg): Subclinical CNS involvement in PNL demonstrated by VEP, SSEP, BAEPs
6. **Shukla et al. 2020** (J Peripher Nerv Syst): Comprehensive electrophysiologic, ultrasonographic, and histological evaluation in 100 PNL patients
