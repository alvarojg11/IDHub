# Pseudomonas aeruginosa etymology, oil degradation, and clinical significance — HistorID source notes

## Core narrative

*Pseudomonas* was named by Walter Migula in 1894 from Greek *pseudes* (false) + *monas* (unit) because he thought the Gram-negative rods were single-celled protozoa. The species *aeruginosa* means "verdigris" in Latin, describing the blue-green pigment produced by pyocyanin (blue phenazine) and pyoverdin (yellow-green siderophore). But the defining trait of *Pseudomonas* is metabolic versatility: it can use crude oil, jet fuel, benzene, toluene, and hundreds of other organic compounds as sole carbon and energy sources. The same oxygenase enzymes and efflux systems that degrade hydrocarbons also degrade antibiotics. In 1980, *Pseudomonas putida* became the first patented living organism in *Diamond v. Chakrabarty*.

## Etymology

- Greek *pseudes* (ψευδής) = false, lying
- Greek *monas* (μονάς) = unit, single-celled organism, protozoan
- *Pseudomonas* = "false unit" — Migula (1894) mistook them for protozoa
- Latin *aeruginosa* = verdigris, copper rust — the blue-green patina of weathered copper
- *aeruginosa* describes the colonies' color: pyocyanin (blue) + pyoverdin (yellow-green)
- Colloquial: "Bacillus pyocyaneus" = blue-pus bacillus (from wound infections in the 1890s)
- Other species: *Pseudomonas putida* = from Latin *putidus* (stinking, rotten)
- Antipseudomonal = a drug active against Pseudomonas

## Sources

1. Migula W. Über ein neues System der Bakterien. Arb Bakteriol Inst Karlsruhe. 1894;1:235-238.
   Key details: Original genus description. Migula was at the Karlsruhe Technical Institute. Classified bacteria by morphology, motility, and pigment production. The name *Pseudomonas* reflects his mistaken belief that they were protozoa.

2. Migula W. System der Bakterien. Vol. 2. Jena: Gustav Fischer; 1900. pp. 873-892.
   Key details: Formal species description of *Pseudomonas aeruginosa*. Migula described the fluorescent pigments and the oxidase reaction. The classification system was morphological.

3. Chakrabarty AM. Plasmids in Pseudomonas. Annu Rev Genet. 1976;10:7-30.
   Key details: Describes the plasmid biology of Pseudomonas species. Plasmids encoding degradative pathways for camphor, octane, xylene, naphthalene, salicylate. These plasmids form the basis for Chakrabarty's engineered strain — he combined four degradative plasmids into a single strain of P. putida.

4. Diamond v. Chakrabarty, 447 U.S. 303 (1980).
   Key details: 5-4 Supreme Court decision. Chakrabarty had filed patent claims on the genetically engineered Pseudomonas strain. The Patent Office rejected claims to the living organism (allowed claims on the process). The Supreme Court reversed, holding that a living, human-made microorganism is patentable subject matter under 35 U.S.C. § 101. Chief Justice Burger: the bacterium is "a manufacture or composition of matter." Dissent by Justice Brennan argued that Congress, not courts, should decide whether living things are patentable.

5. Stover CK, Pham XQ, Erwin AL, et al. Complete genome sequence of Pseudomonas aeruginosa PAO1, an opportunistic pathogen. Nature. 2000;406(6799):959-964.
   DOI: 10.1038/35023079
   Key details: 6.3 million base pairs — the largest bacterial genome at the time. 5,570 predicted genes. >8% of genes dedicated to transcriptional regulation (about double E. coli). Large number of transport systems and catabolic pathways. The genome explained metabolic versatility at the molecular level. PAO1 is the reference strain isolated from a wound in 1955. The genomic era confirmed what microbiologists had suspected: Pseudomonas is built to degrade almost anything.

6. Lister PD, Wolter DJ, Hanson ND. Antibacterial-resistant Pseudomonas aeruginosa: clinical impact and complex regulation of chromosomally encoded resistance mechanisms. Clin Microbiol Rev. 2009;22(4):582-610.
   DOI: 10.1128/CMR.00040-09
   Key details: Comprehensive review of resistance mechanisms. Efflux pumps (MexAB-OprM, MexXY-OprM), AmpC beta-lactamase (chromosomally encoded, inducible), outer membrane permeability, acquired carbapenemases. Covers clinical impact, epidemiology, treatment options.

7. Wikimedia Commons: Pseudomonas aeruginosa pigment production on agar. Photo by HansN. CC BY-SA 4.0.
   URL: https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Pseudomonas_aeruginosa_pigment_production.jpg
   Key details: Shows blue-green pigment on various agar media. Pyocyanin (blue) + pyoverdin (yellow-green) combine to create the characteristic color. Also shows Gram stain, oxidase test, and colony morphologies.

## Clinical context for drafting

### Pseudomonas aeruginosa key facts
- Gram-negative, oxidase-positive, non-fermenting rod
- Motile (single polar flagellum)
- Oxidase-positive — distinguishes from Enterobacterales
- Non-lactose fermenter on MacConkey agar
- Grows at 42°C (distinguishes from other Pseudomonas spp.)
- Grape-like odor (aminoacetophenone) — classic clinical pearl
- Blue-green pigment on agar (pyocyanin + pyoverdin)
- Cetrimide agar: selective medium

### Infections caused
- Ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP)
- Burn wound sepsis
- Chronic lung infection in cystic fibrosis (biofilm, alginate overproduction, mucoid phenotype)
- Malignant otitis externa (diabetics, elderly)
- Ecthyma gangrenosum (neutropenic patients — vasculitis from bacterial invasion)
- Hot tub folliculitis
- Contact lens-associated keratitis
- Osteomyelitis (following puncture wounds through sneakers)
- Right-sided endocarditis in IV drug users
- Nosocomial UTI (catheter-associated)
- ~10% of all nosocomial infections

### Resistance mechanisms
- Low outer membrane permeability (porin restriction)
- Efflux pumps: MexAB-OprM (beta-lactams, FQ, tetracyclines, chloramphenicol), MexXY-OprM (aminoglycosides), MexCD-OprJ, MexEF-OprN
- AmpC beta-lactamase: chromosomal, inducible, confers resistance to penicillins and cephalosporins (not carbapenems unless combined with porin loss)
- Acquired carbapenemases: VIM, IMP, NDM (metallo-beta-lactamases), KPC (serine carbapenemase)
- Biofilm formation: alginate matrix in CF — antibiotics cannot penetrate
- Aminoglycoside-modifying enzymes (acquired)

### Treatment
- Piperacillin-tazobactam (for susceptible strains)
- Cefepime or ceftazidime (for susceptible)
- Carbapenems (meropenem, imipenem) — for susceptible
- Ciprofloxacin or levofloxacin — oral option but resistance is common
- Ceftolozane-tazobactam — active against many MDR strains, including AmpC-overexpressing, but NOT metallo-beta-lactamases
- Ceftazidime-avibactam — active against AmpC and some carbapenemases (not MBLs)
- Cefiderocol — siderophore cephalosporin, novel mechanism, active against MBLs
- Colistin (polymyxin E) — last resort, nephrotoxic
- Aminoglycosides (tobramycin, amikacin) — in combination
- Aztreonam — monobactam, active against MBL-producing strains (MBLs spare aztreonam)
- Combination therapy often used for severe infections

### WHO Priority
- Carbapenem-resistant P. aeruginosa: **Critical Priority** (Tier 1, along with CRAB and CRE)
- Major driver of the antimicrobial resistance crisis

## Metabolic versatility — the oil connection
- Broad-spectrum oxygenases: alkane hydroxylase, catechol dioxygenase, toluene dioxygenase
- Degrades alkanes (linear hydrocarbons, C5-C16), aromatics (benzene, toluene, xylene, naphthalene, phenanthrene)
- Biosurfactants: rhamnolipids — emulsify hydrocarbons, increasing bioavailability
- Polyhydroxyalkanoate (PHA) accumulation: carbon storage polymer, potential for bioplastic production
- Uses >75 different organic compounds as sole carbon and energy sources
- The same enzymatic machinery that degrades oil also degrades and modifies antibiotics
- This is the unifying theme: metabolic versatility = environmental ubiquity + clinical danger + antibiotic resistance
