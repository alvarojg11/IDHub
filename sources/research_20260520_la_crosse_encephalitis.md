## Core Concept

- Build a difficult summer encephalitis case in a 17-year-old from Appalachia
- Make the syndrome initially look like HSV encephalitis
- Use temporal-lobe EEG abnormalities plus hyponatremia to force a fellow-level pivot toward La Crosse virus

---

## Key Clinical Facts

### Virology and ecology
- La Crosse virus is a California serogroup **Orthobunyavirus** in the family **Peribunyaviridae**
- Enveloped, tripartite, negative-sense single-stranded RNA virus
- Main vector: **Aedes triseriatus** (eastern tree-hole mosquito)
- Amplifying hosts: chipmunks and squirrels
- Humans are **dead-end hosts**
- Virus is maintained by **transovarial transmission** in mosquito eggs, allowing overwintering
- Mosquitoes are aggressive **daytime biters**
- Breeding sites include **tree holes** and **discarded tires / containers with standing water**

### Epidemiology
- Most cases occur in the **upper Midwest**, **mid-Atlantic**, and **Appalachian / southeastern** United States
- Severe disease is most common in **children younger than 16**, but older adolescents remain susceptible
- Incubation period: **5-15 days**

### Clinical syndrome
- Initial symptoms: fever, headache, nausea, vomiting, fatigue, lethargy
- Neuroinvasive disease: encephalitis, meningoencephalitis, aseptic meningitis
- Common severe features: altered mental status, seizures, aphasia/dysarthria, paresis, cranial nerve deficits
- La Crosse virus is **not associated with acute flaccid paralysis**
- Fatality is low, about **1%**, but chronic neurologic sequelae occur in **6-15%**

### CSF / imaging / EEG
- CSF typically shows **lymphocytic pleocytosis**, **normal glucose**, and only mild-to-moderate protein elevation
- CT is often normal
- EEG abnormalities are common and may resemble HSV encephalitis
- Published cases show **temporal periodic lateralized epileptiform discharges (PLEDs / PLEDS)**
- Temporal-lobe MRI abnormalities have been described and can strongly mimic HSV encephalitis

### Diagnostic approach
- **Serology is primary**
- Send **La Crosse virus IgM** on serum and/or CSF
- Positive IgM should be confirmed by **neutralizing antibody testing** because of cross-reactivity with related viruses such as **Jamestown Canyon virus**
- CDC notes that viral RNA is rarely recovered from routine clinical specimens; RT-PCR is confirmatory only when positive
- Molecular testing may be more useful in **severely immunocompromised** patients, but not as the main test in a typical immunocompetent host
- Coordinate testing through **state public health laboratory** or **CDC**

### Management
- No proven antiviral therapy
- Supportive care only
- Empiric **acyclovir** is still appropriate initially because HSV remains the most dangerous treatable mimic
- Priorities: seizure control, sodium management, neuro checks, intracranial pressure awareness, ICU monitoring when worsening
- In the major NEJM series, **worsening hyponatremia and increasing body temperature** were associated with clinical deterioration

---

## High-yield Fellow Angles

1. **HSV mimic**
- Temporal-lobe MRI change + temporal PLEDs + aphasia should bait the fellow toward HSV first

2. **Serology over PCR**
- The key teaching pivot is that La Crosse confirmation is mainly serologic, not PCR-based

3. **Neurocritical-care supportive management**
- The harder management question is not "what antiviral?" but rather which physiologic trend predicts deterioration

## Question Design Chosen

### Q1 — Diagnosis
- Correct answer: **La Crosse virus neuroinvasive disease**
- Main distractor: HSV-1 encephalitis
- Supporting distractors: Powassan, West Nile, anti-NMDA receptor encephalitis

### Q2 — Diagnostic method
- Correct answer: **serum/CSF La Crosse IgM with confirmatory neutralizing antibody testing**
- Main teaching point: serology is primary; PCR is confirmatory only when positive

### Q3 — Deterioration marker
- Correct answer: **progressive hyponatremia with rising temperature**
- Main teaching point: this physiologic trend was associated with worsening disease in the major series
