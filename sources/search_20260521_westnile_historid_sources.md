# West Nile virus HistorID source notes

## Sources checked

1. Smithburn KC, Hughes TP, Burke AW, Paul JH. A neurotropic virus isolated from the blood of a native of Uganda. Am J Trop Med Hyg. 1940;20:471-492.
   URL: https://doi.org/10.4269/ajtmh.1940.s1-20.471
   Key details: Original discovery paper. Smithburn and colleagues at the Yellow Fever Research Institute in Entebbe isolated a novel neurotropic virus from the blood of a febrile woman from Omogo, West Nile district. The virus was characterized by intracerebral inoculation of Swiss mice. Antigenically related to Japanese encephalitis and St. Louis encephalitis viruses.

2. World Health Organization. West Nile virus fact sheet.
   URL: https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/west-nile-virus
   Key details: Confirms first isolation in 1937 in West Nile district, Uganda. Documents outbreaks in Israel (1950s), birds in Egypt (1953), birds in Israel (1997), and the 1999 NYC outbreak. Notes that WNV is maintained in a bird-mosquito cycle.

3. Nash D, Mostashari F, Fine A, et al. The outbreak of West Nile virus infection in the New York City area in 1999. N Engl J Med. 2001;344(24):1807-1814.
   URL: https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJM200106143442401
   Key details: Landmark NEJM paper describing the first outbreak of WNV in the Western Hemisphere. 62 human cases, 7 deaths. Massive bird die-offs, especially American crows. The strain was nearly identical to one circulating in Israel/Tunisia.

4. Sejvar JJ. West Nile virus: an historical overview. Ochsner J. 2003;5(3):6-10.
   URL: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21765761/
   Key details: Historical review of WNV from discovery to the 1999 NYC outbreak and subsequent spread across the US. Provides context on the evolution from obscure African arbovirus to major public health threat.

5. ECDC factsheet about West Nile virus.
   URL: https://www.ecdc.europa.eu/en/west-nile-fever/facts
   Key details: Confirms discovery in 1937 in West Nile district. Documents European outbreaks including Camargue (1962) and Romania (1996).

6. Wikimedia Commons: Albert Nile Bridge, Pakwach, Uganda.
   URL: https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Albert_Nile_Bridge_in_Pakwach_district,_north_eastern_Uganda.jpg
   Key details: Photo of the Albert Nile Bridge in Pakwach district. The bridge crosses the Albert Nile — the river that gave the West Nile district its name. CC BY-SA 4.0, photo by Jim Joel.

## Drafting notes

- The strongest hook is the misdirection: most people assume the virus is named after the Nile River. It's named after the West Nile district.
- The geography chain: district → named for being west of the Albert Nile → virus named for district. Three degrees of naming.
- The anonymous patient matters. Her name was never recorded. She recovered fully. The virus outlasted her anonymity.
- Smithburn was doing yellow fever surveillance. The discovery was an accident — looking for one thing, found another. This is a classic arbovirus discovery pattern.
- The 60-year gap between discovery (1937) and global emergence (1999) creates narrative tension.
- Birds weren't victims until 1997. That's a surprising detail that ID readers will appreciate.
- The 1999 NYC outbreak was a watershed for American arboviral surveillance. Dead bird reporting, sentinel chickens, mosquito pool testing — all became standardized.
- The ending should circle back to the place: a district in Uganda that became a household word 6,000 miles and 62 years later.
- Tone: conversational, precise. Match "When Malaria Meant Bad Air" and "When Influenza Meant the Stars."
