Let me set the scene: I had a patient with HIV on ART and a positive IGRA (Quantiferon), but no signs or symptoms of active TB. My initial plan was to offer <strong>at least 6-9 months of daily isoniazid plus vitamin B6 (6H to 9H)</strong>, given its proven safety and the fact that it doesn&#8217;t require adjusting ART dosing.</p><p>However, in discussion with my attending and our ID pharmacist, they mentioned <strong>1 month of daily rifapentine plus isoniazid (1HP)</strong>. I had no idea that such a short therapy existed for latent TB infection. That night, I dove into the literature and even brought it up at our fellow journal club. To my surprise, very few colleagues &#8212; attendings or co-fellows &#8212; had heard of this regimen. The 2020 WHO recommendations incorporate evidence published in 2018&#8211;2019 regarding rifamycin-containing regimens.</p><p>Before diving into regimens, it&#8217;s important to note that these are <strong>global recommendations</strong>, including high TB-incidence countries where LTBI testing may not be feasible due to logistic, economic, social, or infrastructural barriers. WHO acknowledges these challenges and recommends LTBI testing whenever feasible to identify those at highest risk for progression. However, testing is <strong>not required</strong> in people living with HIV or in household contacts under 5 years old. In HIV-negative household contacts &#8805;5 years or other risk groups, LTBI testing is recommended, but lack of testing should <strong>not prevent offering treatment</strong> if someone is judged high-risk. In high-prevalence countries, this often means preventative therapy is offered broadly, regardless of testing.</p><div><hr></div><h3><strong>TB Preventive Therapy: Two Main Categories</strong></h3><p><strong>1. Isoniazid Monotherapy</strong><br>Isoniazid has been widely studied and remains commonly used due to proven efficacy and safety, even in pregnancy. Typically referred to as <strong>6H</strong>, it can be offered for up to 12 months. The use of <strong>9 months (9H)</strong> comes from re-analyses of U.S. Public Health Service trials in the 1950s&#8211;1960s, which showed that benefit increases up to 9&#8211;10 months and stabilizes thereafter (<a href=\"https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10524579/\">PMID: 10524579</a><strong>)</strong>. No controlled clinical trials directly compared 9H vs 6H. In people living with HIV, the TEMPRANO trial (<a href=\"https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26193126/\">PMID: 26193126</a><strong>) </strong>demonstrated that 6H reduced severe HIV morbidity and mortality, even in those without confirmed LTBI. WHO guidelines note that <strong>9H is equivalent to 6H</strong> in well-resourced countries, but 6H is generally preferred for feasibility, patient acceptability, and resource considerations.</p><div><hr></div><p><strong>2. Hepatotoxicity was significantly lower in people living with HIV (PLHIV) (RR 0.26; 95% CI 0.12&#8211;0.55) and in HIV-negative adults (RR 0.16; 95% CI 0.10&#8211;0.27). Completion rates were higher across all subgroups.
