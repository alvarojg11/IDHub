This week, I&#8217;m part of the antibiotic stewardship team, and as part of the rotation we&#8217;re encouraged to write an educational piece for others in training. I wanted to tackle a classic yet controversial question: the role of gentamicin in endocarditis. Is it really needed? What about prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE)? And are there still situations where it makes sense? Let&#8217;s walk through the evidence together.</p><div><hr></div><h3>Why Gentamicin?</h3><p>Gentamicin is an aminoglycoside antibiotic that inhibits protein synthesis by binding to the 30S ribosomal subunit. Historically, it was used as an <strong>adjunctive agent</strong> alongside beta-lactams or glycopeptides for serious staphylococcal infections, including infective endocarditis (IE).</p><p>The rationale came from animal models in the 1970s&#8211;80s showing that gentamicin, when combined with penicillin or cephalosporins, had a <strong>synergistic effect</strong>, killing bacteria faster than beta-lactams alone and sterilizing vegetations more quickly. This concept was carried over into clinical practice, and for decades, &#8220;gentamicin synergy&#8221; became standard teaching.</p><p>But animal models don&#8217;t always translate into patient benefit. And as we&#8217;ve gained more data, the harms of aminoglycosides have become clearer.</p><div><hr></div><h3>The Case Against Gentamicin: Toxicity</h3><p>The problem is toxicity. In IE, renal failure is the second most common complication after heart failure.</p><ul><li><p>The <strong>EURO-ENDO registry</strong> reported acute renal failure in nearly <strong>18% of patients</strong> with IE.</p></li><li><p>Single-center studies suggest <strong>40&#8211;69%</strong> of patients develop some degree of renal dysfunction, with <strong>6% requiring dialysis</strong>, which carries a <strong>40% mortality risk</strong>.</p></li></ul><p>Causes of kidney injury include glomerulonephritis from immune complexes, septic emboli, hemodynamic instability, and drug toxicity (<strong>especially aminoglycosides</strong>, vancomycin, and certain beta-lactams). Adding gentamicin to this mix is like pouring fuel on the fire.</p><div><hr></div><h2>Organism-Specific Evidence</h2><h3><strong>Staphylococcus spp.</strong></h3><p><strong>Native Valve Endocarditis (NVE):</strong><br>The early belief was that gentamicin helped clear bacteremia faster. Several small RCTs (Abrams, Korzeniowski, Ribera, Fortun, Fowler) put that to the test: beta-lactam &#177; gentamicin.<a href=\"https://www.cochrane.org/evidence/CD003344_single-beta-lactam-antibiotic-versus-beta-lactam-aminoglycoside-combination-patients-severe\"> A meta-analysis showed</a>:</p><ul><li><p><strong>No reduction in relapse</strong> (RR 0.86; 95% CI 0.30&#8211;2.47)</p></li><li><p><strong>Higher risk of renal failure</strong> (RR 2.12; 95% CI 0.93&#8211;4.81)</p></li><li><p><strong>Higher mortality</strong> (RR 2.89; 95% CI 1.1&#8211;7.6)</p></li></ul><p>In the largest study, <strong>22%</strong> of patients receiving gentamicin developed reduced creatinine clearance vs <strong>8%</strong> without (P = .005).<br>Conclusion: gentamicin should <strong>not</strong> be used for native valve staphylococcal IE.</p><p><strong>Prosthetic Valve Endocarditis (PVE):</strong><br>Traditionally, staphylococcal PVE has been treated with a triple regimen: a backbone (oxacillin or vancomycin) + rifampin + gentamicin. This was based on in vitro synergy data and the theoretical need for more aggressive regimens in prosthetic infections.</p><p>But observational data have been underwhelming. Studies by <a href=\"https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36408468/\">Ryder, Ramos-Martinez, and Drinkovic</a> showed <strong>no survival benefit</strong> with triple therapy and suggested worse toxicity. Some analyses even hinted that rifampin-based dual therapy without gentamicin was safer and equally effective.</p><div><hr></div><h3><strong>Viridans Group Streptococci (VGS) and </strong><em><strong>Streptococcus gallolyticus</strong></em></h3><p><strong>Penicillin-Susceptible Isolates:</strong></p><ul><li><p>Classic regimens used 4 weeks of penicillin &#177; gentamicin.</p></li><li><p>A 51-patient RCT tested 2 weeks of ceftriaxone + gentamicin vs 4 weeks of ceftriaxone. Cure rates were similar &#8212; but crucially, there was <strong>no arm of ceftriaxone 2 weeks alone</strong>, leaving it unclear whether gentamicin was necessary.</p></li><li><p>In the OPAT era, the appeal of shortening therapy with gentamicin is outweighed by the risk of nephrotoxicity, especially in older patients.</p></li></ul><p><strong>Penicillin-Intermediate Isolates (MIC 0.25&#8211;0.5 &#181;g/mL):</strong></p><ul><li><p>Guidelines recommend adding gentamicin, citing concerns about borderline beta-lactam activity.</p></li><li><p>But evidence is extremely limited. Cure rates were good in younger patients, but renal toxicity was frequent, especially in older adults.
