<HistorIDShell fact={fact}>
  <p>
    Most people meet toxoplasmosis backward. First comes the warning about cats, undercooked meat,
    pregnancy, or maybe an HIV board question about brain lesions. Only later, if ever, comes the
    naming story. But the name is the fun part. <em>Toxoplasma gondii</em> was not built from cat
    litter or congenital infection. It came from what early investigators saw under the microscope
    and from the small rodent in which the parasite first drew attention.
  </p>

  <h2>Historical scene</h2>
  <p>
    In 1908, scientists in North Africa and Brazil were both staring at something odd. In Tunis,
    Charles Nicolle and Louis Manceaux found a strange protozoan in the tissues of a gundi, a small
    desert rodent with round ears and a talent for hiding in rock crevices. Around the
    same time in Brazil, Alfonso Splendore saw a similar organism in rabbits. The parasite entered
    science from two directions at once, which already tells you something about it: this was not a
    neat single-moment discovery.
  </p>

  <h2>What happened</h2>
  <p>
    Nicolle and Manceaux named the organism in 1909. The genus, <em>Toxoplasma</em>, came from the
    bow-like curve of the parasite's shape. The species name, <em>gondii</em>, came from the gundi.
    It is one of those scientific names that sounds intimidating until you break it apart, and then
    it becomes almost literal: a molded, arched little thing from a gundi.
  </p>

  <p>
    What is striking in retrospect is who was not yet at the center of the story. Cats now dominate
    the public imagination around toxoplasmosis, but they were not the naming scene. Early on, the
    parasite was a zoologic and microscopic puzzle more than a settled human pathogen. The full life
    cycle stayed incomplete for decades.
  </p>

  <h2>Why it changed infectious diseases</h2>
  <p>
    Toxoplasma is good ID history because its importance unfolded slowly. The parasite was discovered
    in 1908 and named in 1909, but its medical significance remained blurry for years. In 1939, it
    was identified in a congenitally infected infant, which changed how physicians thought about it.
    In 1948, the Sabin-Feldman dye test helped reveal how widespread infection really was. Then in
    1970, cats were finally recognized as the definitive host, and the environmentally durable oocyst
    stage made the organism's worldwide reach much easier to explain.
  </p>

  <p>
    That timeline matters. Medicine did not get the whole answer at once. It got shape first, then a
    host name, then congenital disease, then serology, then the cat life cycle. The story is a good
    reminder that discovery in ID often arrives in fragments.
  </p>

  <h2>Why it still matters now</h2>
  <p>
    Toxoplasma still lives in that gap between what people think they know and what actually matters.
    Yes, cats are part of the story, but they are not the whole story. So are undercooked meat,
    contaminated soil, unwashed produce, and timing during pregnancy. In most healthy people,
    infection passes quietly. In a fetus, the eye and brain can carry the cost for years. In someone
    with a weak immune system, the parasite can come roaring back in the CNS. The modern lesson is
    not "fear cats" in the abstract. It is to understand the life cycle well enough to know who is at
    risk, when the risk is highest, and why a parasite first seen in a gundi still shows up in ID
    clinics a century later.
  </p>
