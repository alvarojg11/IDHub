<HistorIDShell fact={fact}>
  <p>
    Edmond Nocard was a veterinarian at a school outside Paris when he pulled
    a strange branching filament from the lymph nodes of a dying cow. He called
    it <em>Streptothrix farcinica</em>. He could not have known that the same
    organism lived in the soil beneath the pasture, that it would be renamed in
    his honor within a year, or that it would spend the next century confusing
    microbiologists and killing immunocompromised patients in ways they were
    slow to recognize.
  </p>

  <h2>Historical scene</h2>
  <p>
    In 1888, Nocard was working at the École Vétérinaire de Maisons-Alfort,
    the oldest veterinary school in France, just southeast of Paris. He was
    forty-eight, a graduate of Alfort himself, and had spent his career on the
    diseases of animals: bovine tuberculosis, contagious mastitis, peripneumonia.
    He was also a member of the first editorial board of the <em>Annales de
    l'Institut Pasteur</em>, the journal Pasteur had just founded to organize
    the flood of discoveries pouring out of his laboratory on Rue Dutot. Nocard
    was not a physician. He was firmly inside the Pasteur orbit, but his daily
    work was cattle, not humans.
  </p>

  <p>
    He had been there for the anthrax vaccination at Pouilly-le-Fort in 1881,
    the public demonstration where Pasteur's attenuated vaccine protected sheep
    and goats before a crowd of farmers, veterinarians, and journalists. He had
    traveled to Egypt with Roux and Thuiller in 1883 during a cholera epidemic.
    His students included Camille Guérin, who would later co-develop the BCG
    vaccine with Albert Calmette. Nocard moved through the same corridors as the
    giants of French microbiology, but his own discoveries came from the barn and
    the slaughterhouse, not the ward.
  </p>

  <h2>What happened</h2>
  <p>
    The disease Nocard was investigating was bovine farcy, a chronic
    suppurative lymphangitis of cattle characterized by abscesses along the
    lymphatic vessels of the limbs. It caused economic losses large enough to
    attract attention but had resisted etiological explanation. When Nocard
    isolated the organism in 1888 and examined it under the microscope, he saw
    thin, branching filaments that looked more like a fungus than a bacterium.
    He placed it in the genus <em>Streptothrix</em> and named it{" "}
    <em>Streptothrix farcinica</em>, the "farcy" organism. The name was
    descriptive and temporary. It did not last a year.
  </p>

  <p>
    In 1889, the Italian botanist Vittore Trevisan proposed a new genus,{" "}
    <em>Nocardia</em>, to honor Nocard, and reclassified the organism as{" "}
    <em>Nocardia farcinica</em>. The genus name was a tribute. The species name
    preserved the disease. Both were reasonable. Neither turned out to be stable.
  </p>

  <p>
    In 1890 or 1891, the pathologist Eppinger described the first human case.
    A patient presented with pneumonia and a brain abscess. From the abscess,
    Eppinger recovered a branching filamentous organism that he named{" "}
    <em>Cladothrix asteroides</em>. The species epithet <em>asteroides</em>{" "}
    referred to the star-like, stellate colonies the organism formed on solid
    media. In 1896, Blanchard reclassified Eppinger's organism into Nocard's
    genus, and it became <em>Nocardia asteroides</em>. What had been a
    veterinary pathogen now had a human counterpart. The two species would spend
    the next century in a taxonomic tangle that no one fully resolved until
    molecular methods arrived in the 1990s.
  </p>

  <p>
    The confusion between <em>Nocardia</em> and <em>Actinomyces</em> was the
    worst of it. Both formed branching filaments under the microscope, and for
    decades they were treated as close relatives. But they are not.{" "}
    <em>Actinomyces</em> is anaerobic, part of the normal oral flora, and not
    acid-fast. <em>Nocardia</em> is aerobic, lives in soil, and is partially
    acid-fast when stained with a weak decolorizer. The distinction matters
    because the diseases they cause require different treatments. It took most of
    the twentieth century for this separation to become routine in clinical
    laboratories.
  </p>

  <p>
    The taxonomy got stranger before it got straight. In 1954,{" "}
    <em>N. farcinica</em> was designated the type species of the genus. But the
    original type strain had been maintained in two different culture collections:
    NCTC 4524 and ATCC 3318T. When Gordon and Mihm studied them carefully in the
    1960s, they found that the two supposedly identical strains were not
    identical at all. One was a <em>Mycobacterium</em>. The other was a{" "}
    <em>Nocardia</em>. They petitioned the Judicial Commission to merge{" "}
    <em>N. farcinica</em> and <em>N. asteroides</em> into a single species under
    the name <em>N. asteroides</em>, which became the new type. The merger stood
    for decades. When molecular methods finally separated them again in the
    1990s, it turned out that <em>N. farcinica</em> was not only a distinct
    species but one of the most drug-resistant members of the genus. The
    confusion had not been academic. It had clinical consequences.
  </p>

  <p>
    And what everyone called <em>N. asteroides</em> was not one species either.
    In 1988, Wallace and colleagues showed that isolates identified as{" "}
    <em>N. asteroides</em> fell into at least six different drug susceptibility
    patterns. In the 1990s and 2000s, 16S rRNA gene sequencing pulled these
    apart into separate species: <em>N. abscessus</em>,{" "}
    <em>N. cyriacigeorgica</em>, <em>N. veterana</em>,{" "}
    <em>N. kruczakiae</em>, and others. Each had a different susceptibility
    profile. The organism that had been treated as a single pathogen for a
    century was actually a complex, and treating all of its members the same way
    had been a mistake.
  </p>

  <h2>Why it changed infectious diseases</h2>
  <p>
    <em>Nocardia</em> was one of the first organisms that clearly bridged
    veterinary and human medicine. Nocard found it in a cow. Eppinger found it in
    a human brain. The same genus did both, and it took decades for clinicians
    to accept that a soil organism inhaled from dust could travel to the lungs
    and then seed the brain in an immunocompromised host.
  </p>

  <p>
    The organism also forced laboratories to adapt. <em>Nocardia</em> is
    partially acid-fast, but it loses its color when standard Ziehl-Neelsen
    staining uses strong acid decolorizers. It requires a modified technique,
    either 1% sulfuric acid instead of the usual hydrochloric acid, or the
    Fite-Faraco method, to preserve the delicate beaded filaments. Under the
    microscope, those filaments branch at right angles and show a characteristic
    beading that distinguishes <em>Nocardia</em> from{" "}
    <em>Actinomyces</em>, which does not stain acid-fast, and{" "}
    <em>Mycobacterium</em>, which does not branch. This three-way distinction is
    still taught in microbiology courses because it still matters at the bench.
  </p>

  <p>
    <em>Nocardia</em> also changed how clinicians think about brain abscesses.
    The spread from lung to brain is so consistent that a ring-enhancing
    intracranial lesion alongside a pulmonary infiltrate in an
    immunocompromised host should trigger the thought almost automatically.
    Untreated CNS nocardiosis kills roughly 80 percent of the time.
  </p>

  <h2>Why the name still matters now</h2>
  <p>
    The organism is not receding. The expanding population of transplant
    recipients, patients on biologic therapies, and long-term corticosteroid
    users has made nocardiosis more common, not less. Solid organ transplant
    recipients, especially lung transplant patients, carry an incidence of 0.6 to
    3 percent. TMP-SMX prophylaxis for <em>Pneumocystis</em> provides some
    protection, but breakthrough infections occur, particularly with intermittent
    dosing. And <em>N. farcinica</em>, the species that started the whole story
    in Nocard's cow, is often resistant to TMP-SMX. The original organism is
    also one of the hardest to treat.
  </p>

  <p>
    Species-level identification now guides therapy in ways that were impossible
    when everything was called <em>N. asteroides</em>. <em>N. farcinica</em> is
    often multidrug-resistant. <em>N. nova</em> is usually susceptible to most
    agents. <em>N. brasiliensis</em> is the dominant cause of cutaneous disease,
    especially mycetoma, in tropical regions. <em>N. otitidiscaviarum</em> is
    often resistant to TMP-SMX but susceptible to fluoroquinolones. The clinician
    who knows the species has a meaningful head start on treatment.
  </p>

  <p>
    Two details from the 2004 genome sequence of <em>N. farcinica</em> are
    worth remembering. The organism carries two copies of the RNA polymerase beta
    subunit gene, <em>rpoB</em>. One is rifampin-sensitive. The other is
    rifampin-resistant. This dual-gene system, the first known in any bacterium,
    is a pre-built escape hatch: the organism is ready for rifampin exposure
    without needing to acquire new resistance genes. And the genome encodes at
    least 103 oxygenases and 27 cytochrome P450 enzymes, a metabolic toolkit
    that helps explain how <em>Nocardia</em> survives in both decaying soil and
    living tissue. It is a generalist. That is what makes it dangerous.
  </p>

  <p>
    The name <em>Nocardia</em> means "of Nocard." Edmond Nocard died in 1903,
    fifteen years after his discovery. He was fifty-three. The organism he
    pulled from a cow's lymph node turned out to live in soil on every
    continent, cause brain abscesses in transplant patients, carry a pre-built
    resistance mechanism against a drug class invented long after his death, and
    spend a full century being misidentified in laboratories around the world.
    The name is a tribute to a veterinarian. The organism is a reminder that
    soil bacteria do not respect the boundary between veterinary and human
    medicine, and that a name on a culture report can carry a hundred years of
    mistaken identity inside it.
  </p>
