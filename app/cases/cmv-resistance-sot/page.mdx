<h2 className="text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900 mb-6">
  Rationale
</h2>

<div className="mx-auto max-w-5xl">
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Cytomegalovirus remains the single most important opportunistic viral
    pathogen in solid organ transplantation, and lung transplant recipients
    carry the highest risk of CMV disease among all SOT populations. The
    transplanted lung contains a large reservoir of latently infected cells
    in donor airway and parenchymal tissue, and the intense immunosuppression
    required to prevent allograft rejection profoundly suppresses the
    CMV-specific CD4 and CD8 T-cell responses that control viral reactivation.
    The D+/R&minus; serostatus represents the highest-risk donor-recipient
    mismatch: the recipient lacks pre-existing CMV-specific cellular immunity,
    and the donor organ transmits both latent virus and potentially replicating
    CMV in donor-derived leukocytes. Even with twelve months of valganciclovir
    prophylaxis, the extended duration recommended for D+/R&minus; lung
    transplant recipients, late-onset CMV disease occurs in a substantial
    proportion of patients after prophylaxis discontinuation. Late CMV disease
    reflects the failure to develop adequate de novo CMV-specific T-cell
    immunity during the period of pharmacologic viral suppression, leaving the
    patient vulnerable once antiviral pressure is removed.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Ganciclovir is the cornerstone of CMV treatment. It is a nucleoside analog
    that requires sequential phosphorylation to its active triphosphate form,
    with the critical first phosphorylation step catalyzed by the viral UL97
    serine-threonine kinase. Mutations in UL97 account for 85 to 90 percent of
    clinical ganciclovir resistance and most commonly occur at codons 460,
    594, and 595. These mutations reduce or eliminate the kinase's ability to
    phosphorylate ganciclovir, rendering the drug pharmacologically inert
    regardless of dose. The incidence of ganciclovir resistance in SOT
    recipients treated for CMV disease is approximately 5 to 10 percent
    overall but rises significantly in D+/R&minus; recipients, those with high
    pretreatment viral loads, those receiving prolonged antiviral exposure,
    and those with intense immunosuppression, all of which are present in this
    lung transplant patient. Clinical recognition of resistance is paramount:
    rising or non-declining CMV PCR after two to three weeks of appropriately
    dosed IV ganciclovir should trigger genotypic resistance testing rather
    than dose escalation or the addition of immunoglobulin products.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Genotypic resistance testing by PCR-based sequencing of UL97 and UL54 from
    plasma is the diagnostic standard for suspected ganciclovir resistance,
    offering clinically actionable results within one to two weeks. The
    genotypic result has direct therapeutic implications because the resistance
    mechanism determines which alternative agents retain activity. For UL97
    mutations such as this patient's A594V, foscarnet and cidofovir are
    predicted to retain activity because neither drug depends on UL97 for
    activation. Foscarnet directly inhibits the viral DNA polymerase at the
    pyrophosphate binding site, and cidofovir is phosphorylated by host
    cellular kinases. Foscarnet is the consensus first-line therapy for
    UL97-mediated resistance, but it carries substantial toxicity:
    nephrotoxicity through crystalline nephropathy and acute tubular necrosis,
    severe electrolyte wasting from tubular cation chelation, and genital
    ulceration from urinary excretion. In transplant recipients already
    receiving nephrotoxic calcineurin inhibitors, foscarnet-induced renal
    injury can be rapidly progressive. If UL54 mutations are present, the
    cross-resistance profile is more complex: specific UL54 mutations confer
    resistance to ganciclovir plus cidofovir, others confer resistance to
    ganciclovir plus foscarnet, and some confer pan-resistance to all three
    agents. The absence of UL54 mutations in this patient simplified the
    therapeutic decision by preserving multiple active alternatives.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Maribavir represents a mechanistically novel approach to CMV therapy. As
    a benzimidazole L-riboside, it binds directly to the ATP-binding pocket of
    the UL97 protein kinase and inhibits its enzymatic activity, blocking
    viral DNA encapsidation and nuclear egress. Because maribavir inhibits
    rather than depends on UL97, most ganciclovir-resistance UL97 mutations
    do not confer maribavir resistance. The FDA approved maribavir in November
    2021 based on the SOLSTICE trial, a Phase 3 open-label study of 352
    transplant recipients with post-transplant CMV refractory or resistant to
    conventional therapy. Maribavir 400 mg orally twice daily achieved
    confirmed CMV DNA clearance at Week 8 sustained through Week 16 in 55.7
    percent of patients versus 23.9 percent with investigator-initiated
    therapy. Its oral bioavailability, absence of nephrotoxicity, and
    favorable hematologic profile make it particularly valuable in transplant
    recipients with renal dysfunction or cytopenias. Two clinical caveats are
    important: taste disturbance occurs in nearly half of patients and can
    affect adherence, and paradoxical CMV DNA increases may occur in the first
    weeks of therapy, thought to reflect the drug's unique mechanism of
    inhibiting viral egress rather than DNA replication. These transient rises
    should not be misinterpreted as treatment failure in a clinically
    improving patient.
  </p>
