<h2 className="text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900 mb-6">
  Rationale
</h2>

<div className="mx-auto max-w-5xl">
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Murine typhus is caused by <em>Rickettsia typhi</em>, an obligate
    intracellular gram-negative bacterium of the typhus group (along with
    <em>R. prowazekii</em>). It is endemic in warm coastal and periurban
    areas worldwide, with South Texas representing one of the highest-burden
    regions in the United States. The classic epidemiologic cycle involves
    rats and the oriental rat flea (<em>Xenopsylla cheopis</em>), but the
    domestic cycle in Texas increasingly implicates opossums and cats as
    reservoir hosts, with the cat flea (<em>Ctenocephalides felis</em>)
    as the principal vector. This explains why murine typhus is no longer
    confined to seaports or impoverished areas and is now recognized as a
    suburban and periurban disease affecting households with outdoor pets.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    The clinical presentation is characteristically subacute. After an
    incubation period of one to two weeks, patients develop the classic triad
    of fever, headache, and rash. The rash is maculopapular, begins on the
    trunk, and spreads centrifugally to the extremities while sparing the
    palms, soles, and face, a pattern opposite to that of RMSF (which spreads
    centripetally from the distal extremities). The rash is absent in up to
    half of patients and may be subtle, particularly in patients with darker
    skin tones. Laboratory findings include thrombocytopenia, elevated
    transaminases, elevated LDH, and hyponatremia, reflecting endothelial
    infection and systemic inflammation. Severe complications including
    neurologic involvement, renal failure, and respiratory failure occur
    primarily in delayed or untreated cases.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Diagnosis is confirmed by Rickettsia-specific IgG serology using
    indirect immunofluorescence assay (IFA), but acute-phase titers are
    frequently negative in the first week of illness, as antibodies do not
    rise until one to two weeks after symptom onset. Paired convalescent
    titers (drawn two to four weeks later) showing a fourfold rise are
    diagnostic. PCR on whole blood or eschar biopsy (when present) can
    provide early confirmation. Treatment must never be delayed pending
    serologic confirmation: empiric doxycycline should be started on
    clinical suspicion. The response is typically rapid, with defervescence
    within 24 to 72 hours. Failure to improve with doxycycline should
    prompt reconsideration of the diagnosis.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    A common clinical error is withholding doxycycline in pregnant patients
    out of concern for dental staining or fetal effects. In the setting of
    a potentially life-threatening rickettsial infection, the risk of
    untreated disease to both mother and fetus far outweighs the risk of
    a short doxycycline course. Current IDSA and CDC guidelines explicitly
    recommend doxycycline for rickettsial infections in pregnancy. Prevention
    involves flea control in household pets and their environment, limiting
    pet exposure to wildlife, and prompt removal of fleas from pets and
    bedding.
  </p>
</div>

      not direct transmitters.
    </li>
    <li>

      the palms, soles, and face. This centrifugal pattern distinguishes
      murine typhus from RMSF, which spreads centripetally from the wrists
      and ankles and involves acral surfaces.
