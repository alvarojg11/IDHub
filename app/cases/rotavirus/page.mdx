<h2 className="text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900 mb-6">
  Rationale
</h2>

<div className="mx-auto max-w-5xl">
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Rotavirus is a member of the family Reoviridae and is the leading cause
    of severe dehydrating gastroenteritis in children under five worldwide,
    responsible for an estimated 200,000 deaths annually before the
    introduction of vaccines. In adults, rotavirus causes a milder but
    clinically significant illness, and is increasingly recognized as an
    important cause of nosocomial gastroenteritis outbreaks in hospitals and
    long-term care facilities, particularly among the elderly. The name derives
    from the Latin rota (wheel), reflecting the characteristic wheel-like
    appearance of the triple-layered capsid on electron microscopy.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Structurally, rotavirus is a non-enveloped, double-stranded RNA virus with
    a genome of 11 segments encoding six structural (VP1-VP4, VP6, VP7) and
    six non-structural proteins. The virion has three concentric protein
    layers: the inner core (VP2), the intermediate layer (VP6, which carries
    the group antigen used in rapid antigen tests), and the outer capsid
    formed by VP7 (G antigen) and VP4 (P antigen spike protein). This
    protein-only outer capsid, lacking any lipid membrane, is the key
    structural feature with direct clinical relevance: non-enveloped viruses
    are intrinsically more resistant to alcohol-based hand sanitizers than
    enveloped viruses such as influenza or SARS-CoV-2, whose lipid membranes
    are readily disrupted by ethanol. During rotavirus outbreaks, soap and
    water hand hygiene is therefore preferred over alcohol-based hand rub.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Rotavirus infects mature enterocytes of the small intestinal villi,
    causing villous blunting, malabsorptive diarrhea, and secretory diarrhea
    mediated by the NSP4 enterotoxin. The incubation period is one to three
    days, and the illness typically lasts three to eight days. Transmission is
    via the fecal-oral route; rotavirus is highly infectious, with an estimated
    infectious dose as low as 10 viral particles. The virus is environmentally
    stable and can survive on hard surfaces for weeks. Hospital outbreaks are
    common and can be difficult to control once established. Appropriate
    measures include contact precautions, cohorting, soap and water hand
    hygiene, and environmental disinfection with bleach-based or accelerated
    hydrogen peroxide products effective against non-enveloped viruses.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Prevention in the community relies on oral live attenuated vaccines
    (Rotarix, a monovalent G1P[8] vaccine; RotaTeq, a pentavalent
    human-bovine reassortant vaccine), which have dramatically reduced
    childhood rotavirus hospitalizations and deaths in countries with high
    vaccine coverage. Adult rotavirus vaccines are under investigation but not
    yet approved. Diagnosis in the clinical setting is typically made by stool
    rapid antigen testing detecting VP6, with a sensitivity of 85 to 95
    percent; RT-PCR is available for genotyping during outbreak investigations.
  </p>
</div>
