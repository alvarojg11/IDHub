<h2 className="text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900 mb-6">
  Rationale
</h2>

<div className="mx-auto max-w-5xl">
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    <em>Corynebacterium kroppenstedtii</em> is a lipophilic, gram-positive rod that has
    emerged as an important and underrecognized cause of granulomatous mastitis. It is
    specifically associated with a histologic pattern known as cystic neutrophilic
    granulomatous mastitis (CNGM), characterized by lipogranulomas with cystic spaces
    surrounded by neutrophils. This distinctive pathology should prompt the clinician to
    consider <em>C. kroppenstedtii</em> even when standard cultures are negative.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    The organism is difficult to isolate on routine culture due to its lipophilic nature
    and relatively slow growth. Standard aerobic cultures may fail to recover it, and
    microbiologic identification increasingly relies on molecular methods such as 16S rRNA
    gene sequencing or other nucleic acid-based techniques. The advent of MALDI-TOF mass
    spectrometry and improved sequencing platforms has substantially improved recognition
    of this organism in clinical practice.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Granulomatous mastitis caused by <em>C. kroppenstedtii</em> typically presents in
    women of reproductive age, often with a history of prior lactation. The clinical
    presentation — a painful, enlarging breast mass with overlying skin changes — closely
    mimics inflammatory breast cancer, frequently leading to extensive oncologic workup
    before the diagnosis is established. The periareolar region is commonly involved.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Although <em>C. kroppenstedtii</em> is generally susceptible to many antimicrobials,
    high-level resistance to penicillin, lincosamides, macrolides, and tetracyclines has
    been reported across multiple case series. Multidrug-resistant strains carrying
    multiple resistance genes have also been described. For this reason, empiric therapy
    should not assume beta-lactam or macrolide susceptibility, and antimicrobial
    susceptibility testing is essential to guide treatment.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Treatment consists of targeted antimicrobial therapy based on susceptibility results,
    drainage of drainable abscesses, and prolonged antibiotic courses when necessary.
    Recurrence is a well-documented challenge, reported across multiple case series, and
    patients should be counseled about this risk. It is worth noting that{" "}
    <em>C. kroppenstedtii</em> has also been identified as a cause of native and prosthetic
    valve endocarditis, highlighting its capacity for invasive disease beyond the breast.
    Two related species, <em>Corynebacterium parakroppenstedtii</em> and{" "}
    <em>Corynebacterium pseudokroppenstedtii</em>, have also been associated with mastitis.
  </p>

      Cystic neutrophilic granulomatous mastitis (CNGM) — lipogranulomas with cystic

    </li>
    <li>
      The clinical presentation mimics inflammatory breast cancer; biopsy is essential
      both to exclude malignancy and to identify the granulomatous pattern.
    </li>
    <li>
      Standard cultures often fail to recover <em>C. kroppenstedtii</em> due to its
      lipophilic growth requirements; 16S rRNA gene sequencing or MALDI-TOF on tissue
      specimens should be considered when CNGM is identified on pathology.
    </li>
    <li>
      Do not assume beta-lactam or macrolide susceptibility — high-level resistance to
      penicillin, macrolides, lincosamides, and tetracyclines is well described.
      Susceptibility testing is essential.
    </li>
    <li>
      Management includes targeted antimicrobials, abscess drainage, and consideration
      of prolonged therapy given well-documented recurrence risk.
    </li>
    <li>
      When granulomatous mastitis is diagnosed, steroids should not be used empirically
      without first excluding an infectious cause such as <em>C. kroppenstedtii</em>,
      mycobacteria, or fungi.
