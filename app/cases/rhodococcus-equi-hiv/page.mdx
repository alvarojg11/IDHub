<h2 className="text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900 mb-6">
  Rationale
</h2>

<div className="mx-auto max-w-5xl">
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    <em>Rhodococcus equi</em> (formerly <em>Corynebacterium equi</em>) is a gram-positive, partially acid-fast, obligate aerobic coccobacillus found worldwide in soil, particularly in environments enriched by herbivore manure. It belongs to the order Mycobacteriales, family Nocardiaceae — placing it taxonomically alongside mycobacteria and <em>Nocardia</em>, all of which share mycolic acid-containing cell walls that account for their variable acid-fastness on staining.
  </p>
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    The organism's defining clinical feature is its ability to cause cavitary pulmonary infection in immunocompromised hosts, particularly those with advanced HIV and CD4 counts below 200 cells per microliter. The clinical and radiographic presentation — subacute fever, productive cough, weight loss, night sweats, and upper lobe cavitary lesions — so closely mimics tuberculosis that patients are frequently started on empiric anti-tuberculous therapy before the correct diagnosis is established. In published series, the median CD4 count at presentation is approximately 30 to 50 cells per microliter, reflecting the degree of cell-mediated immunodeficiency required for the organism to cause invasive disease.
  </p>
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    The virulence of <em>R. equi</em> depends on a large conjugative plasmid carrying a pathogenicity island that includes the <em>vapA</em> gene and multiple <em>vap</em> homologues. VapA is a lipid-anchored surface protein essential for the organism's ability to survive within macrophages. After phagocytosis, the <em>R. equi</em>-containing vacuole is diverted from the normal endosomal maturation pathway: it does not acquire lysosomal markers, does not acidify, and does not undergo the respiratory burst. This allows the organism to replicate freely within the macrophage. After approximately 48 hours, the infected macrophage undergoes necrosis, releasing organisms that are phagocytosed by newly recruited phagocytes, perpetuating the infectious cycle.
  </p>
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    The host defense against <em>R. equi</em> depends on intact cell-mediated immunity — specifically, CD4-positive T lymphocytes producing interferon-gamma to activate infected macrophages and overcome the phagosome maturation block. This is precisely the immune compartment that is depleted in advanced HIV, explaining the strong association between severe <em>R. equi</em> infection and low CD4 counts. It also explains why antiretroviral therapy is not merely an adjunct but an essential component of treatment: without immune reconstitution, relapse is expected regardless of the duration or intensity of antimicrobial therapy.
  </p>
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    The diagnosis of <em>R. equi</em> infection is frequently delayed because the organism is dismissed as a diphtheroid contaminant — a coryneform gram-positive rod that the laboratory may not fully identify in the absence of specific clinical context. The critical microbiologic clues that should prevent this error are: the organism is partially acid-fast on modified Kinyoun stain (using 1% sulfuric acid decolorization), it produces characteristically salmon-pink to coral-red mucoid colonies on blood agar, it is catalase-positive and urease-positive, and it does not ferment carbohydrates. MALDI-TOF mass spectrometry has improved the speed and accuracy of identification.
  </p>
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    The most important diagnostic distinction is from <em>Nocardia</em> species, which are also partially acid-fast and can cause cavitary pulmonary disease in immunocompromised hosts. The key differences are morphologic: <em>R. equi</em> appears as a coccobacillus on Gram stain (not filamentous or branching), produces salmon-pink mucoid colonies (not the chalky, dry colonies typical of <em>Nocardia</em>), and is reliably urease-positive (<em>Nocardia</em> is urease-variable). Both organisms grow on routine media, but the colony morphology and Gram stain appearance allow experienced microbiologists to distinguish them before definitive identification.
  </p>
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Treatment of <em>R. equi</em> infection requires prolonged combination antimicrobial therapy guided by in vitro susceptibility testing. There are no randomized controlled trials. Agents with reliable activity include vancomycin, linezolid, imipenem, levofloxacin, rifampin, and macrolides such as azithromycin. A common approach is to use two to three of these agents in combination for at least 2 to 6 months, with an initial period of intravenous therapy until clinical improvement is documented. Antiretroviral therapy should be initiated concurrently to achieve immune reconstitution. If the patient does not recover CD4 counts above 200 cells per microliter, lifelong suppressive oral therapy may be necessary to prevent relapse. Surgical resection of refractory cavitary lesions may be required in selected cases.
  </p>
