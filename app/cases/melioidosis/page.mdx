<h2 className="text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900 mb-6">
  Rationale
</h2>

<div className="mx-auto max-w-5xl">
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Melioidosis is caused by <em>Burkholderia pseudomallei</em>, a gram-negative
    environmental saprophyte found in soil and surface water in endemic tropical regions,
    particularly Southeast Asia and northern Australia. Humans are infected primarily
    through percutaneous inoculation (cuts and abrasions during contact with contaminated
    soil or water), inhalation during severe weather events, or ingestion of unchlorinated
    water. The disease is highly seasonal, with approximately 80% of cases presenting
    during the wet season.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Host immunocompromise is central to disease susceptibility. Diabetes mellitus is the
    predominant risk factor, present in over 50% of cases. Other risk factors include
    hazardous alcohol use, chronic kidney disease, chronic lung disease, thalassemia,
    immunosuppressive therapy, and malignancy. Notably, HIV infection is not a major
    risk factor for melioidosis — a distinctive epidemiologic feature.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    The clinical spectrum ranges from localized skin and soft tissue infection to
    fulminant septicemia. Bacteremia is present in 40% to 60% of cases, and over half
    of patients present with pneumonia. The organism has a remarkable propensity for
    abscess formation in visceral organs — liver, spleen, prostate, and parotid are
    classic sites. Approximately 10% to 15% of bacteremic cases have no identifiable
    clinical focus. The case fatality rate ranges from 10% to 40%, and is even higher
    in resource-limited settings with delayed diagnosis.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    A modeling study has estimated 165,000 cases and 89,000 deaths from melioidosis
    per year worldwide, making it a significant neglected tropical disease — though it
    does not appear on the WHO&apos;s official NTD list. The disease is almost certainly
    underdiagnosed in many endemic regions due to limited laboratory capacity.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Diagnosis is confirmed by isolation and identification of <em>B. pseudomallei</em>{" "}
    from clinical specimens. In endemic areas, immunofluorescence, lateral flow
    immunoassays, and latex agglutination on isolates can accelerate identification.
    Serology has limited utility due to high background seropositivity in endemic
    populations. Imaging — particularly abdominal ultrasound or CT — is essential to
    identify occult visceral abscesses.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Treatment is divided into two mandatory phases. The intensive phase consists of IV
    ceftazidime or meropenem (preferred for severe or CNS disease) for a minimum of 2
    weeks, often extended until clinical improvement. The eradication phase uses oral
    co-trimoxazole for 3 to 6 months to prevent relapse; co-amoxiclav is an alternative
    but carries higher relapse rates. Surgical drainage of abscesses and rigorous
    glycemic control are essential adjuncts. <em>B. pseudomallei</em> is intrinsically
    resistant to aminoglycosides, most third-generation cephalosporins (including
    ceftriaxone), and first-line antistaphylococcal agents — a critical point, since
    empiric coverage for presumed staphylococcal abscess will be ineffective.
  </p>
</div>

<div className="mx-auto max-w-5xl">
