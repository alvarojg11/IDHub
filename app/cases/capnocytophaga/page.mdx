<h2 className="text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900 mb-6">
  Rationale
</h2>

<div className="mx-auto max-w-5xl">
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    <em>Capnocytophaga canimorsus</em> is a slender, elongated, fusiform gram-negative
    rod that colonizes the oral flora of dogs and, less commonly, cats. The genus name
    encodes two of its defining laboratory characteristics: <em>capno-</em> (from the
    Greek for smoke or vapor, referring to CO&#8322;) and <em>-cytophaga</em> (cell
    eater, reflecting its gliding motility and ability to lyse erythrocytes). The
    organism is a commensal that causes no disease in its animal hosts but can produce
    fulminant infection in susceptible humans following dog bites, scratches, or even
    salivary contact with non-intact skin or mucous membranes. Formal bites are
    reported in only half of cases; licks to abraded or eczematous skin are a
    frequently underappreciated route.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    The organism is fastidious: it grows slowly (typically 5 to 7 days on solid media,
    though blood culture systems may flag earlier), requires CO&#8322;-enriched
    conditions, and fails to grow on MacConkey agar. These characteristics place it
    among the group of clinically important gram-negative rods that laboratories may
    struggle to identify without extended incubation and enhanced media. Matrix-assisted
    laser desorption ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF) has
    significantly improved species-level identification in clinical laboratories.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Asplenic patients are at disproportionate risk for fulminant <em>Capnocytophaga</em>
    bacteremia. The spleen is critical for the opsonization and clearance of
    encapsulated organisms; <em>C. canimorsus</em> possesses surface structures that
    resist phagocytosis and complement-mediated killing in the absence of splenic
    function. Mortality from <em>C. canimorsus</em> septicemia in asplenic patients
    can exceed 30%, and the syndrome may include septic shock, disseminated
    intravascular coagulation, purpura fulminans, and peripheral gangrene. Meningitis
    is an established but less common manifestation. Additional risk factors include
    alcohol use disorder, hematologic malignancy, and corticosteroid therapy.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Beta-lactam antibiotics are the treatment of choice. <em>Capnocytophaga canimorsus</em>
    is consistently susceptible to penicillin G, ampicillin-sulbactam, piperacillin-tazobactam,
    cephalosporins, carbapenems, clindamycin, fluoroquinolones, and metronidazole.
    For non-CNS infections (including bacteremia) beta-lactam/beta-lactamase inhibitor
    combinations are preferred: ampicillin-sulbactam or piperacillin-tazobactam IV, with
    oral amoxicillin-clavulanate for step-down or outpatient therapy. For CNS infections,
    meropenem is preferred because the beta-lactamase inhibitor components of combination
    agents (sulbactam, tazobactam) penetrate the blood-brain barrier poorly; meropenem
    crosses the BBB intact and achieves therapeutic bactericidal concentrations in the CSF.
    The critical resistance to recognize is intrinsic resistance to aztreonam, a monobactam
    active against aerobic gram-negative rods but with no activity against
    <em>Capnocytophaga</em>. Narrowing to aztreonam when de-escalating gram-negative
    therapy is a familiar but dangerous error in this organism, and the susceptibility
    report must be reviewed carefully before any adjustment.
  </p>
</div>

      additional risk factors. Mortality in asplenic patients can exceed 30%.
    </li>
    <li>
      The Gram stain is distinctive: slender, elongated, fusiform gram-negative
      rods with tapered ends. Fusobacterium nucleatum shares this morphology but
      is an obligate anaerobe from human oral flora with no association with
      animal contact.
    </li>
    <li>
      The organism is fastidious: capnophilic (requires CO&#8322; enrichment),
      fails to grow on MacConkey agar, and may require extended incubation.
      MALDI-TOF has improved identification.
    </li>
    <li>
      <em>Capnocytophaga canimorsus</em> is intrinsically resistant to aztreonam.
      Do not narrow to aztreonam when de-escalating therapy in gram-negative
      bacteremia without confirming susceptibility — this is a potentially fatal
      error.
    </li>
    <li>
      For non-CNS infections (bacteremia), beta-lactam/beta-lactamase inhibitors
      are preferred: ampicillin-sulbactam or piperacillin-tazobactam IV, with
      amoxicillin-clavulanate orally for step-down. For CNS infections, meropenem

      the BBB intact.
    </li>
    <li>
      Asplenic patients with dog bites or dog exposure and fever should be
      treated as a medical emergency and started on empiric beta-lactam therapy
      promptly. Functional asplenia (e.g., sickle cell disease) carries the
      same risk as surgical asplenia.
