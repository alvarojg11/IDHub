<h2 className="text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900 mb-6">
  Rationale
</h2>

<div className="mx-auto max-w-5xl">
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    <em>Trypanosoma cruzi</em> is an obligate intracellular protozoan transmitted by triatomine
    insects (kissing bugs) that defecate near their bite wound; infective metacyclic
    trypomastigotes penetrate through mucous membranes or skin abrasions. Chronic infection is
    endemic across Latin America, with Bolivia carrying the highest seroprevalence in the world,
    reaching 20 to 30% in rural communities where triatomine-infested adobe housing is
    widespread. In immunocompetent hosts, the acute phase resolves and the organism persists
    indefinitely as quiescent intracellular amastigotes, predominantly in cardiac and smooth
    muscle, without producing further symptoms in the majority of infected individuals.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    In patients with chronic T. cruzi infection who develop advanced HIV disease, the collapse
    of cell-mediated immunity permits reactivation of dormant amastigotes. CNS reactivation
    typically occurs when the CD4 count falls below 200 cells/μL and most commonly below 100
    cells/μL. The result is cerebral trypanosomiasis: space-occupying, ring-enhancing lesions
    with surrounding vasogenic edema that are radiographically indistinguishable from cerebral
    toxoplasmosis. This overlap creates a diagnostic trap: empiric anti-toxoplasma therapy is
    frequently initiated before the correct diagnosis is entertained, delaying appropriate
    treatment by days to weeks.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Two clues at initial presentation should raise suspicion and shorten this delay.
    First, Toxoplasma IgG serology is detectable in more than 95% of confirmed CNS
    toxoplasmosis cases; a negative result meaningfully shifts the differential toward
    alternative diagnoses, including Chagas reactivation, primary CNS lymphoma, and other
    infections. Second, the epidemiologic history matters enormously: patients from rural
    Bolivia, Brazil, Argentina, Paraguay, or other highly endemic regions who have documented
    or suspected chronic T. cruzi infection deserve early consideration of this diagnosis even
    before empiric therapy fails. When the CSF is examined by wet mount, the identification of
    mobile flagellated trypomastigotes establishes the diagnosis with certainty and without the
    need for brain biopsy. T. cruzi serology (IgG by ELISA or indirect immunofluorescence) is
    typically strongly positive, reflecting the underlying chronic infection, and provides
    confirmatory serologic evidence.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Benznidazole is the preferred treatment for CNS Chagas reactivation, dosed at 5 to
    7 mg/kg/day in two divided doses for 60 days. It acts as a prodrug selectively reduced by
    T. cruzi nitroreductase enzymes, generating reactive intermediates that alkylate parasite
    DNA and disrupt thiol-dependent redox homeostasis. Common adverse effects include rash,
    peripheral neuropathy, and gastrointestinal intolerance. Nifurtimox (8 to 10 mg/kg/day
    in three to four divided doses for 60 to 90 days) is an active alternative with a similar
    mechanism but a less favorable tolerability profile. Both drugs are available in the United
    States through specialized compounding pharmacies and via the CDC. Antiretroviral therapy
    must be initiated or optimized concurrently: sustained immune reconstitution above
    200 cells/μL is the most reliable protection against future reactivation, and without ART,
    recurrence is nearly certain.
  </p>
</div>

    <li>
      CNS <em>T. cruzi</em> reactivation should be considered in any HIV patient with
      CD4 below 200 cells/μL and ring-enhancing brain lesions who originates from a
      Chagas-endemic region, particularly Bolivia, Brazil, Argentina, or Paraguay; Bolivia
      carries the highest seroprevalence worldwide, up to 20 to 30% in rural communities.
    </li>
    <li>
      Negative Toxoplasma IgG substantially lowers the probability of cerebral toxoplasmosis
      (fewer than 5% of cases are seronegative); failure to improve after 14 days of empiric
      pyrimethamine and sulfadiazine should prompt urgent workup for alternative diagnoses.
    </li>
    <li>
      Mobile flagellated trypomastigotes on CSF wet mount are pathognomonic for CNS Chagas
      reactivation, establishing the diagnosis without biopsy; blood smear examination may
      also reveal trypomastigotes during reactivation.
    </li>
    <li>
      Benznidazole 5 to 7 mg/kg/day in two divided doses for 60 days is the preferred
      treatment; nifurtimox is an active but less well-tolerated alternative dosed more
      frequently and for a longer duration.
    </li>
    <li>
      Antiretroviral therapy initiation is essential: sustained CD4 recovery above 200
      cells/μL is the most reliable protection against recurrence, and secondary prophylaxis
      with benznidazole may be required until immune reconstitution is achieved.
    </li>
  </ul>
