<h2 className="text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900 mb-6">
  Rationale
</h2>

<div className="mx-auto max-w-5xl">
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    <em>Naegleria fowleri</em> is a thermophilic free-living amoeba found in warm freshwater, soil, hot springs, and poorly maintained recreational water systems. It has three life cycle stages: a resistant cyst (present in the environment but not in human tissue), a trophozoite (the feeding, reproductive, and tissue-destructive form, 10 to 35 micrometers, with lobate pseudopods and a prominent central karyosome), and a transient flagellated form (non-feeding, non-dividing, seen in the laboratory when trophozoites are placed in distilled water). Only trophozoites are found in human CSF and brain tissue.
  </p>
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    The pathogenesis of PAM follows a specific anatomic pathway. Trophozoites in contaminated water enter the nasal passages during swimming, diving, or nasal irrigation. They adhere to the nasal mucosa, migrate to the olfactory neuroepithelium, and traverse the cribriform plate along the olfactory nerve filaments to access the subarachnoid space. The first structures destroyed are the olfactory bulbs, which explains why early anosmia or ageusia is a characteristic localizing sign. From there, the infection spreads as a hemorrhagic, necrotizing meningoencephalitis involving the basal forebrain, temporal and frontal lobes, and eventually the entire cerebral cortex. The median time from symptom onset to death in confirmed cases is approximately 5 days.
  </p>
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Critically, <em>N. fowleri</em> is NOT acquired by drinking contaminated water. Trophozoites are killed by stomach acid and cannot penetrate gastrointestinal mucosa. The organism requires direct access to the olfactory neuroepithelium via the nasal route. Person-to-person transmission has never been documented.
  </p>
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    The CSF profile in PAM closely mimics acute bacterial meningitis: a neutrophil-predominant pleocytosis often in the thousands, profoundly low glucose, markedly elevated protein, and elevated red blood cells reflecting the hemorrhagic nature of the necrosis. In published series, 75% of patients were initially treated with antibacterial therapy for presumed bacterial meningitis before the correct diagnosis was established. The key to diagnosis is maintaining a high clinical suspicion based on the exposure history and performing a wet mount on fresh, room-temperature CSF. The wet mount must not be performed on refrigerated CSF, because cold temperatures cause the thermophilic trophozoites to round up and stop moving. Heat fixation for Gram stain destroys the trophozoites entirely, which is why the CDC explicitly recommends avoiding Gram stain when PAM is suspected.
  </p>
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    The current CDC-recommended treatment regimen, updated in November 2025, is a multi-drug combination: conventional (deoxycholate) amphotericin B administered both intravenously and intrathecally, miltefosine, azithromycin, posaconazole (which has replaced fluconazole due to superior in vitro activity), rifampin, and dexamethasone for cerebral edema management. Conventional amphotericin B is specifically preferred over the liposomal formulation because its minimum inhibitory concentration against <em>N. fowleri</em> is approximately tenfold lower. The investigational drug nitroxoline may also be available through the CDC under expanded access.
  </p>
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    PAM must be distinguished from granulomatous amebic encephalitis (GAE) caused by <em>Balamuthia mandrillaris</em> and <em>Acanthamoeba</em> species. PAM is fulminant, evolving over days, in previously healthy individuals with warm freshwater exposure; it is diagnosed by CSF wet mount showing motile trophozoites and treated with an amphotericin B-based regimen. GAE is subacute to chronic, evolving over weeks to months, may occur in immunocompromised or immunocompetent hosts, and <em>Balamuthia</em> GAE is often preceded by a characteristic central facial plaque; it is diagnosed by brain biopsy with immunostaining or PCR, and treated with a miltefosine-based combination that does not include amphotericin B.
  </p>
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    The case fatality rate for PAM exceeds 97%, with only five documented survivors in North America. The strongest predictor of survival in these cases was early treatment initiation — the median time from symptom onset to treatment in survivors was 2.5 days. The CDC Emergency Operations Center provides 24-hour diagnostic assistance and treatment guidance at 770-488-7100.
  </p>
