<h2 className="text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900 mb-6">
  Rationale
</h2>

<div className="mx-auto max-w-5xl">
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Multicentric Castleman disease (MCD) is a lymphoproliferative disorder
    characterized by systemic lymphadenopathy, splenomegaly, and pronounced
    constitutional symptoms driven by a cytokine storm dominated by interleukin-6
    signaling. Two major subtypes are recognized: HHV-8-associated MCD, which
    occurs predominantly in immunosuppressed patients, and idiopathic MCD
    (iMCD), which is HHV-8-negative. Distinguishing these entities is critical
    because their treatment strategies differ fundamentally.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    HHV-8, also known as Kaposi sarcoma-associated herpesvirus (KSHV), is an
    enveloped double-stranded DNA virus classified in the genus
    <em> Rhadinovirus</em> within the <em>Gammaherpesvirinae</em> subfamily of
    <em>Herpesviridae</em>. This is the same subfamily that includes
    Epstein-Barr virus (HHV-4), and both viruses share a tropism for
    lymphocytes and the capacity to establish lifelong latency. HHV-8
    establishes latent infection in B lymphocytes, where the viral episome is
    tethered to host chromatin by latency-associated nuclear antigen-1
    (LANA-1). Immunohistochemical staining for LANA-1 on lymph node tissue is
    the diagnostic gold standard for confirming HHV-8 involvement in Castleman
    disease, as it identifies the virus within the scattered plasmablasts that
    populate the interfollicular regions of affected nodes.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    The pathogenic mechanism centers on viral interleukin-6 (vIL-6), a
    homolog of human IL-6 encoded by the HHV-8 genome. Unlike human IL-6,
    which requires binding to both the membrane-bound IL-6 receptor and the
    gp130 signal-transducing subunit, vIL-6 can activate the gp130/JAK/STAT
    signaling pathway directly without requiring the IL-6 receptor. This
    receptor-independent signaling drives B-cell proliferation, stimulates
    hepatic acute-phase reactant production, and generates the systemic
    inflammatory phenotype that defines the disease: fever, night sweats,
    weight loss, elevated CRP and ESR, anemia, and hypoalbuminemia. The
    plasmablasts seen on biopsy are the primary source of vIL-6, and their
    presence is what distinguishes HHV-8-associated MCD from the idiopathic
    form on histopathology.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    The clinical presentation in this patient is characteristic. An
    HIV-infected individual with a CD4 count below 200 cells/&#956;L develops
    subacute systemic inflammation with diffuse lymphadenopathy,
    splenomegaly, and laboratory evidence of a hyperinflammatory state. The
    differential diagnosis is broad in this context and includes Hodgkin
    lymphoma, disseminated mycobacterial infection, Kaposi sarcoma with
    lymph node involvement, and idiopathic MCD. The lymph node biopsy
    resolves the question: Castleman disease histology with plasmablasts in
    the interfollicular zones identifies the HHV-8-associated form. On
    imaging, the intensely enhancing retroperitoneal mass with calcifications
    is a classic CT appearance of Castleman disease, reflecting the
    hypervascular nature of the affected lymphoid tissue.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Treatment of HHV-8-associated MCD targets the infected B-cell reservoir
    with rituximab, a chimeric monoclonal antibody against CD20. By depleting
    CD20-positive B lymphocytes, rituximab eliminates the cells that harbor
    HHV-8 and produce vIL-6, interrupting the pathogenic cytokine cascade at
    its source. This mechanistic rationale is supported by clinical outcomes:
    a prospective cohort of 61 HIV-associated MCD patients treated with
    rituximab-based therapy demonstrated 2-year overall survival of 94% and
    5-year overall survival of 90%, a dramatic improvement over the 42% and
    33% survival rates observed in the pre-rituximab era. In contrast,
    siltuximab, an anti-IL-6 monoclonal antibody, is the approved treatment
    for iMCD but does not address the viral reservoir and is not appropriate
    for the HHV-8-associated form. Ganciclovir targets lytic viral
    replication but does not effectively eradicate latently infected cells.
    ART alone, while essential for long-term immune health, does not control
    active Castleman disease when the HIV viral load is already suppressed.
  </p>
</div>
