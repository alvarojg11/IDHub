<h2 className="text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900 mb-6">
  Rationale
</h2>

<div className="mx-auto max-w-5xl">
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome (PRES) is a clinico-radiological syndrome
    characterized by headache, seizures, altered consciousness, and visual disturbances in
    association with bilateral posterior-predominant white matter changes on MRI. It is a
    well-recognized complication of solid-organ transplantation, reported in 0.5–5% of
    kidney transplant recipients, and tacrolimus is the most commonly implicated agent.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    The pathophysiology centers on failure of cerebrovascular autoregulation. Under
    physiologic conditions, cerebral arterioles constrict in response to rising mean
    arterial pressure to maintain constant cerebral blood flow. When this autoregulatory
    capacity is overwhelmed — by severe hypertension, calcineurin inhibitor–mediated
    endothelial injury, or both — the result is forced dilatation of cerebral arterioles,
    breakthrough hyperperfusion, and vasogenic edema. The posterior cerebral circulation
    is disproportionately affected because it has sparser sympathetic innervation compared
    to the anterior circulation, making it less able to mount a vasoconstrictive response.
    This accounts for the characteristic posterior parieto-occipital distribution.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    From an infectious diseases perspective, the critical diagnostic task in this setting
    is distinguishing PRES from CNS opportunistic infection. Both can present with
    encephalopathy and seizures in an immunosuppressed transplant recipient. The key
    features favoring PRES over infection are: (1) non-inflammatory CSF; (2) bilateral
    posterior FLAIR hyperintensities without restricted diffusion; (3) severe hypertension;
    (4) supratherapeutic or high-normal tacrolimus level; and (5) rapid onset (days rather
    than weeks). Normal or near-normal CSF is the single most important feature arguing
    against meningitis or encephalitis caused by CMV, Cryptococcus, HSV, or other organisms.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    The absence of restricted diffusion on diffusion-weighted MRI is equally important.
    Vasogenic edema — the hallmark of PRES — shows normal or elevated apparent diffusion
    coefficient (ADC) values because water moves freely in the extracellular space.
    Ischemic injury, by contrast, causes cytotoxic edema with restricted diffusion and
    low ADC values. This distinction differentiates PRES from posterior circulation stroke,
    which can present with similar visual loss and altered consciousness.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    An important clinical trap is attributing the mild creatinine rise to acute rejection
    and responding with high-dose steroids. This would worsen hypertension, increase
    fluid retention, and exacerbate the vasogenic edema driving PRES. In cases where
    graft dysfunction and PRES coexist, transplant nephrology guidance is essential before
    escalating immunosuppression. The tacrolimus level itself — supratherapeutic here at
    15.8 ng/mL — mandates dose reduction or temporary discontinuation regardless of
    whether rejection is suspected.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    With prompt treatment, the prognosis of PRES is generally favorable. MRI changes and
    neurologic deficits are typically reversible within days to weeks. However, delayed
    recognition — particularly when PRES progresses to ischemic infarction — can result
    in permanent neurologic injury. Cortical blindness, as in this case, is usually fully
    reversible with timely intervention.
  </p>
</div>
