<h2 className="text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900 mb-6">
  Rationale
</h2>

<div className="mx-auto max-w-5xl">
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    <em>Clonorchis sinensis</em>, the Chinese liver fluke, is a foodborne
    trematode that infects an estimated 15 million people worldwide and is
    endemic throughout East Asia, with the highest burden in China (particularly
    Guangdong and Guangxi provinces), Korea, Taiwan, and northern Vietnam.
    Humans acquire infection by eating raw or undercooked freshwater fish of the
    family Cyprinidae (carp and their relatives), in whose muscle tissue the
    infective metacercariae encyst. Lightly salted, smoked, or pickled fish may
    still harbor viable organisms. Classic cultural dishes associated with
    transmission include Korean <em>hoe</em> (raw freshwater fish), Vietnamese
    <em>goi ca</em>, and Chinese raw-fish congee. Reservoir hosts include dogs,
    cats, pigs, and rats, which maintain the zoonotic cycle. After ingestion,
    metacercariae excyst in the duodenum and the immature flukes migrate through
    the ampulla of Vater up into the intrahepatic bile ducts, where they mature
    into adult flukes measuring 10 to 25 mm in length. They can survive for an
    extraordinary 20 to 30 years in the human host, making even remote dietary
    exposures clinically relevant decades later.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Most infections are asymptomatic or produce only vague abdominal discomfort,
    and the fluke's presence may go undetected for decades. Heavier infections
    cause recurrent pyogenic cholangitis, biliary colic, obstructive jaundice,
    and pancreatitis; the flukes can also serve as nidi for gallstone formation.
    The most devastating consequence is cholangiocarcinoma, which is why
    <em> C. sinensis</em> was classified as an IARC Group 1 carcinogen in 2009
    (published in the IARC Monograph Volume 100B, 2012) alongside its close
    relative <em>Opisthorchis viverrini</em>, the Southeast Asian liver fluke.
    Critically, <em>Fasciola hepatica</em> &mdash; another biliary trematode
    &mdash; is not classified as carcinogenic, underscoring that the cancer risk
    is not a generic property of biliary flukes but is specific to the sucker-
    mediated injury pattern of <em>Clonorchis</em> and <em>Opisthorchis</em>.
    A dose-response relationship exists between infection intensity, duration,
    and cholangiocarcinoma risk.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    The carcinogenic mechanism is a multi-step, inflammation-driven process.
    Adult flukes anchor themselves to the bile duct epithelium using their oral
    and ventral suckers, producing chronic mechanical erosion, ulceration, and
    desquamation of the ductal lining. This persistent injury recruits
    inflammatory cells &mdash; macrophages, neutrophils, and eosinophils &mdash;
    that generate large quantities of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and reactive
    nitrogen species (RNS), with nitric oxide playing a particularly important
    role. These oxidizing and nitrosating agents cause DNA strand breaks, base
    modifications, and inhibition of DNA repair enzymes, driving genomic
    instability in the replicating biliary epithelium. The epithelium undergoes
    progressive proliferative change: from simple hyperplasia to adenomatous
    (&ldquo;mulberry-like&rdquo;) proliferation and finally to invasive
    cholangiocarcinoma. This pathway &mdash; chronic injury, inflammation-driven
    DNA damage, and regenerative proliferation culminating in cancer &mdash; is
    a paradigm of inflammation-associated oncogenesis that shares mechanistic
    features with Helicobacter pylori-associated gastric cancer and
    schistosomiasis-associated bladder cancer.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Diagnosis rests on demonstrating the characteristic operculate eggs in stool.
    Three specimens on separate days are recommended because egg shedding can be
    intermittent. The eggs are small (approximately 27&ndash;35 &micro;m),
    yellowish-brown, and possess a distinct operculum with opercular shoulders
    and a small terminal knob at the abopercular end &mdash; features that help
    distinguish them from other trematode eggs, though they are nearly
    indistinguishable from <em>Opisthorchis</em> eggs by routine microscopy.
    Imaging with ultrasound, CT, or MRCP typically reveals diffuse, uniform
    dilation of the peripheral intrahepatic bile ducts. ERCP is both diagnostic
    and therapeutic: it may directly visualize adult flukes within the ducts and
    permits biliary decompression and fluke extraction. Serologic testing (ELISA)
    is available but cannot distinguish current from past infection and is not
    useful for determining whether a prior fluke infection caused a present
    cholangiocarcinoma.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    The drug of choice is praziquantel, dosed at 25 mg/kg three times daily for
    two consecutive days (total 75 mg/kg/day), with cure rates of 85 to 100%.
    Praziquantel increases calcium permeability across the fluke's tegument,
    causing a sustained influx of calcium that produces tetanic contraction and
    paralysis of the parasite, after which the host immune system clears the
    immobilized worms. However, the most critical counseling point for this
    patient is that deworming treats the cause but not the consequence: decades
    of mechanical and inflammatory injury have already driven the biliary
    epithelium through adenomatous hyperplasia to invasive
    cholangiocarcinoma, and eliminating the fluke does not reliably reverse or
    halt this process once premalignant or malignant changes are established.
    The cholangiocarcinoma requires its own oncologic management &mdash;
    surgical resection with negative margins if the tumor is operable, or
    systemic chemotherapy with gemcitabine and cisplatin (established by the
    ABC-02 trial) for advanced disease &mdash; alongside biliary stenting for
    palliation of obstruction. Praziquantel is necessary to stop ongoing
    parasitic injury, but it is not cancer therapy.
  </p>
</div>
