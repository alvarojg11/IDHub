<h2 className="text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900 mb-6">
  Rationale
</h2>

<div className="mx-auto max-w-5xl">
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    <em>Fasciola hepatica</em> is a large liver fluke (trematode) with a worldwide distribution,
    causing disease primarily in cattle and sheep but with significant human infection in
    endemic regions. Bolivia — particularly the altiplano surrounding Lake Titicaca and the
    departments of Oruro, La Paz, and Potosí — has some of the highest rates of human
    fascioliasis recorded globally, with community prevalence reaching 15 to 67% in some
    hyperendemic villages. Peru, Ecuador, Egypt, and Iran are other high-burden regions.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    The life cycle of <em>F. hepatica</em> depends on a freshwater snail intermediate host
    (most commonly <em>Lymnaea</em> species) in which cercariae develop and are released
    into the water, where they encyst as metacercariae on aquatic vegetation. Human infection
    occurs almost exclusively through ingestion of metacercariae on raw watercress
    (<em>berros</em>), other aquatic plants, or contaminated water. In Andean communities,
    watercress gathered from irrigation channels shared with livestock is the dominant
    transmission route.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Fascioliasis has two clinically distinct phases. In the <strong>acute (migratory) phase</strong>,
    which begins within days to weeks of ingestion, excysted juvenile flukes penetrate the
    intestinal wall, cross the peritoneal cavity, and bore through Glisson's capsule into the
    liver parenchyma, where they tunnel through hepatic tissue toward the bile ducts. This
    phase produces the febrile hepatitis syndrome seen in this patient: fever, right upper
    quadrant pain, hepatomegaly, striking peripheral eosinophilia (often exceeding 30 to 50%),
    and elevated liver enzymes with a disproportionate rise in alkaline phosphatase reflecting
    biliary involvement. CT during this phase characteristically shows multiple hypodense
    nodular lesions with linear branching or tunneling tracts in the hepatic parenchyma — the
    radiographic footprint of larval migration.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    In the <strong>chronic (biliary) phase</strong>, adult worms reach the intrahepatic and
    common bile ducts, where they can survive for years. Symptoms shift to biliary colic,
    recurrent cholangitis, cholelithiasis, and biliary obstruction. Eosinophilia may decrease
    or normalize. Fasciola eggs first appear in the stool in this phase, typically 3 to 4 months
    after initial infection — which explains why three consecutive stool O&P examinations were
    negative in this patient who is still in the acute migratory phase.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    The treatment landscape for fascioliasis is defined by one critical pharmacological fact:
    <em>Fasciola hepatica</em> is intrinsically resistant to praziquantel. Unlike virtually
    all other trematode infections — schistosomiasis, clonorchiasis, opisthorchiasis,
    paragonimiasis — for which praziquantel is highly effective, <em>F. hepatica</em> does
    not respond to praziquantel at any dose. The mechanism involves differences in the fluke's
    tegument and reduced drug accumulation. Triclabendazole, a benzimidazole derivative, is
    the only agent with proven efficacy against all developmental stages of <em>F. hepatica</em>.
    A single oral dose of 10 mg/kg with food achieves cure rates exceeding 80 to 90% in most
    series; a second dose is reserved for treatment failure or severe disease.
  </p>
</div>
