<h2 className="text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900 mb-6">
  Rationale
</h2>

<div className="mx-auto max-w-5xl">
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Chromoblastomycosis is a chronic, progressive subcutaneous mycosis caused by
    melanized (dematiaceous) fungi of the order Chaetothyriales. The most common
    causative species worldwide is <em>Fonsecaea pedrosoi</em>, followed by
    <em>Cladophialophora carrionii</em> (more prevalent in arid regions),
    <em>Phialophora verrucosa</em>, and <em>Rhinocladiella aquaspersa</em>.
    The disease is endemic in tropical and subtropical regions with high rainfall
    and abundant vegetation, including Brazil, Madagascar, India, and parts of
    Asia. Agricultural workers with regular skin contact with soil, wood, and
    decaying plant material are at highest risk. Infection follows traumatic
    inoculation of fungal elements into the skin, usually through a minor wound
    from thorns, splinters, or vegetable matter.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    The clinical evolution is characteristically slow: lesions evolve over months
    to years from a small papule into verrucous, nodular, or plaque-type lesions
    with a warty, cauliflower-like surface. The lower extremities are affected in
    the majority of cases. Despite the striking appearance, systemic dissemination
    is rare in immunocompetent hosts; the infection remains localized to the
    subcutaneous tissues and skin. Secondary bacterial superinfection and
    lymphedema are common complications in long-standing disease. There is a
    well-established association between chromoblastomycosis and the development
    of squamous cell carcinoma arising in chronic lesions, underscoring the
    importance of treatment even in patients with slowly progressive disease.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    The pathognomonic tissue finding is the Medlar body (also called sclerotic
    body or muriform cell): a brown to golden-brown, thick-walled, spherical
    to polyhedral cell measuring 5 to 12 micrometers, with transverse and
    longitudinal internal septation producing a multilocular appearance. These
    cells are often described as resembling copper pennies in hematoxylin and
    eosin sections. They reproduce by septation rather than budding, a process
    called muriform division, and represent the tissue parasitic form regardless
    of which species is responsible. The presence of Medlar bodies in a skin
    biopsy from a patient with verrucous lesions is diagnostic of chromoblastomycosis
    and does not require culture confirmation for diagnosis, though culture is
    performed to identify the causative species.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    <em>Fonsecaea pedrosoi</em> is distinguished in culture by its polymorphic
    conidiation: it simultaneously produces conidia by three different mechanisms
    (Rhinocladiella-type, Cladosporium-type, and Phialophora-type), a feature
    that separates it from other dematiaceous fungi. Treatment is prolonged and
    challenging. Itraconazole 200 to 400 mg daily for at least 12 months is the
    standard of care, with terbinafine 250 to 500 mg daily as an alternative or
    combination partner for severe disease. Voriconazole and posaconazole have
    demonstrated activity in vitro and in case reports and may be considered for
    refractory cases. Amphotericin B and fluconazole have limited clinical efficacy
    and are not recommended. Local thermotherapy (application of heat pads to
    raise tissue temperature, exploiting the thermal sensitivity of the organism)
    can be used as an adjunct. Cure is defined as clinical resolution with negative
    biopsy and culture, which may require years of treatment in extensive disease.
  </p>
</div>

    </li>
