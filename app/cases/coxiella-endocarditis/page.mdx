<h2 className="text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900 mb-6">
  Rationale
</h2>

<div className="mx-auto max-w-5xl">
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Coxiella burnetii is the agent of Q fever and one of the classic causes of
    culture-negative endocarditis worldwide. The organism is an obligate
    intracellular bacterium that replicates within the phagolysosome of infected
    monocytes and macrophages, a niche that renders it invisible to routine blood
    culture systems and resistant to many antibiotics that cannot penetrate or
    function in an acidic intracellular compartment.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Chronic Q fever endocarditis develops almost exclusively in patients with
    pre-existing valvular pathology. Bicuspid aortic valve, prosthetic valves,
    rheumatic valve disease, and other structural abnormalities create the
    substrate on which the organism establishes persistent infection. In the
    Queensland series covering 16 years of cases, 92% of confirmed Q fever
    endocarditis patients had an underlying valvular defect. The disease is
    indolent: fevers may be low-grade or absent, and the dominant presentation
    is often months of fatigue, weight loss, and arthralgias that mimic a
    rheumatologic condition before a murmur or embolic event triggers cardiac
    evaluation.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    The zoonotic reservoirs are farm animals, particularly sheep, cattle, and
    goats. Parturient cats are a well-documented source because the organism
    reaches enormous concentrations in the placenta and birth products of
    infected animals. Amniotic fluid, placental tissue, and contaminated dust
    from birthing environments can transmit infection through aerosolized
    particles that travel considerable distances. Unpasteurized milk from
    infected goats or cows is another recognized route. The patient in this case
    had multiple overlapping exposures: a sheep farming occupation, direct
    contact with a parturient cat and newborn kittens in a barn, and regular
    raw goat milk consumption.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    The diagnostic key is the Phase I and Phase II IgG serology by indirect
    immunofluorescence. C. burnetii undergoes antigenic variation between Phase
    I (smooth, full-length lipopolysaccharide, virulent form) and Phase II
    (rough, truncated lipopolysaccharide, avirulent in immunocompetent hosts).
    Acute Q fever generates a dominant Phase II IgG response. Chronic infection
    flips this pattern: Phase I IgG rises to equal or exceed Phase II, and the
    absolute Phase I titer climbs above 1:800. Titers above 1:1600 are
    considered highly specific for endocarditis when the clinical context fits.
    This inversion is not a laboratory artifact; it reflects the immune
    system's ongoing struggle with a pathogen that persists inside cells by
    adapting its surface antigens.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Treatment targets the intracellular niche. Doxycycline alone is insufficient
    because the acidic pH of the Coxiella-containing phagolysosome neutralizes
    its bactericidal mechanism. Hydroxychloroquine raises the phagolysosomal pH,
    restoring doxycycline activity and converting the regimen from bacteriostatic
    to bactericidal in vitro. The combination of doxycycline 100 mg twice daily
    with hydroxychloroquine 200 mg three times daily for at least 18 months in
    native valve infection, and 24 months for prosthetic valve infection, is the
    regimen with the most clinical experience. The Million 26-year survey of 104
    patients demonstrated that 18 months of treatment was sufficient to
    sterilize valves in nearly all native valve cases, while relapse was
    associated with shorter courses. Monitoring Phase I IgG titers every 3 to 6
    months guides the treatment endpoint: a fourfold decline predicts cure, and
    patients should be followed serologically for at least 5 years because late
    relapse remains a risk.
  </p>
</div>
