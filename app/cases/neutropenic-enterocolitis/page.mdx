<h2 className="text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900 mb-6">
  Rationale
</h2>

<div className="mx-auto max-w-5xl">
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Neutropenic enterocolitis, or typhlitis, is a necrotizing inflammation of
    the bowel wall that complicates profound neutropenia, most often in the
    window of maximal myelosuppression ten to fourteen days after cytotoxic
    chemotherapy. The pathophysiology is a two-hit process: chemotherapy first
    strips the rapidly dividing intestinal mucosa, producing denuded epithelium
    and impaired barrier function, and the resulting neutropenia then removes
    the phagocytic defense that would normally contain translocating bacteria.
    The cecum is the classic site because it is the most distensible segment of
    colon, with the thinnest wall and a relatively limited blood supply, making
    it vulnerable to ischemia, distension-related injury, and stasis of its
    microbe-rich contents. Although historically described in acute leukemia
    induction and hematopoietic stem cell transplant conditioning, typhlitis is
    increasingly recognized after intensive solid-tumor regimens such as the
    cisplatin and etoposide used for high-grade neuroendocrine carcinoma, which
    produce comparable depths and durations of neutropenia.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    The diagnosis is clinical and radiographic, not endoscopic. In a neutropenic
    patient with fever and abdominal pain — typically localizing to the right
    lower quadrant — contrast-enhanced CT demonstrating circumferential bowel
    wall thickening (greater than 4 mm) of the cecum and terminal ileum, with
    peri-colonic fat stranding and sometimes pneumatosis intestinalis,
    establishes the diagnosis. Crucially, C. difficile colitis must always be
    excluded because it can mimic and coexist with typhlitis, but a negative
    NAAT combined with a focal ileocecal pattern points away from
    pseudomembranous colitis, which is typically diffuse and pancolonic.
    Diagnostic colonoscopy is avoided because the fragile bowel wall is at high
    risk of perforation. The microbiology reflects the source: polymicrobial
    translocation of enteric gram-negative bacilli, obligate anaerobes,
    enterococci, and, with prolonged neutropenia, Candida. Among the clostridia,
    Clostridium septicum is classically linked to typhlitis and underlying
    malignancy, and its alpha-toxin can convert a contained wall infection into
    gas gangrene and myonecrosis — making any new crepitus or rapid hemodynamic
    collapse a surgical emergency.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    The management principle that this case turns on is that the standard
    neutropenic fever regimen is not sufficient for typhlitis. Cefepime
    monotherapy — the IDSA-recommended empiric backbone for uncomplicated
    febrile neutropenia — covers Pseudomonas and aerobic gram-negatives but has
    unreliable activity against the obligate anaerobes that populate the
    ulcerated bowel wall. Typhlitis demands an agent that covers both
    anti-pseudomonal gram-negatives and anaerobes from the outset, such as
    piperacillin-tazobactam or a carbapenem as monotherapy, or the addition of
    metronidazole to cefepime. Gram-positive coverage with vancomycin or
    linezolid is reserved for specific indications — severe mucositis, known
    MRSA risk, catheter-related infection, or hemodynamic instability — rather
    than reflexive escalation, which is a core antimicrobial stewardship
    principle. Antifungal escalation, typically to an echinocandin for
    candidemia or a mold-active azole or amphotericin depending on risk and
    imaging, follows the IDSA guidance of persistent fever despite four to seven
    days of appropriate antibacterials, and is guided by serial fungal markers
    and high-resolution imaging rather than reflex empiricism on day one.
    Supportive therapy — G-CSF to hasten neutrophil recovery, strict bowel rest,
    nasogastric decompression for ileus, and meticulous supportive care — is
    essential, and early surgical consultation ensures that a surgeon is already
    engaged if perforation, uncontrolled hemorrhage, or refractory sepsis
    develops. Antibiotics are continued until clinical resolution and sustained
    count recovery, and de-escalated according to culture data.
  </p>
</div>
