<h2 className="text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900 mb-6">
  Rationale
</h2>

<div className="mx-auto max-w-5xl">
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    The diagnostic challenge here is cognitive: a clinician who frames malaria as something that requires recent travel or inadequate prophylaxis will miss this diagnosis entirely. The patient took her prophylaxis correctly, felt well during and after travel, and presents six months later with what looks like a viral syndrome with a curious cyclical fever pattern. The 48-hour periodicity of the fevers, the splenomegaly, the thrombocytopenia, the mild hemolytic anemia, and the travel history to Papua New Guinea combine to make malaria the working diagnosis. The smear confirms it, and the species profile (enlarged erythrocytes, ameboid trophozoites, Schüffner's stippling, pLDH-positive and HRP2-negative RDT) is diagnostic of <em>P. vivax</em>.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    <em>Plasmodium vivax</em> is the second most common cause of malaria worldwide and the most geographically widespread, with significant transmission in South Asia, Southeast Asia, the Pacific, and Latin America. Its defining biological feature is the formation of hypnozoites: dormant liver-stage forms that develop from sporozoites inoculated during the mosquito bite and enter a state of metabolic quiescence rather than immediately initiating blood-stage infection. Short-latency tropical strains, predominant in PNG and the Pacific, reactivate most commonly at three to nine months. Long-latency temperate strains, found historically in parts of Europe and the Middle East, may remain dormant for more than a year. This hypnozoite biology explains why atovaquone-proguanil, an excellent prophylactic drug with causal activity against <em>P. falciparum</em> liver stages, completely failed to prevent this patient's illness: it has no anti-hypnozoite activity and the P. vivax liver-stage parasites established silently during her time in PNG, surviving the prophylaxis course and the post-travel tail.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Diagnosis relies on the combination of thick and thin blood smear, rapid diagnostic testing, and PCR. On thin smear, P. vivax trophozoites are recognized by three features absent in other species: erythrocyte enlargement, Schüffner's stippling (fine eosinophilic dots representing membrane protein deposition), and an ameboid, irregular cytoplasmic outline. The RDT detects pan-Plasmodium pLDH (positive for any species) and P. falciparum-specific HRP2; a pLDH-positive, HRP2-negative result in a traveler from PNG is P. vivax until proven otherwise and directs the clinician away from falciparum-specific treatment algorithms. PCR provides species confirmation and detects mixed infections.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Treatment requires two components. The blood stage is treated with chloroquine in sensitive regions, but Papua New Guinea has documented chloroquine-resistant <em>P. vivax</em>, and artemisinin-based combination therapy is a reasonable blood-stage alternative here. The critical and non-negotiable second component is radical cure: eradication of the hepatic hypnozoite reservoir. Primaquine at 30 mg base daily for 14 days is the standard agent. Tafenoquine at a single 300 mg dose after blood-stage treatment is an FDA-approved alternative with better adherence. Both drugs are 8-aminoquinolines and both cause dose-dependent acute hemolytic anemia in G6PD-deficient individuals; G6PD testing before administration is mandatory. Primaquine and tafenoquine are contraindicated in pregnancy and in individuals with severe G6PD deficiency. For prophylaxis in travelers to high-vivax-burden regions, primaquine (30 mg base daily) is the only standard option that also prevents hypnozoite formation; tafenoquine (200 mg weekly) is the FDA-approved alternative. All other standard prophylaxis drugs protect against blood-stage disease but leave the door open for vivax relapse.
  </p>
</div>
