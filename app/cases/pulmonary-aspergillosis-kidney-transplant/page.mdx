<h2 className="text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900 mb-6">
  Rationale
</h2>

<div className="mx-auto max-w-5xl">
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Invasive aspergillosis is the most common invasive mold infection in solid organ transplant recipients, with an incidence of approximately 1 to 9% depending on the organ transplanted. Kidney transplant recipients have a lower incidence than lung or liver recipients but still face substantial risk, particularly during periods of augmented immunosuppression. The median time to onset after kidney transplantation is approximately 4 to 6 months, coinciding with the window when maintenance immunosuppression is still relatively high and when rejection episodes often trigger additional corticosteroid pulses.
  </p>
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    The pathogenesis of invasive pulmonary aspergillosis in the non-neutropenic host differs from the classic neutropenic model. In neutropenic patients, the primary deficit is the absence of neutrophil-mediated hyphal killing, allowing angioinvasion to proceed unchecked. In solid organ transplant recipients, the key defect is impaired macrophage function and T-cell mediated immunity from calcineurin inhibition and corticosteroids. Corticosteroids are particularly potent risk amplifiers because they suppress both alveolar macrophage activity, which is the first line of defense against inhaled conidia, and Th1 responses that coordinate antifungal immunity. A pulse of high-dose methylprednisolone can suppress macrophage conidial killing for weeks, creating a window of vulnerability during which environmental or previously colonizing <em>Aspergillus</em> conidia can germinate, invade tissue, and enter the vasculature.
  </p>
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    The halo sign on chest CT, which refers to ground-glass attenuation surrounding a pulmonary nodule, is a radiographic hallmark of angioinvasive mold infection. It represents perinodular hemorrhage caused by hyphal invasion of pulmonary vessels. While classically described in neutropenic patients with invasive aspergillosis, it is also seen in non-neutropenic SOT recipients, though it may be less prominent and less frequently present because residual neutrophil function limits the extent of hemorrhagic infarction. The combination of multiple bilateral peripheral nodules, at least some cavitation, and surrounding ground-glass halos in a recently steroid-pulsed transplant recipient should immediately raise concern for invasive mold infection.
  </p>
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    CNS dissemination occurs in approximately 10 to 20% of solid organ transplant recipients with invasive pulmonary aspergillosis. <em>Aspergillus</em> hyphae fragment during vascular invasion, travel as septic emboli through the systemic circulation, and lodge in the cerebral vasculature where they cause angioinvasive lesions with surrounding infarction and edema. The resulting brain lesions are typically ring-enhancing on contrast MRI, reflecting central necrosis surrounded by a rim of inflammatory tissue and edema. The clinical presentation ranges from headache and subtle cognitive changes, as in this patient, to seizures, focal motor deficits, and rapid neurologic deterioration. The frontal lobe is a common location because of preferential middle cerebral artery territory seeding.
  </p>
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    The diagnostic approach to invasive aspergillosis in SOT recipients must account for the well-documented limitations of serum biomarkers in this population. Serum galactomannan, a polysaccharide component of the <em>Aspergillus</em> cell wall released during tissue invasion, has a sensitivity of approximately 80 to 90% in neutropenic hematologic patients but only 22 to 42% in non-neutropenic SOT recipients. The lower sensitivity is attributed to intact neutrophil function that clears circulating galactomannan before it accumulates to detectable levels. BAL galactomannan overcomes this limitation by detecting the antigen at the site of pulmonary infection before systemic clearance can occur, with reported sensitivity of 80 to 90% in SOT recipients when an appropriate optical density index cutoff is used.
  </p>
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Voriconazole is the cornerstone of treatment for invasive aspergillosis, including CNS disease. The landmark 2002 randomized trial by Herbrecht and colleagues demonstrated superior survival and response rates with voriconazole compared to conventional amphotericin B, establishing it as the primary therapy for invasive aspergillosis. Voriconazole achieves CSF concentrations approximately 50% of plasma levels and brain tissue concentrations exceeding plasma, making it the azole of choice for CNS disease. Isavuconazole is a reasonable alternative based on the SECURE trial, which demonstrated non-inferiority for invasive mold infections overall and a more favorable drug interaction profile, but voriconazole remains the best-studied agent for cerebral aspergillosis specifically.
  </p>
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    The voriconazole-tacrolimus interaction is among the most clinically consequential drug interactions in transplant infectious diseases. Voriconazole is a potent CYP3A4 inhibitor, and tacrolimus is almost entirely metabolized by CYP3A4 in the liver and intestines. When voriconazole is initiated, tacrolimus trough concentrations can increase threefold to fivefold within 24 to 48 hours, risking acute nephrotoxicity, neurotoxicity including posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome, and electrolyte disturbances. The recommended approach is to reduce the tacrolimus dose to approximately one-third to one-fifth of the pre-voriconazole dose on the first day of antifungal therapy, then check a trough within 48 to 72 hours and adjust. Both voriconazole and tacrolimus levels should be monitored throughout treatment. Voriconazole metabolism is also affected by CYP2C19 polymorphisms, which can produce wide inter-patient variability in serum concentrations, making therapeutic drug monitoring of voriconazole itself equally important.
  </p>
