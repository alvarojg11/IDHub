<CaseReveal pollId="case-strongy-hyper-q2">
<h2 className="text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900 mb-6">
  Rationale
</h2>

<div className="mx-auto max-w-5xl">
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    <em>Strongyloides stercoralis</em> is a soil-transmitted nematode with a unique ability to
    perpetuate infection through autoinfection. Rhabditiform larvae can mature into infective
    filariform larvae within the host, then penetrate the intestinal mucosa or perianal skin and
    restart the migration cycle. This allows chronic infection to persist for years or even
    lifelong, often with few or nonspecific symptoms.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Hyperinfection syndrome occurs when autoinfection accelerates, typically in the setting of
    impaired cell-mediated immunity. Corticosteroids are the most common trigger and can precipitate
    severe disease within days. HTLV-1 infection is another major risk factor. During hyperinfection,
    larvae are abundant in organs involved in the normal life cycle (GI tract and lungs), causing
    GI symptoms, pulmonary infiltrates, respiratory failure, and a marked increase in parasite burden.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Briefly, hyperinfection refers to marked amplification of the usual autoinfection cycle (mainly
    GI tract and lungs), whereas disseminated strongyloidiasis means larvae have spread to ectopic
    sites beyond the normal life cycle (e.g., CNS, skin, liver, or urinary tract).
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    A common complication is translocation of enteric bacteria across damaged intestinal mucosa during
    larval penetration. This explains the frequent association with gram-negative or polymicrobial
    bacteremia, meningitis, pneumonia, and septic shock. The combination of steroid exposure,
    pulmonary and GI symptoms, and enteric bacteremia should immediately raise suspicion for severe
    strongyloidiasis. Eosinophilia may be absent in hyperinfection and in immunocompromised hosts,
    so a normal eosinophil count does not exclude the diagnosis.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Diagnosis of chronic strongyloidiasis is often difficult because stool shedding is intermittent
    and single stool microscopy has low sensitivity; repeated stool exams (often 3 to 7), stool
    culture techniques (including agar plate culture), and serology are commonly combined. In
    hyperinfection syndrome, however, parasite burden is much higher, and larvae may be identified
    in stool, sputum, bronchoalveolar lavage, and other clinical specimens. Finding larvae in
    respiratory samples strongly supports hyperinfection.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Ivermectin is the cornerstone of treatment. For hyperinfection or disseminated strongyloidiasis,
    daily ivermectin (200 μg/kg/day) is generally recommended for at least 14 days and often longer,
    continued until clinical improvement and repeated parasitologic studies are negative (stool culture). Concurrent
    bacterial/fungal infections must be treated promptly, and immunosuppressive therapy should be
    reduced whenever feasible. In patients unable to absorb oral medication, alternative routes
    (including rectal or subcutaneous veterinary formulations under special protocols) have been
    used in severe cases.
  </p>
</div>

<h2 className="text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900 mb-6">
  Teaching points
</h2>

<div className="mx-auto max-w-5xl">
  <ul className="list-disc pl-6 text-lg leading-7 text-gray-800 space-y-2">
    <li>
      <em>Strongyloides</em> can persist for years through autoinfection, so remote exposure in endemic regions remains clinically relevant.
    </li>
    <li>
      Corticosteroids are the most common trigger for hyperinfection syndrome and can precipitate rapid deterioration.
    </li>
    <li>
      Hyperinfection often presents with combined GI and pulmonary symptoms plus enteric bacteremia or sepsis.
    </li>
    <li>
      Absence of eosinophilia does not exclude severe strongyloidiasis, especially in immunocompromised patients.
    </li>
    <li>
      Chronic strongyloidiasis often requires repeated stool testing and/or serology because of intermittent larval shedding.
    </li>
    <li>
      Hyperinfection requires daily ivermectin and aggressive management of concurrent bacterial/fungal infections; do not delay therapy while waiting for serology.
    </li>
    <li>
      Screening before immunosuppression (especially steroids or transplantation) is essential in patients with epidemiologic risk.
    </li>
  </ul>
