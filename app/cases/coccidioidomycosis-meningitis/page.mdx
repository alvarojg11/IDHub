<h2 className="text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900 mb-6">
  Rationale
</h2>

<div className="mx-auto max-w-5xl">
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Coccidioidal meningitis is the most devastating manifestation of
    <em> Coccidioides immitis</em> or <em>C. posadasii</em> infection. It
    develops in approximately 1% of all symptomatic coccidioidal infections but
    is far more frequent in certain high-risk populations. Filipino ancestry
    carries a dramatically elevated risk of disseminated disease &mdash; on the
    order of 175-fold higher than non-Hispanic whites &mdash; followed by
    African Americans at roughly 10-fold elevated risk. The mechanism is not
    fully elucidated but is considered immunogenetic, likely involving
    differences in innate and Th17-mediated immune responses to the organism's
    spherule phase. Other risk factors for dissemination include HIV/AIDS (CD4
    count below 250 cells/&micro;L), solid organ transplantation, TNF-alpha
    inhibitor therapy, high-dose corticosteroids, pregnancy (especially late
    third trimester and postpartum), and diabetes mellitus. This patient had no
    overt immunosuppression, but his Filipino ancestry and occupational dust
    exposure in Phoenix placed him at substantially elevated risk.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    The clinical hallmark of coccidioidal meningitis is a subacute to chronic
    progressive headache, often evolving over weeks to months and frequently the
    earliest and most prominent symptom. Meningeal signs such as nuchal rigidity
    and Kernig/Brudzinski responses are frequently absent or subtle, which can
    delay diagnosis. Cranial nerve palsies &mdash; particularly bilateral sixth
    (abducens) nerve palsy, as seen in this patient &mdash; reflect the
    predilection of the basilar inflammatory exudate for the skull base
    cisterns through which the cranial nerves traverse. The CSF pattern is
    characteristic: lymphocytic pleocytosis, markedly depressed glucose,
    elevated protein, and, in a meaningful minority of patients, CSF
    eosinophilia. The presence of eosinophils in the CSF is a valuable clue
    that should prompt consideration of coccidioidal meningitis, particularly in
    an endemic area, though it is not universally present. Neuroimaging with
    contrast MRI typically shows basilar leptomeningeal enhancement, reflecting
    the gravity-dependent accumulation of granulomatous exudate at the skull
    base. Hydrocephalus &mdash; usually communicating, from impaired CSF
    resorption at the basal cisterns &mdash; and vasculitic infarcts from
    involvement of penetrating vessels are important complications that may
    develop during the disease course.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    The diagnostic cornerstone for coccidioidal meningitis is the CSF
    <em> Coccidioides</em> complement-fixation antibody, which is positive in
    roughly 70 to 80% of cases and is highly specific; a positive result is
    essentially diagnostic. CSF enzyme immunoassay is more sensitive but less
    specific and is useful as a screening adjunct. CSF culture is frequently
    negative &mdash; a negative culture never excludes the diagnosis &mdash;
    and when positive grows slowly, often requiring weeks. Importantly,
    <em> Coccidioides</em> is a BSL-3 pathogen, and laboratory-acquired
    infections have occurred from culture manipulation, making safe handling in
    a BSL-3 facility essential. Serum serology (EIA, immunodiffusion, and
    complement fixation) should always be obtained in parallel, but the CSF
    complement-fixation titer is the critical result for both diagnosis and
    monitoring. Histopathologic demonstration of spherules in tissue or CSF
    sediment is 100% specific when found but has low sensitivity, since spherules
    are rarely visualized in CSF.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    The treatment of coccidioidal meningitis underwent a paradigm shift with the
    introduction of fluconazole in the late 1980s. Tucker and colleagues
    demonstrated in 1988 that fluconazole achieves CSF concentrations exceeding
    50 to 70% of serum levels &mdash; a CSF-to-plasma ratio far superior to any
    other available antifungal. This property, combined with excellent oral
    bioavailability and tolerability, made high-dose oral fluconazole
    (800&ndash;1,200 mg daily) the first-line therapy, replacing the historical
    standard of intrathecal or intraventricular amphotericin B administered via
    an Ommaya reservoir. Intrathecal amphotericin, while life-saving in the
    pre-azole era, was extraordinarily morbid, causing chemical arachnoiditis,
    seizures, and procedural complications. It is now reserved for refractory
    disease or azole failure. Itraconazole has poor CSF penetration and is not
    preferred for meningitis; voriconazole and isavuconazole are used as salvage
    agents when fluconazole fails, which occurs in approximately 25% of patients.
    Systemic IV amphotericin B penetrates CSF poorly but is used in pregnancy
    (azoles are teratogenic) or in rapidly progressive disease.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Perhaps the most critical management principle is that coccidioidal
    meningitis requires lifelong therapy. There are no validated criteria for
    discontinuation, and the relapse rate after cessation is alarmingly high
    &mdash; approximately 71% in one of the largest cohorts &mdash; with the
    central nervous system having the highest relapse rate of any coccidioidal
    syndrome. This means the clinician is initiating a chronic suppressive
    regimen from the first day of treatment, not a time-limited course. Response
    is monitored through serial lumbar punctures tracking CSF white blood cell
    count, glucose, protein, and complement-fixation titer until they normalize
    or stabilize, with the interval between punctures gradually lengthening as
    improvement is documented. Opening pressure should be measured at each tap,
    since persistent elevation signals developing hydrocephalus that may require
    ventriculoperitoneal shunt placement. The shunt manages the pressure
    complication but does not replace the underlying lifelong antifungal
    therapy. Clinicians must also remain vigilant for vasculitic infarcts,
    cranial nerve dysfunction, and &mdash; in the rare HIV co-infected patient
    &mdash; immune reconstitution inflammatory syndrome upon ART initiation.
  </p>
</div>
