<CaseReveal pollId="case-actinomycosis-q2">
<h2 className="text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900 mb-6">
  Rationale
</h2>

<div className="mx-auto max-w-5xl">
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Actinomycosis is a chronic, slowly progressive bacterial infection caused by
    <em> Actinomyces </em> species and related genera. The most classically implicated
    species is <em>Actinomyces israelii</em>, but human disease also involves other species
    including <em>A. naeslundii</em>, <em>A. viscosus</em>, <em>A. meyeri</em>, and related
    organisms that have undergone taxonomic reclassification. Invasive disease is often
    polymicrobial, with "companion organisms" from oral or gastrointestinal flora.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Epidemiologically, these organisms are endogenous human commensals of oral,
    gastrointestinal, and female genital mucosa. Disease usually follows mucosal disruption
    rather than person-to-person transmission. Common predisposing contexts include dental
    disease or procedures, aspiration risk, bowel inflammation or perforation, prior abdominal
    surgery, and prolonged intrauterine device use for pelvic disease.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Clinical manifestations are broad, but shared hallmarks are chronicity, spread across
    tissue planes, and mass-like lesions that mimic neoplasia. Cervicofacial disease is the
    most common form, while thoracic, abdominal, pelvic, musculoskeletal, and central nervous
    system involvement also occur. Sinus tracts may develop and recur; lymphadenopathy is often
    absent or limited despite extensive local invasion.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Key diagnostic clues include sulfur granules and branching filamentous gram-positive rods
    on pathology. Diagnosis is frequently delayed because cultures can be negative, especially
    after prior antibiotics. When suspected, the microbiology laboratory should be alerted for
    anaerobic processing and prolonged incubation.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    For bulky or deep disease, treatment generally starts with high-dose IV penicillin-class
    therapy, often for 2–6 weeks, then transitions to prolonged oral therapy (commonly total
    duration 6–12 months, individualized by response and serial imaging). Source control,
    including image-guided drainage, is useful when collections are present.
  </p>
</div>
