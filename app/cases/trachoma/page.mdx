<h2 className="text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900 mb-6">
  Rationale
</h2>

<div className="mx-auto max-w-5xl">
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Trachoma is a chronic keratoconjunctivitis caused by recurrent infection with
    ocular serovars of <em>Chlamydia trachomatis</em>, classically A through C. Active
    disease is concentrated in children living in poor, crowded settings with limited
    water access and ongoing transmission through ocular and nasal secretions, fomites,
    and possibly eye-seeking flies. The hallmark examination finding is follicular
    inflammation of the upper tarsal conjunctiva, which requires lid eversion to see.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Under the WHO simplified grading system, trachomatous inflammation-follicular (TF)
    is defined by the presence of five or more follicles at least 0.5 mm in diameter on
    the upper tarsal conjunctiva. This child has the typical age group, exposure
    setting, and conjunctival findings for active trachoma. She does not yet have the
    late blinding complications of conjunctival scarring, entropion, trichiasis, or
    corneal opacity, which develop after repeated episodes of inflammation over many
    years.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Public health management depends on community prevalence. The WHO defines trachoma
    as a public health problem when TF prevalence is at least 10% in children aged 1 to
    9 years or when trachomatous trichiasis prevalence is at least 1% in those older
    than 14 years. With a district-level TF prevalence of about 40%, this scenario
    clearly warrants community-wide control. Oral azithromycin mass treatment is used to
    reduce the chlamydial reservoir, and where baseline prevalence is very high,
    multiple annual rounds and sometimes longer treatment programs may be needed before
    reassessment. These antibiotic efforts should be paired with facial cleanliness and
    environmental improvement, while surgery remains reserved for patients whose lashes
    are already abrading the globe.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Inclusion conjunctivitis remains an important differential diagnosis because it is
    also caused by <em>C. trachomatis</em>, but it is linked to genital serovars D
    through K and follows genital-to-eye transmission in adults or perinatal exposure in
    neonates. In contrast, this case represents classic endemic pediatric trachoma.
  </p>
</div>
