<h2 className="text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900 mb-6">
  Rationale
</h2>

<div className="mx-auto max-w-5xl">
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Chronic granulomatous disease arises from loss-of-function mutations in
    the five subunits of the phagocyte NADPH oxidase (NOX2) complex — most
    often X-linked CYBB (gp91phox, roughly two-thirds of cases), followed by
    autosomal recessive NCF1 (p47phox), the most common AR form in the United
    States and Europe. The assembly of this complex at the phagolysosomal
    membrane transfers electrons from NADPH to oxygen, generating the
    superoxide that dismutates to hydrogen peroxide and feeds the
    myeloperoxidase–halide system. Without this respiratory burst, neutrophils
    still swarm and ingest organisms normally — but the microbes survive
    inside them. Catalase is the trait that separates the organisms that
    plague CGD patients from those that do not: catalase-positive organisms
    (<em>S. aureus</em>, <em>Serratia</em>, <em>Burkholderia cepacia</em>{" "}
    complex, <em>Nocardia</em>, mycobacteria including BCG,{" "}
    <em>Aspergillus</em>) destroy their own hydrogen peroxide, leaving the
    CGD phagocyte with no substrate for oxidative killing. Catalase-negative
    organisms such as pneumococci and streptococci leak the hydrogen peroxide
    they produce, which CGD neutrophils can borrow — which is why invasive
    pneumococcal disease is strikingly rare in CGD. Surviving organisms then
    drive a frustrated, exuberant macrophage response, producing the
    necrotizing granulomas — suppurative and granulomatous inflammation
    together — that characterized this patient's lymph node and now his lung.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    The clinical timeline of this case is the natural history of CGD told in
    one patient. In BCG-vaccinating countries, suppurative regional
    lymphadenitis after neonatal BCG is a recognized herald: in a Chinese
    cohort of 169 BCG-exposed CGD patients, BCG complications were frequent
    and often the first manifestation (Zhou et al., J Clin Immunol 2018).
    Recurrent pneumonias with abscess formation — classically with{" "}
    <em>Serratia</em>, as here, or     <em>Burkholderia</em>, which can cause
    fulminant "Burkholderia cepacia syndrome" — hepatic and perianal
    abscesses, osteomyelitis, and granulomatous complications (colitis,
    obstructive bladder granulomas, GU tract disease) complete the syndrome.
    The registry of Winkelstein and colleagues documented pneumonia in over
    80% of patients and abscesses of lung, liver, and skin as the leading
    manifestations, with X-linked cases presenting earlier and more severely.
    The maternal uncle who died of "pneumonia" in childhood is the classic
    X-linked pedigree clue. Invasive aspergillosis remains the principal
    cause of mortality, which is why any new pulmonary infiltrate in CGD
    warrants aggressive evaluation, and why avoiding soil, mulch, and
    renovation dust (practical counseling for <em>Aspergillus</em> and{" "}
    <em>Nocardia</em> exposure) is part of routine care.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Diagnosis rests on demonstrating absent or reduced oxidative function
    per cell, and the dihydrorhodamine 123 flow cytometric assay is the
    modern standard (Vowells et al., J Immunol Methods 1995). PMA-stimulated
    normal neutrophils oxidize DHR to fluorescent rhodamine 123; X-linked
    CGD shows no shift, autosomal recessive forms often show residual
    activity, and X-linked carriers show the diagnostic bimodal histogram
    produced by random X-inactivation — one blood draw that diagnoses,
    stages, and determines inheritance. Genetic testing (CYBB first in this
    pedigree, then an AR panel) confirms the genotype for counseling,
    carrier detection, and eligibility for emerging gene therapy. Two
    practical microbiology points complete the diagnostic picture: first,{" "}
    <em>Serratia</em> is an AmpC beta-lactamase producer, so isolates
    resistant to amoxicillin-clavulanate and third-generation cephalosporins
    (as in this treatment failure) should be treated with cefepime, a
    carbapenem, or a fluoroquinolone; second, prodigiosin, the red pigment
    of <em>S. marcescens</em>, is temperature-regulated and often absent
    from clinical plates incubated at 37 °C, so a non-red isolate is still{" "}
    <em>Serratia</em>.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Management proceeds in layers. Acutely: obtain cultures from every
    accessible site of infection before starting antibiotics whenever
    feasible, choose beta-lactamase-stable therapy active against AmpC
    producers, and drain abscesses early — CGD abscesses respond poorly to
    antibiotics alone and source control is essential. Chronically: the prophylaxis backbone is
    TMP-SMX (Margolis et al., J Infect Dis 1990) plus itraconazole (Gallin
    et al., NEJM 2003), with consideration of subcutaneous IFN-gamma, which
    reduced serious infections by about 70% in the randomized trial of the
    International CGD Cooperative Study Group (NEJM 1991). Definitively:
    HLA-matched hematopoietic stem cell transplantation with
    reduced-intensity conditioning has excellent long-term survival even in
    patients with active or recent infection (Gungor et al., Lancet 2014),
    and lentiviral gene therapy for X-linked CGD has shown early clinical
    success (Kohn et al., Nat Med 2020). For this 19-year-old — two decades
    of accumulated infection and a preserved pulmonary reserve — referral
    for transplant evaluation, with sibling HLA typing, is the appropriate
    next step alongside lifelong prophylaxis.
  </p>
</div>
