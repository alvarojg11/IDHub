<h2 className="text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900 mb-6">
  Rationale
</h2>

<div className="mx-auto max-w-5xl">
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Louse-borne relapsing fever (LBRF) is caused by <em>Borrelia recurrentis</em> and is classically
    associated with crowding, forced migration, poverty, and poor access to hygiene that favor
    infestation with the human body louse (<em>Pediculus humanus corporis</em>). Unlike tick-borne
    relapsing fevers, there is no known animal reservoir; humans are the reservoir.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Transmission occurs when infected louse feces is inoculated into
    broken skin (often by scratching) or mucous membranes. Body lice usually live in clothing
    seams rather than remaining on the skin between feeds, which makes clothing decontamination
    and pediculosis treatment central to outbreak control.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    The relapse phenomenon is a hallmark of disease and reflects antigenic variation of borrelial
    outer membrane proteins. Patients typically have abrupt fever, rigors, headache, severe pain,
    prostration, and may also develop petechiae, epistaxis, jaundice, hepatosplenomegaly, and
    thrombocytopenia/coagulopathy. Untreated attacks may resolve temporarily, followed by afebrile
    intervals and recurrent episodes.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Diagnosis can often be made rapidly at the bedside by microscopy of thick and thin blood films
    obtained while the patient is febrile, because spirochaetaemia in LBRF can be high and more
    persistent than in other relapsing fevers. PCR is increasingly used for confirmation and
    species-level characterization, especially in imported cases, but treatment should not be delayed
    in a clinically compatible, smear-positive patient.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Prompt antibiotic therapy is life-saving, but clinicians must anticipate the Jarisch-Herxheimer
    reaction, which may occur within 1 to 2 hours after treatment and can be severe, with
    rigors, hyperpyrexia, hemodynamic instability, and cardiorespiratory deterioration. This is why
    treatment should be initiated with close observation and supportive care readiness, especially in
    severe disease. Doxycycline or tetracycline are commonly used treatments, with other options
    (such as penicillin-class agents or macrolides) considered in specific scenarios. Severe
    falciparum malaria and leptospirosis remain urgent differential diagnoses in appropriate
    epidemiologic settings and should be assessed in parallel when uncertainty remains.
  </p>
</div>

<h2 className="text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900 mb-6">
  Teaching points
</h2>

<div className="mx-auto max-w-5xl">
  <ul className="list-disc pl-6 text-lg leading-7 text-gray-800 space-y-2">
    <li>
      Recurrent febrile episodes with jaundice/bleeding clues and body louse exposure should raise suspicion for louse-borne relapsing fever.
    </li>
    <li>
      Thick and thin blood smear microscopy during febrile periods can provide rapid diagnosis.
    </li>
    <li>
      The relapse pattern is driven by antigenic variation of Borrelia outer membrane proteins.
    </li>
    <li>
      Antibiotic therapy can precipitate a severe Jarisch-Herxheimer reaction; monitor closely after treatment initiation.
    </li>
    <li>
      Louse control (pediculicide plus clothing decontamination/hygiene measures) is essential for public health control but does not replace antibiotic treatment.
    </li>
    <li>
      Severe falciparum malaria and leptospirosis are urgent differentials in travelers/migrants from endemic regions.
    </li>
  </ul>
