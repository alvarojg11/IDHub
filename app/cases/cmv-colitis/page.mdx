<h2 className="text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900 mb-6">
  Rationale
</h2>

<div className="mx-auto max-w-5xl">
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Cytomegalovirus is a beta-herpesvirus that establishes lifelong latency
    in myeloid progenitor cells and monocytes after primary infection. In
    immunocompetent hosts, CMV is controlled by robust CMV-specific CD4 and
    CD8 T-cell responses. In solid organ transplant recipients, the combination
    of pharmacologic immunosuppression, particularly calcineurin inhibitors
    and mycophenolate, and the absence of pre-existing CMV-specific cellular
    immunity in seronegative recipients creates a permissive environment for
    viral reactivation and end-organ disease. The D+/R- serostatus, present
    in this patient, carries the highest risk for CMV disease: the seropositive
    donor organ introduces virus (including latently infected donor leukocytes)
    into a recipient with no immunologic memory against CMV. Standard-of-care
    valganciclovir prophylaxis for six months reduces but does not eliminate
    this risk, and the post-prophylaxis period ("late CMV disease") has
    emerged as a clinically important window as prophylaxis duration has
    extended. Late CMV disease occurs in 15 to 35% of D+/R- solid organ
    transplant recipients despite six months of prophylaxis, driven by the
    failure to develop adequate CMV-specific T-cell immunity during the
    period of viral suppression.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    CMV colitis is the most common form of CMV GI end-organ disease in
    solid organ transplant recipients. The colon is particularly susceptible
    because of its high density of CMV-susceptible endothelial and stromal
    cells and its exposure to microbial factors that may trigger CMV
    reactivation. Clinically, CMV colitis presents as bloody diarrhea,
    abdominal cramping, weight loss, and fever, often mimicking other
    common diagnoses in transplant patients: C. difficile colitis, MMF-induced
    colitis, and de novo IBD. This case illustrates a particularly treacherous
    scenario: a preceding true C. difficile infection that was appropriately
    treated, but whose resolution unmasked a concurrent CMV infection that
    had been contributing to the GI syndrome from the outset. Co-infection
    with C. difficile and CMV is well-documented and occurs in approximately
    10 to 15% of CMV colitis cases in transplant series. The diagnostic
    principle is clear: a transplant patient with persistent or worsening
    GI disease after adequate C. difficile treatment requires endoscopy
    with biopsies for histopathology and IHC, regardless of the serum CMV
    viral load.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Ganciclovir resistance occurs in approximately 5 to 10% of transplant
    recipients treated for CMV disease and is substantially more common
    in D+/R- recipients after prolonged antiviral exposure. The molecular
    basis is well-characterized: the UL97 kinase mutation (most commonly
    at codons 460, 594, or 595) eliminates the initial phosphorylation step
    required for ganciclovir activation, rendering the drug inactive
    regardless of dose. UL54 mutations in the viral DNA polymerase confer
    broader cross-resistance. Clinically, ganciclovir resistance should be
    suspected whenever CMV PCR rises or fails to decline after two to three
    weeks of properly dosed IV therapy. Genotypic resistance testing by
    sequencing UL97 and UL54 from plasma is diagnostic and guides agent
    selection. Foscarnet, acting directly on the viral DNA polymerase without
    requiring UL97-mediated activation, is the primary treatment for
    UL97-mediated resistance. Maribavir, a novel oral UL97 inhibitor
    approved by the FDA in 2021, has demonstrated non-inferiority to
    investigator-initiated therapy (foscarnet, cidofovir, or high-dose
    ganciclovir) for refractory or resistant CMV in transplant recipients
    in the SOLSTICE trial, with a more favorable tolerability profile and
    the ability to be taken orally — a major practical advantage for
    outpatient management.
  </p>
</div>
