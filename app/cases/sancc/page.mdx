<CaseReveal pollId="case-sancc-q2">
<h2 className="text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900 mb-6">
  Rationale
</h2>

<div className="mx-auto max-w-5xl">
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Subarachnoid neurocysticercosis is caused by the larval form of <em>Taenia solium</em>
    in the subarachnoid spaces of the central nervous system. It is one of the most
    severe forms of neurocysticercosis because the parasite can proliferate in an
    aberrant racemose pattern, especially in the basal cisterns and Sylvian fissures,
    producing large multiloculated cystic lesions and marked local inflammation.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    The pathophysiology is driven not only by the cyst burden but also by the host
    inflammatory response. Inflammation around the cysts can cause chronic arachnoiditis,
    meningitis, communicating hydrocephalus, and cerebrovascular complications. Small
    perforating arteries are often affected by inflammatory endarteritis, which can lead
    to lacunar infarcts adjacent to cysts. Because of this, CT angiography may be normal
    even when clinically important vascular involvement is present.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Neuroimaging is central to diagnosis. CT is helpful for identifying calcified
    parenchymal lesions, while MRI is superior for detecting subarachnoid cysts,
    enhancement, and the full extent of inflammation. Serologic testing with enzyme-linked
    immunotransfer blot (immunoblot) is highly sensitive in patients with multiple lesions
    and is generally positive in subarachnoid disease because of the higher parasite burden.
    Circulating parasite antigen can help assess the presence of viable cysts and can be
    followed over time as a marker of treatment response. Because asymptomatic spinal
    involvement can coexist, spine MRI should also be considered.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Treatment differs from the approach used for isolated parenchymal neurocysticercosis.
    Patients with subarachnoid disease usually require antiparasitic therapy, often with
    albendazole plus praziquantel, together with corticosteroids to control treatment-related
    inflammation. Therapy is frequently prolonged and is often continued until radiographic
    resolution or clear inactivity on follow-up imaging. Serial neuroimaging, and when
    available antigen testing, help guide duration and assess for relapse or persistent
    viable disease.
  </p>
</div>

  Teaching points

<div className="mx-auto max-w-5xl">
  <ul className="list-disc pl-6 text-lg leading-7 text-gray-800 space-y-2">
    <li>
      Subarachnoid neurocysticercosis is a severe extraparenchymal form of
      <em> Taenia solium</em> infection.
    </li>
    <li>
      MRI is better than CT for defining subarachnoid cysts and inflammatory burden,
      while CT is useful for calcifications.
    </li>
    <li>
      Vascular involvement can produce lacunar infarcts even when CT angiography is normal.
    </li>
    <li>
      Serology and, when available, parasite antigen testing complement imaging and can
      support follow-up.
    </li>
    <li>
      Management usually requires corticosteroids plus prolonged antiparasitic therapy,
      not the short regimens used for uncomplicated parenchymal disease.
    </li>
  </ul>
