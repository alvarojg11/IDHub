<h2 className="text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900 mb-6">
  Rationale
</h2>

<div className="mx-auto max-w-5xl">
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Lymphatic filariasis caused by <em>Brugia malayi</em> is a mosquito-transmitted
    nematode infection endemic to South and Southeast Asia, including India, Malaysia,
    Indonesia, and the Philippines. It is transmitted primarily by <em>Mansonia</em>
    species mosquitoes and, to a lesser extent, <em>Anopheles</em> species. Unlike
    <em>Wuchereria bancrofti</em> — for which humans are the only natural reservoir —
    <em>B. malayi</em> has feline and primate reservoirs, complicating elimination efforts.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    The clinical syndrome described in this case illustrates several features that
    distinguish brugian from bancroftian filariasis. First, lymphedema in brugian
    filariasis is characteristically restricted to the distal extremities below the
    knee or elbow, in contrast to W. bancrofti, which can cause swelling of the entire
    limb, genitalia, and breast. Second, urogenital manifestations — hydrocele,
    epididymitis, chyluria — do not occur with Brugia infection. Third, the
    adenolymphangitis of brugian filariasis is distinctive: a single sterile abscess
    can form along the lymphatic tract, typically in the inguinal or axillary region,
    which can suppurate, spontaneously drain lymph, and heal with a characteristic scar.
    This abscess pattern is pathognomonic for brugian filariasis and was the key
    clinical clue in this case.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    The recurrent febrile episodes with leg swelling — repeatedly misdiagnosed as
    bacterial cellulitis — represent acute dermatolymphangioadenitis (ADLA). Unlike
    true filarial lymphangitis (AFL), which progresses distally along the lymphatic
    vessel from the lymph node outward (retrograde), ADLA develops in a reticular
    pattern and is caused by bacterial superinfection of small collecting lymphatics
    in areas of existing lymphatic dysfunction. ADLA can last 3 to 15 days and is
    frequently indistinguishable from bacterial cellulitis clinically. The absence
    of response to antibiotics and the recurrent, stereotyped nature of episodes
    should always raise suspicion for an underlying lymphatic cause.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Nocturnal periodicity of microfilaremia is a critical diagnostic concept.
    <em>B. malayi</em> microfilariae circulate in peripheral blood at highest density
    between 10 PM and 2 AM, sequestering in deep pulmonary vascular beds during
    the day. A daytime blood smear is therefore expected to be negative even in
    active infection. On Giemsa or hematoxylin-stained thick film, <em>B. malayi</em>
    microfilariae can be distinguished from <em>W. bancrofti</em> by the presence of
    nuclei extending to the tip of the tail — <em>W. bancrofti</em> microfilariae have
    a clear, nucleus-free tail tip.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    The Wolbachia endosymbiont is a defining feature of <em>W. bancrofti</em> and
    <em>Brugia</em> species. This obligate intracellular bacterium supports essential
    biochemical pathways for parasite embryogenesis, molting, and adult worm survival.
    An 8-week course of doxycycline (200 mg daily) depletes Wolbachia and achieves
    long-term sterilization and death of greater than 90% of adult worms —
    macrofilaricidal activity that DEC and ivermectin cannot reliably provide.
    Anti-Wolbachia therapy also reduces plasma VEGF-C and improves lymphatic pathology,
    making it the preferred approach for individual patient treatment.
  </p>
</div>
