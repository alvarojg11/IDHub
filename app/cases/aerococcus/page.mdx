<h2 className="text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900 mb-6">Rationale</h2>

<div className="mx-auto max-w-5xl">
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Aerococcus urinae is a Gram-positive, catalase-negative coccus that was first
    described in the 1990s as a causative organism of urinary tract infections
    (1). Although the organism is considered to be of low pathogenicity and may
    not always need to be treated, there are descriptions of severe, sometimes
    fatal infections, such as endocarditis, often in the presence of urinary
    tract infections or symptoms typical of this infection.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Aerococcus can be mistaken for viridans streptococci or enterococci because
    colonies may be alpha-hemolytic and catalase negative. Modern identification
    methods (e.g., MALDI-TOF MS or sequencing) have increased recognition of
    Aerococcus as a true pathogen, particularly in older patients with
    genitourinary disease.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    For penicillin-susceptible Aerococcus species, IV penicillin (or ampicillin)
    is preferred. Observational multicenter experience suggests ~2 weeks for
    uncomplicated bloodstream infection and ~4 weeks for endocarditis, with
    ceftriaxone as a commonly used once-daily alternative when susceptible.
    Aminoglycoside “synergy” is not universal and should not be automatic given
    toxicity risk.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Differentiation between A. urinae and Aerococcus viridans can be done by
    testing for pyrrolidinyl aminopeptidase (PYR) and leucine aminopeptidase
    (LAP), and confirmatory testing (e.g., 16S rRNA sequencing) can be used when
    needed (PMID: 20660216).
  </p>
</div>
