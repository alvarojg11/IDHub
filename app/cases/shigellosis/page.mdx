<h2 className="text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900 mb-6">
  Rationale
</h2>

<div className="mx-auto max-w-5xl">
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    This patient has the dysentery syndrome: fever, abdominal cramps, tenesmus, and
    repeated passage of small-volume stools containing blood and mucus. In endemic
    low-resource settings, that clinical pattern strongly supports shigellosis,
    even when stool culture is not available.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    <em>Shigella</em> species are invasive gram-negative bacilli that infect the distal
    ileum and colon, invade colonic epithelial cells, and trigger intense local
    inflammation. Mucosal ulceration, inflammatory exudate, and small-vessel injury
    produce the characteristic bloody-mucoid stool.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Humans are the only important reservoir, and spread is typically fecal-oral or
    direct person-to-person, especially where sanitation is poor or children are in
    close contact such as daycare settings. The infectious inoculum is very low, so
    clusters of disease are common.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Other causes of bloody diarrhea remain in the differential, but the combination
    of fever, marked tenesmus, inflammatory stool microscopy, and a high-risk exposure
    setting makes shigellosis the most likely diagnosis here.
  </p>
</div>

<h2 className="text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900 mb-6">
  Teaching points
</h2>

<div className="mx-auto max-w-5xl">
  <ul className="list-disc pl-6 text-lg leading-7 text-gray-800 space-y-2">
    <li>
      Shigellosis classically presents as dysentery with fever, tenesmus, abdominal
      cramps, and bloody-mucoid stools, but some patients may initially have watery diarrhea.
    </li>
    <li>
      A board-style clue for Shigella is the combination of frequent scant bloody stools
      plus prominent tenesmus, reflecting distal colonic inflammation.
    </li>
    <li>
      In many endemic low-resource settings, treatment is started on a presumptive
      clinical diagnosis because stool culture is unavailable or delayed.
    </li>
    <li>
      Antimicrobial therapy is most clearly beneficial in patients with dysentery
      or systemic symptoms and should be guided by local resistance patterns.
    </li>
    <li>
      Ciprofloxacin and azithromycin remain common outpatient options, but resistance
      to both is increasing.
    </li>
    <li>
      Complications to remember include seizures, reactive arthritis, and, with
      <em> S. dysenteriae</em> type 1, hemolytic uremic syndrome.
    </li>
  </ul>
