  </p>

<h2 className="text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900 mb-6">
  Rationale
</h2>

<div className="mx-auto max-w-5xl">
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Candida species are commensal organisms of the human gastrointestinal tract and skin that cause invasive disease when mucosal barriers and immune defenses are disrupted. Among the non-albicans Candida species, C. tropicalis has a particular propensity for tissue invasion and dissemination in neutropenic hosts, producing higher rates of deep-organ seeding compared to C. albicans. In the setting of prolonged neutropenia following chemotherapy for acute leukemia, translocation of Candida from the gut to the bloodstream occurs across damaged intestinal mucosa, and the absence of neutrophil-mediated fungal killing allows sustained candidemia with hematogenous dissemination to sanctuary sites including the central nervous system, eyes, and heart valves.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    The critical diagnostic insight in this case is the negative cryptococcal antigen. In immunocompromised patients with subacute lymphocytic meningitis, Cryptococcus neoformans is the reflex diagnosis, and appropriately so — it accounts for the vast majority of fungal meningitis in this population. However, the CrAg lateral flow assay has a sensitivity exceeding 99 percent for Cryptococcus, meaning that a negative result effectively excludes the diagnosis. Unlike Cryptococcus, Candida species do not produce a polysaccharide capsule, so both the India ink preparation and the CrAg are consistently negative. The serum (1,3)-beta-D-glucan provides the critical bridging data: it is a nonspecific marker of invasive fungal infection that is positive in Candida infections because beta-1,3-glucan is a major cell wall component. A markedly elevated BDG in the setting of negative CrAg and prior documented candidemia should immediately redirect the differential toward Candida CNS infection.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    The echinocandin sanctuary-site failure is one of the most important antifungal pharmacology concepts in clinical practice. Echinocandins inhibit beta-1,3-glucan synthase, an enzyme essential for fungal cell wall synthesis, and are first-line therapy for candidemia and invasive candidiasis. However, they are large, highly protein-bound molecules that do not cross the blood-brain barrier, achieving low or undetectable concentrations in the CSF. This means that a patient can have sterile blood cultures on an echinocandin while harboring active, untreated fungal infection in the CNS, vitreous humor, or on cardiac valves. When CNS candidiasis is diagnosed in a patient receiving an echinocandin, the drug must be changed to liposomal amphotericin B, which achieves detectable therapeutic CSF concentrations despite limited overall CNS distribution, and flucytosine, which penetrates the CSF at 70 to 80 percent of serum levels and provides synergistic fungicidal activity.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    The IDSA 2016 guidelines recommend liposomal amphotericin B at 3 to 5 mg/kg/day plus flucytosine at 25 mg/kg orally four times daily as initial therapy for CNS candidiasis, with transition to fluconazole 400 to 800 mg daily after clinical improvement and CSF sterilization. The minimum treatment duration is 4 to 6 weeks after documented CSF clearance, with longer courses for persistent microabscesses or slow clinical response. Flucytosine monitoring requires twice-weekly complete blood counts and liver function tests due to risks of bone marrow suppression and hepatotoxicity, a particular concern in patients with recent chemotherapy-induced cytopenias. All patients with documented candidemia should undergo dilated fundoscopic examination to evaluate for Candida endophthalmitis and echocardiography to evaluate for Candida endocarditis, as these sanctuary-site infections frequently coexist and affect treatment duration and approach.
  </p>
