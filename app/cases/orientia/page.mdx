<h2 className="text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900 mb-6">
  Rationale
</h2>

<div className="mx-auto max-w-5xl">
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Scrub typhus is caused by <em>Orientia tsutsugamushi</em>, an obligate intracellular
    gram-negative bacterium transmitted by the bite of larval trombiculid mites —
    commonly called chiggers — of the genus <em>Leptotrombidium</em>. It is endemic
    across the "tsutsugamushi triangle," a vast region spanning Japan and Korea in the
    north to Australia in the south and Pakistan in the west, including Nepal, India,
    Sri Lanka, Southeast Asia, and the western Pacific Islands. Scrub typhus is one
    of the most common causes of undifferentiated febrile illness in rural Asia,
    accounting for up to 20% of febrile hospitalizations in some hyperendemic regions.
    Travelers who venture through scrubby vegetation, forest undergrowth, riverbanks,
    and agricultural land are at risk.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    The eschar is the clinical hallmark of scrub typhus and the most important physical
    examination finding in any febrile patient with appropriate geographic exposure.
    It forms at the site of the chigger bite as a painless papule that ulcerates and
    develops a central black crust surrounded by an erythematous halo, typically
    measuring 5 to 20 mm. Because the bite is painless, patients are almost universally
    unaware of the lesion. It is found in skin folds and on concealed surfaces — the
    axillae, groin, perineum, behind the ears, the scalp, and between the toes —
    and will be missed without a deliberate and thorough full-body skin examination.
    Eschar detection rates range from 50% to over 90% depending on the geographic
    strain and examiner thoroughness.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    The clinical syndrome of fever, headache, maculopapular rash, hepatosplenomegaly,
    lymphadenopathy, thrombocytopenia, and transaminitis in a patient returning from
    endemic rural Asia has a broad differential that includes dengue, malaria,
    leptospirosis, murine typhus, and enteric fever. In the early febrile phase,
    dengue in particular can be clinically indistinguishable from scrub typhus.
    The eschar is the discriminating finding — no other common travel-related febrile
    illness produces it. In its absence, the diagnosis rests on the exposure history
    and laboratory pattern, with confirmation by serology (immunofluorescence assay)
    or PCR from eschar swabs or blood.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Doxycycline is the treatment of choice and should be started empirically when
    the diagnosis is suspected clinically. <em>O. tsutsugamushi</em> is an obligate
    intracellular organism that replicates free in the host cell cytoplasm — a
    property that renders it intrinsically resistant to beta-lactams (which target
    the outer cell wall) and poorly susceptible to fluoroquinolones. The rapid
    defervescence within 24 to 48 hours of initiating doxycycline is itself a
    diagnostically useful feature: failure to defervesce within this window should
    prompt reconsideration of the diagnosis or evaluation for complications.
    Azithromycin is an effective alternative in pregnancy and pediatric patients,
    and is preferred in geographic areas with documented doxycycline resistance.
  </p>
</div>
