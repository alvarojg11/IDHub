<h2 className="text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900 mb-6">
  Rationale
</h2>

<div className="mx-auto max-w-5xl">
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    <em>Klebsiella aerogenes</em>, formerly classified as <em>Enterobacter aerogenes</em>, was reclassified into the genus <em>Klebsiella</em> in 2019 based on whole-genome phylogenetic analysis. Despite the name change, its antimicrobial resistance profile and clinical behavior remain unchanged. It is a member of the Enterobacterales order that constitutively carries a chromosomal <code>ampC</code> gene encoding an inducible Ambler class C beta-lactamase. The gene is regulated by the <code>ampR</code> transcriptional repressor, which keeps basal expression low under non-inducing conditions. When certain beta-lactam drugs bind to penicillin-binding proteins in the cell wall, the resulting disruption of peptidoglycan recycling leads to accumulation of 1,6-anhydro-N-acetylmuramyl peptide precursors that displace AmpR from its repressor conformation, converting it into a transcriptional activator. AmpC expression then increases dramatically.
  </p>
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    The clinical problem arises because spontaneous mutations in the <code>ampR</code> regulatory region, the <code>ampC</code> promoter, or the <code>ampD</code> attenuator gene can produce stably derepressed mutants that constitutively overproduce AmpC regardless of inducing conditions. These mutants arise at frequencies of approximately 10 to the minus 6 to 10 to the minus 7 per cell division. In a high-inoculum infection such as bacteremic cholangitis, where the bacterial burden in the biliary tree can reach 10 to the 8 to 10 to the 10 colony-forming units, the absolute number of pre-existing derepressed mutants is substantial. When a beta-lactam that is both a strong inducer and susceptible to AmpC hydrolysis is used as therapy, these mutants are selected for, expand, and lead to clinical failure with a resistant isolate on repeat culture.
  </p>
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Third-generation cephalosporins such as ceftriaxone and ceftazidime are the most dangerous drugs in this setting because they are potent inducers of AmpC expression and are efficiently hydrolyzed by the enzyme. Piperacillin-tazobactam is a moderate inducer and is partially hydrolyzed. Cefepime, by contrast, is a weak inducer and penetrates the outer membrane porin channels more efficiently, and its zwitterionic structure makes it relatively stable to AmpC hydrolysis even when the enzyme is expressed at high levels. Carbapenems are stable to AmpC hydrolysis but represent broader spectrum therapy than necessary.
  </p>
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    The 2024 IDSA guidance on the treatment of antimicrobial-resistant Gram-negative infections addresses this scenario explicitly. Question 2.3 of the guidance states that for infections caused by moderate-risk AmpC organisms, including <em>Enterobacter cloacae</em> complex, <em>Klebsiella aerogenes</em>, and <em>Citrobacter freundii</em> complex, cefepime is suggested as a preferred treatment option. The guidance notes that cefepime is a weak inducer of AmpC and relatively stable to AmpC hydrolysis. Importantly, the 2024 update removed the prior caution against using cefepime for these organisms when the MIC was in the SDD range of 4 to 8 mcg/mL, after rereview of existing data showed no clear association between cefepime SDD MICs and clinical failure in this population.
  </p>
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    The susceptible-dose dependent category for cefepime is an exposure-dependent interpretation, not a resistance categorization. The CLSI breakpoint defines cefepime SDD as an MIC of 4 to 8 mcg/mL for Enterobacterales. The IDSA guidance specifies that cefepime SDD MICs are based on cefepime 2 g every 8 hours infused over 3 hours, which achieves sustained free drug concentrations above the MIC throughout the dosing interval. Standard intermittent dosing such as 1 g every 12 hours or 2 g every 12 hours may not achieve adequate time above the MIC when the MIC is 4 mcg/mL, which is why the extended-infusion high-dose regimen is mandatory in the SDD range.
  </p>
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    This case also illustrates why source control is inseparable from antimicrobial decision-making in cholangitis. ERCP with stent exchange and biliary drainage addressed the anatomic cause of infection, and the patient's hemodynamic improvement after drainage reflects the primacy of source control. However, the antibiotic choice matters for the remaining duration of therapy because the biliary tree remains colonized, and an inadequate beta-lactam can select for resistance during the treatment course even after source control.
  </p>
</div>
