<h2 className="text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900 mb-6">
  Rationale
</h2>

<div className="mx-auto max-w-5xl">
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Whipple's disease is a rare, chronic, systemic infection caused by
    <em> Tropheryma whipplei</em>, a gram-positive actinomycete first
    identified by George Hoyt Whipple in 1907 when he described a
    36-year-old physician with arthralgias, chronic diarrhea, and
    mesenteric lymphadenopathy. The organism remained uncultured for
    nearly a century until 2000, when Raoult and colleagues successfully
    propagated it in human embryonic lung fibroblast cell lines. Molecular
    characterization using 16S ribosomal RNA sequencing had previously
    established its phylogenetic position within the
    <em> Actinomycetia</em>, and the name <em>Tropheryma</em> reflects
    this lineage — from the Greek <em>trophe</em> (nourishment) and
    <em>eryma</em> (barrier), describing the tropism for nutrient-absorbing
    tissues. The species name <em>whipplei</em> honors the original
    observer.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Classic Whipple's disease presents as a triad of chronic diarrhea,
    weight loss, and migratory polyarthralgias, but the clinical spectrum
    has expanded substantially with the advent of molecular diagnostics.
    Isolated endocarditis caused by <em>T. whipplei</em> — without overt
    gastrointestinal manifestations — is increasingly recognized as a
    distinct clinical entity. In the largest published series, 28 cases of
    <em> T. whipplei</em> endocarditis diagnosed in Marseille, France were
    compared with cases reported in the literature: the disease occurred
    almost exclusively in men aged 40 to 80, of whom 75% had arthralgias
    and only a minority had gastrointestinal symptoms at presentation.
    The aortic valve was most commonly involved, and Duke criteria for
    infective endocarditis were frequently insensitive before surgical
    valve analysis, because blood cultures are invariably negative and
    the clinical presentation lacks the typical microbiologic anchors
    that satisfy the diagnostic criteria.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    The diagnostic approach to <em>T. whipplei</em> endocarditis reflects
    the organism's unique biology. <em>T. whipplei</em> cannot be cultured
    on routine microbiologic media — it is an obligate intracellular
    pathogen that requires living eukaryotic cells for replication. Blood
    cultures are universally negative, regardless of the number of sets
    drawn or the duration of incubation. The histopathologic hallmark is
    the presence of foamy macrophages distended with PAS-positive,
    diastase-resistant granular material on tissue biopsy — classically
    seen in the lamina propria of the small intestine, where the expanded
    villi filled with organism-laden macrophages produce the pale,
    yellow-white mucosal patches visible on endoscopy. The PAS positivity
    reflects the abundant glycoprotein-rich bacterial cell wall material
    within the macrophages, and diastase resistance distinguishes it from
    glycogen. Molecular confirmation is achieved by 16S ribosomal RNA
    PCR or <em>T. whipplei</em>-specific PCR performed on duodenal biopsy
    tissue, valve tissue, saliva, stool, or other clinical specimens.
    PCR from intestinal or valve tissue has the highest diagnostic yield.
    Serum anti-<em>T. whipplei</em> antibodies are neither sensitive nor
    specific enough for diagnostic use, as seroprevalence is high in
    the general population and does not correlate with active disease.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Treatment of Whipple's disease has evolved considerably. Early regimens
    relied on prolonged courses of trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole after IV
    induction with ceftriaxone or penicillin G, based on the observation
    that the organism was susceptible to sulfonamides in vitro. However,
    TMP-SMX resistance, treatment failures, and CNS relapses prompted
    the development of alternative strategies. The doxycycline-hydroxychloroquine
    combination, pioneered by the Marseille group, exploits a specific
    mechanistic vulnerability of the organism.
    <em> T. whipplei</em> survives within macrophage phagolysosomes, which
    maintain an acidic pH that impairs the activity of doxycycline.
    Hydroxychloroquine, a lysosomotropic weak base, accumulates within
    acidic intracellular compartments and raises the phagolysosomal pH
    toward neutrality, restoring the antibacterial activity of doxycycline.
    This pH-dependent synergy has been validated in both in vitro and
    clinical studies. The recommended regimen consists of IV ceftriaxone
    (or meropenem) for two weeks as induction, followed by oral
    doxycycline 100 mg twice daily plus hydroxychloroquine 200 mg three
    times daily for at least 12 months. Treatment should be prolonged to
    18 to 24 months in patients with CNS involvement or extensive disease.
    Without treatment, classic Whipple's disease is universally fatal.
  </p>
</div>
