<h2 className="text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900 mb-6">
  Rationale
</h2>

<div className="mx-auto max-w-5xl">
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    <em>Haemophilus influenzae</em> is a small, pleomorphic gram-negative
    coccobacillus that colonizes the human upper respiratory tract. Encapsulated
    strains are classified into serotypes a through f based on their capsular
    polysaccharides; type b (Hib) was historically the dominant cause of invasive
    disease, including meningitis, epiglottitis, pneumonia, and septic arthritis
    in children under five. The introduction of conjugate Hib vaccines in the late
    1980s produced one of the most dramatic reductions in vaccine-preventable
    disease ever recorded, reducing Hib invasive disease incidence by more than
    99% in vaccinated populations. In adults, invasive Hib disease now occurs
    almost exclusively in those without protective immunity: the unvaccinated,
    those with waning immunity, and individuals with functional or anatomic asplenia,
    hypogammaglobulinemia, complement deficiency, or alcohol use disorder.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    The Gram stain is the critical early diagnostic clue in bacterial meningitis
    and guides initial treatment narrowing before culture results are available.
    <em>H. influenzae</em> has a characteristic pleomorphic appearance: organisms
    range from tiny coccoid forms to short rods, often with a mix of shapes in
    the same field. This contrasts with the uniform diplococci of <em>Neisseria
    meningitidis</em>, the lancet-shaped diplococci of <em>Streptococcus
    pneumoniae</em>, and the larger, more uniform rods of Enterobacteriaceae.
    Recognizing this morphology narrows the differential significantly and supports
    early targeted therapy.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Antibiotic management requires attention to beta-lactamase status before
    de-escalation. Approximately 30 to 40% of <em>H. influenzae</em> clinical
    isolates in the United States produce beta-lactamase, conferring ampicillin
    resistance. Ceftriaxone is stable to this mechanism and remains active against
    all beta-lactamase-positive isolates, making it the definitive agent of choice
    for Hib meningitis at standard meningitis dosing (2 g IV every 12 hours, or
    every 24 hours in some protocols). De-escalation to ampicillin is appropriate
    only when beta-lactamase testing confirms a negative result and the isolate is
    susceptible. Adjunctive dexamethasone, started before or with the first
    antibiotic dose, should be continued for four days: the evidence base for
    corticosteroid reduction of neurologic sequelae (particularly hearing loss) is
    strongest for Hib meningitis and represents a standard of care in this infection.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Rifampin chemoprophylaxis for close contacts is a critical and frequently
    overlooked component of Hib meningitis management. <em>H. influenzae</em>
    type b spreads via respiratory droplets; household and close contacts of index
    cases are at increased risk for secondary invasive disease, particularly
    unvaccinated children and immunocompromised adults. Rifampin 20 mg/kg/day
    (maximum 600 mg) once daily for four days eradicates nasopharyngeal Hib
    carriage and is the recommended prophylaxis regimen. All close contacts
    should have their Hib vaccination status reviewed. Rifampin
    prophylaxis is not required for all contact scenarios: current guidelines
    recommend individualized assessment based on the vaccination status and age
    of household contacts.
  </p>
</div>

      disorder, asplenia, hypogammaglobulinemia, or complement deficiency.
      On Gram stain, look for small, pleomorphic gram-negative coccobacilli
      ranging from coccoid to short rod forms.
    </li>
    <li>
      Ceftriaxone is the treatment of choice for Hib meningitis. De-escalation
      to ampicillin is appropriate only after beta-lactamase testing confirms a
      negative result: approximately 30 to 40% of <em>H. influenzae</em> isolates
      in the United States are beta-lactamase positive and ampicillin-resistant.
    </li>
    <li>
      Adjunctive dexamethasone, given before or with the first antibiotic dose
      and continued for four days, reduces neurologic sequelae (especially
      hearing loss) in Hib meningitis. The evidence for corticosteroid benefit
      is strongest for this organism.
    </li>
    <li>
      Rifampin 20 mg/kg/day (maximum 600 mg) once daily for 4 days is the
      recommended chemoprophylaxis for close contacts of invasive Hib disease.
       Confusing Hib prophylaxis (rifampin) with meningococcal prophylaxis (ciprofloxacin or rifampin) is
      a common error.
    </li>
    <li>
      All contacts should have Hib vaccination status reviewed. Unvaccinated or
      incompletely vaccinated household members should receive catch-up vaccination
      in addition to rifampin prophylaxis.
    </li>
