<h2 className="text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900 mb-6">
  Rationale
</h2>

<div className="mx-auto max-w-5xl">
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Francisella tularensis is a tiny, gram-negative, aerobic coccobacillus
    that is among the most infectious bacteria known, with an infectious
    dose as low as 10 organisms by the aerosol route for Type A strains
    (F. tularensis subsp. tularensis). It is a CDC Category A bioterrorism
    agent, reflecting its potential for weaponization via aerosolization to
    cause pneumonic tularemia, its extreme infectivity, and the severity of
    disease it produces. In nature, tularemia is a zoonosis with a broad
    host range, most prominently affecting lagomorphs (rabbits, hares) and
    rodents in North America. Human infection occurs via tick bite (the most
    common route in the United States), direct contact with infected animal
    tissue, ingestion of contaminated water or food, or — in the oculoglandular
    form — direct conjunctival inoculation.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Six clinical syndromes are recognized: ulceroglandular (the most common,
    a skin ulcer at the inoculation site plus regional lymphadenopathy),
    glandular (lymphadenopathy without a skin ulcer), oculoglandular (1 to 2%
    of cases), oropharyngeal (ingestion), typhoidal (systemic bacteremia
    without localizing findings), and pneumonic (the most severe, from
    inhalation). Oculoglandular tularemia produces the classic Parinaud's
    oculoglandular syndrome: unilateral follicular or papillary conjunctivitis
    with tender ipsilateral preauricular and submandibular lymphadenopathy.
    The conjunctivitis can progress to nodular ulceration of the palpebral
    conjunctiva and, as in this case, deep corneal involvement with ulceration
    and hypopyon, representing a sight-threatening emergency requiring urgent
    ophthalmologic co-management.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Diagnosis requires a high clinical index of suspicion because standard
    Gram stain and routine bacterial cultures are unreliable: Francisella
    stains poorly and requires cysteine-supplemented media (chocolate agar
    or buffered charcoal yeast extract) with extended incubation under
    biosafety level 2 or 3 precautions. Serologic confirmation by
    microagglutination (fourfold rise in titer between acute and convalescent
    samples, or a single titer of 1:128 or greater) is the most common
    diagnostic method. PCR of tissue or wound specimens is increasingly
    available and provides rapid confirmation. Treatment should not await
    microbiologic confirmation when the clinical and epidemiologic picture
    is compelling. Aminoglycosides (gentamicin IV or streptomycin IM) are
    the standard of care for severe disease, with relapse rates below 1%
    compared to 5 to 15% for doxycycline or fluoroquinolones, reflecting
    their superior bactericidal intracellular activity against this
    obligate intracellular pathogen.
  </p>
</div>
