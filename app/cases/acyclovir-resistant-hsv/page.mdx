<h2 className="text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900 mb-6">
  Rationale
</h2>

<div className="mx-auto max-w-5xl">
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Acyclovir-resistant HSV is uncommon in immunocompetent hosts but becomes clinically important in patients with impaired T-cell immunity, especially hematopoietic stem cell transplant recipients, patients with advanced HIV, and those receiving prolonged antiviral exposure. In transplant recipients, resistant HSV often presents as persistent, progressive, or atypical mucocutaneous disease rather than as a brief crop of vesicles. Lesions may become chronic ulcers, hypertrophic plaques, or deep erosions involving oral, genital, perianal, or cutaneous sites.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Acyclovir is a nucleoside analog that depends on HSV thymidine kinase for the first phosphorylation step. After further phosphorylation by host kinases, acyclovir triphosphate inhibits viral DNA polymerase and causes chain termination. The most common resistance mechanism is mutation in the HSV thymidine kinase gene, <em>UL23</em>, producing absent, reduced, or substrate-altered kinase activity. DNA polymerase mutations in <em>UL30</em> are less common but can produce resistance to acyclovir and broader cross-resistance patterns.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Diagnostic confirmation requires more than a positive HSV PCR. PCR establishes that HSV DNA is present, but it does not determine whether the isolate is susceptible to acyclovir. When lesions progress after 7 to 10 days of appropriately dosed therapy, clinicians should obtain a fresh lesion specimen for HSV culture and phenotypic susceptibility testing if available. Genotypic sequencing for <em>UL23</em> and <em>UL30</em> mutations can be useful, particularly in reference laboratories, but interpretation depends on mutation databases and local assay availability.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Treatment of suspected or confirmed acyclovir-resistant HSV generally requires foscarnet, which directly inhibits viral DNA polymerase and does not require activation by HSV thymidine kinase. This pharmacologic bypass explains why foscarnet retains activity against many thymidine kinase-deficient HSV isolates. Cidofovir is another thymidine kinase-independent option but is limited by nephrotoxicity and the need for probenecid and hydration. Because valacyclovir and famciclovir also rely on viral thymidine kinase activation, they are usually ineffective against thymidine kinase-deficient HSV.
  </p>
</div>
