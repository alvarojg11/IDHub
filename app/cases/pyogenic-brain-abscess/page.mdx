<h2 className="text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900 mb-6">
  Rationale
</h2>

<div className="mx-auto max-w-5xl">
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Parkinson's disease causes progressive oropharyngeal dysphagia in up to 80% of patients,
    driven by impaired pharyngeal coordination, reduced laryngeal elevation, and a blunted
    cough reflex. Chronic microaspiration of saliva and food debris delivers a heavy inoculum
    of oral flora into the lower respiratory tract. When aspiration pneumonia is incompletely
    treated, it can evolve into a frank lung abscess. From there, bacteremia seeds the brain
    hematogenously. The aspiration-to-lung abscess-to-brain pathway is the mechanism in this
    case: the right lower lobe cavitary lesion with an air-fluid level is the proximate source,
    itself a consequence of the undertreated aspiration event six weeks prior.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    The anatomical basis of this pathway explains why pulmonary sources are uniquely efficient
    at seeding the brain. Bacteria originating within an infected lung parenchyma enter the
    pulmonary venous drainage directly: pulmonary veins empty into the left atrium, bypassing
    the pulmonary capillary filter entirely. From the left atrium, bacteria travel to the left
    ventricle, aorta, and cerebral circulation without interception. This contrasts with bacteremia
    arising from extrapulmonary sources (for example, an intra-abdominal abscess), where bacteria
    entering the systemic venous circulation must traverse the pulmonary capillary bed before
    reaching the left heart, giving the lung a chance to filter the inoculum. Pulmonary AVMs in
    hereditary hemorrhagic telangiectasia exploit the same anatomical shortcut and are a classic
    cause of recurrent brain abscess in otherwise healthy patients.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    The microbiology of aspiration-source brain abscess is polymicrobial in up to 60% of cases.
    Streptococcus anginosus group (formerly called the Streptococcus milleri group, comprising
    S. anginosus, S. constellatus, and S. intermedius) is the most commonly isolated organism.
    These streptococci are uniquely abscess-prone: they produce hyaluronidase and other enzymes
    that liquefy tissue and promote cavity formation. Oral anaerobes, particularly Prevotella
    melaninogenica and Fusobacterium nucleatum, are frequent co-isolates reflecting the aspiration
    source. The aspirated culture from this patient grew Streptococcus anginosus and Prevotella
    melaninogenica, a classic pairing.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Empiric antibiotic selection must cover both the streptococcal and anaerobic components.
    Ceftriaxone at 2 g intravenously every 12 hours (CNS dosing) achieves adequate CSF and
    brain parenchyma penetration against Streptococcus anginosus. Metronidazole at 500 mg
    intravenously every 8 hours penetrates the blood-brain barrier reliably and reaches
    bactericidal concentrations within abscess cavities: its CNS penetration approaches 100%
    of serum levels, making it one of the most reliable agents for anaerobic CNS infection.
    Vancomycin is reserved for MRSA risk (post-neurosurgical, nosocomial acquisition, prior
    MRSA colonization), none of which apply here. Once culture and susceptibility data are
    available, therapy is narrowed accordingly.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Total antibiotic duration is typically 6 to 8 weeks following surgical aspiration. Serial
    MRI is performed every 2 to 4 weeks to assess radiographic response: a reduction in lesion
    diameter of 50% or more by week 4 predicts a favorable outcome. Failure to achieve
    radiographic improvement by 4 to 6 weeks should prompt repeat aspiration or open excision.
    Addressing the pulmonary source is equally important: the lung abscess requires its own
    antibiotic course, and swallowing re-evaluation with speech therapy and aspiration precautions
    are essential to prevent recurrence in this Parkinson's patient.
  </p>
</div>

  Teaching points

<div className="mx-auto max-w-5xl">
  <ul className="list-disc pl-6 text-lg leading-7 text-gray-800 space-y-2">
    <li>
      Parkinson's disease dysphagia creates a hematogenous brain abscess pathway: aspiration
      of oral flora seeds the lung, lung abscess causes bacteremia, and bacteremia seeds the brain.
    </li>
    <li>
      Pulmonary sources are uniquely efficient at seeding the brain: bacteria from a lung abscess
      enter pulmonary venous drainage directly into the left atrium, bypassing the pulmonary
      capillary filter. Extrapulmonary sources must traverse the pulmonary capillaries first.
      Pulmonary AVMs (hereditary hemorrhagic telangiectasia) exploit the same shortcut and are
      a classic cause of recurrent brain abscess in young, otherwise healthy patients.
    </li>
    <li>
      Restricted diffusion on DWI (bright signal, dark ADC) within a ring-enhancing cavity is
      pathognomonic for pyogenic abscess and is the key imaging feature distinguishing it from
      toxoplasmosis, tuberculoma, and neurocysticercosis.
    </li>
    <li>
      Streptococcus anginosus group is the most common organism in pyogenic brain abscess; its
      abscess-forming tropism is driven by hyaluronidase production. Oral anaerobes are frequent
      polymicrobial co-isolates when the source is aspiration.
    </li>
    <li>
      Empiric therapy: ceftriaxone (2 g IV every 12 hours) plus metronidazole (500 mg IV every
      8 hours). Metronidazole achieves near-complete CNS penetration and is the cornerstone of
      anaerobic coverage. Do not omit it.
    </li>
    <li>
      Stereotactic aspiration is preferred over open excision for most pyogenic abscesses:
      it provides microbiologic diagnosis and relieves mass effect with lower morbidity.
      Lumbar puncture is contraindicated with significant mass effect and midline shift.
    </li>
    <li>
      Treatment duration is 6 to 8 weeks; monitor with serial MRI.
      Address the pulmonary source and aspiration risk to prevent recurrence.
    </li>
  </ul>
