<div className="mx-auto max-w-5xl">
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    A 57-year-old woman with no known immunocompromising condition presents with a
    15-day history of progressive pruritus, burning, and paresthesia around the right
    eye and forehead. She initially used artificial tears after a first evaluation for
    presumed nonspecific ocular irritation, but symptoms persisted.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Three days later, she developed erythema and swelling involving the right upper
    eyelid and nasal tip, along with nasal congestion. She was diagnosed with
    blepharitis and started on topical tobramycin. Within 24 hours, the erythema
    progressed across the right forehead and periorbital region, and the diagnosis was
    revised to periorbital cellulitis. Oral clindamycin was started, but the lesions
    continued to evolve.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    At follow-up one week later, examination showed grouped crusted and necrotic lesions
    involving the right frontal scalp/forehead and a prominent necrotic-crusted lesion
    on the nasal tip with surrounding erythematous halo.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Vital signs are stable, and she is afebrile. There is right periorbital erythema and
    edema without proptosis. Extraocular movements are intact without pain, and visual
    acuity is preserved on bedside assessment. Ophthalmology evaluation is requested to
    assess for corneal or intraocular involvement.
  </p>
</div>

<h2 className="text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900 mb-6">
  Rationale
</h2>

<div className="mx-auto max-w-5xl">
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Herpes zoster ophthalmicus (HZO) is caused by reactivation of latent varicella-zoster
    virus in the ophthalmic division (V1) of the trigeminal nerve. The combination of
    unilateral dermatomal facial lesions and neuropathic symptoms (burning, paresthesia,
    dysesthesia) should prompt consideration of zoster even when early vesicles are absent
    or atypical.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Hutchinson&apos;s sign refers to vesicular or crusted lesions on the nasal tip and reflects
    nasociliary nerve involvement. This is an important bedside clue because it is strongly
    associated with increased risk of ocular involvement (e.g., keratitis, uveitis, and
    other vision-threatening complications). Patients with suspected HZO should receive
    prompt ophthalmologic assessment even if initial bedside visual acuity appears normal.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Early systemic antiviral therapy is ideally started within 72 hours of rash onset to
    reduce complications, but treatment should not be withheld when presentation is delayed
    if HZO is clinically suspected, especially when lesions are evolving or ocular risk is
    high. Oral valaciclovir is commonly favored in outpatient care because of better
    bioavailability and simpler dosing compared with oral aciclovir.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    PCR from lesion material can confirm varicella-zoster virus and is helpful when the
    diagnosis is delayed or lesions are atypical. However, HZO remains a clinical diagnosis
    in many cases, and therapy should be guided by the urgency of preventing ocular sequelae
    rather than waiting for confirmatory testing.
  </p>
</div>

<h2 className="text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900 mb-6">
  Teaching points
</h2>

<div className="mx-auto max-w-5xl">
  <ul className="list-disc pl-6 text-lg leading-7 text-gray-800 space-y-2">
    <li>
      Unilateral dermatomal facial pain/pruritus with evolving rash should raise suspicion for HZO, even before classic vesicles are obvious.
    </li>
    <li>
      Hutchinson&apos;s sign (nasal tip lesions) is an important predictor of ocular involvement and should prompt urgent eye evaluation.
    </li>
    <li>
      Do not delay systemic antivirals while awaiting PCR if HZO is clinically suspected.
    </li>
    <li>
      HZO can be misdiagnosed as blepharitis or periorbital cellulitis early in the course, especially with atypical prodromal symptoms.
    </li>
  </ul>
</div>
