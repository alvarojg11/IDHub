<h2 className="text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900 mb-6">
  Rationale
</h2>

<div className="mx-auto max-w-5xl">
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Checkpoint inhibitor pneumonitis (CIP) is an immune-related adverse event of
    the anti-PD-1, anti-PD-L1, and anti-CTLA-4 antibodies, in which release of
    inhibitory signaling on T cells produces inflammation directed at normal
    lung parenchyma. It is one of the most clinically important toxicities of
    the class because it can be fatal, because its presentation — subacute
    dyspnea, dry cough, low-grade fever, and pulmonary infiltrates — is
    indistinguishable from infection, and because its correct treatment,
    immunosuppression, is the therapeutic opposite of what untreated infection
    demands. CIP occurs in roughly three to five percent of patients on anti-PD-1
    monotherapy and at higher rates with combination regimens. The median time
    to onset is about two to three months after starting therapy, but the range
    is wide, and cases appear after the first dose, many months in, and even
    weeks after the drug has been discontinued. Pembrolizumab is
    immune-activating rather than immunosuppressive, which is the central
    paradox of this case: the host is not neutropenic or otherwise vulnerable,
    yet the lungs are inflamed by the same mechanism that is fighting the tumor.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    The clinical and radiographic spectrum is broad. Patients most often
    describe progressive dyspnea and a dry cough, with or without fever, and
    examination reveals hypoxemia and crackles out of proportion to a
    relatively unimpressive systemic picture. Chest CT is the imaging modality
    of choice and typically shows ground-glass opacities, often with fine
    reticulation; the Naidoo classification describes five overlapping patterns
    — organizing pneumonia, nonspecific interstitial pneumonia (NSIP)-like,
    hypersensitivity-like, acute interstitial pneumonia/ARDS, and a
    not-otherwise-specified group — none of which is specific enough on its own
    to confirm the diagnosis. In a head and neck cancer patient the differential
    is deliberately wide: typical and atypical community-acquired pneumonia,
    aspiration related to dysphagia and prior chemoradiation, radiation
    pneumonitis within the radiation field and within the first year after
    treatment, pulmonary embolism, lymphangitic spread of malignancy, and
    opportunistic infection if any concomitant steroids or cytopenias are
    present. The bilateral, lower-lobe-predominant ground-glass pattern with a
    low procalcitonin and negative microbiology narrows the field to a
    drug-induced immune process.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    CIP is a diagnosis of exclusion. The diagnostic standard is a compatible
    exposure and syndrome, together with a reasonable effort to exclude
    infection. In a hospitalized, hypoxemic patient, that effort includes
    bronchoscopy with bronchoalveolar lavage, which provides bacterial,
    mycobacterial, and fungal cultures, a respiratory viral panel, cytology to
    exclude malignant involvement, and a cell differential. The lavage in CIP
    typically shows a lymphocytic predominance and no pathogen; an eosinophilic
    predominance should redirect toward drug-induced eosinophilic pneumonia or
    parasitic disease such as Strongyloides. A low procalcitonin is a supportive
    hint against typical bacterial infection but is never diagnostic on its own.
    The reason the workup matters so much is that the definitive therapy for
    CIP — systemic corticosteroids — is directly harmful if the true diagnosis
    is an untreated infection, particularly Pneumocystis, a respiratory virus,
    or an atypical bacterial pathogen. Establishing the diagnosis before
    immunosuppressing is the single most important cognitive checkpoint in the
    case.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Management is governed by CTCAE severity grading. Grade 1 pneumonitis
    (asymptomatic, radiographic only) is managed by holding the checkpoint
    inhibitor and close surveillance with repeat imaging. Grade 2 (symptomatic,
    limiting instrumental activities of daily living) mandates holding the drug
    and starting oral corticosteroids at roughly 1 mg/kg/day. Grade 3 (severe,
    limiting self-care, with hypoxemia requiring supplemental oxygen, as in this
    patient) mandates hospitalization and intravenous corticosteroids at 1 to 2
    mg/kg/day of methylprednisolone equivalent. Grade 4 (life-threatening
    respiratory compromise) requires intensive care, intravenous
    methylprednisolone, and generally permanent discontinuation of the drug
    class. Most patients improve within 48 to 72 hours; failure to improve
    defines refractory CIP and prompts addition of a second-line agent —
    infliximab 5 mg/kg, mycophenolate mofetil, or less commonly
    cyclophosphamide, tocilizumab, or intravenous immunoglobulin. Corticosteroids
    are tapered slowly over at least six weeks because rapid tapers carry a high
    rate of pneumonitis recurrence. Re-challenge with the offending agent or
    another checkpoint inhibitor may be considered for Grade 1 to 2 events that
    resolve completely, but is generally avoided after Grade 3 to 4 pneumonitis.
    The broader principle — and the reason the case belongs in an infectious
    diseases curriculum — is that an immune-activating drug produces a syndrome
    that perfectly mimics infection, and the correct response is not broader
    antibiotics but a disciplined diagnostic workup followed by
    immunosuppression.
  </p>
</div>
