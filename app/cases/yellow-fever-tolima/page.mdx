<h2 className="text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900 mb-6">
  Rationale
</h2>

<div className="mx-auto max-w-5xl">
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Yellow fever is a mosquito-borne flavivirus infection that can begin like many other tropical febrile illnesses. Early symptoms are nonspecific: fever, chills, headache, back pain, myalgias, nausea, and fatigue. The diagnostic clue in this case is the combination of recent travel to rural Tolima, no yellow fever vaccination, daytime mosquito exposure, leukopenia, thrombocytopenia, and early hepatic injury with jaundice. Severe yellow fever is famous for hemorrhage, renal failure, shock, and marked jaundice, but waiting for those features misses the opportunity to recognize the disease early.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    The differential diagnosis includes dengue, malaria, leptospirosis, acute viral hepatitis, chikungunya, Zika, rickettsioses, and enteric fever. Dengue is especially tempting because it shares Aedes transmission, fever, cytopenias, and transaminitis. The early bilirubin rise and AST-predominant hepatitis after travel to a yellow fever risk area should push yellow fever higher on the list, particularly in an unvaccinated traveler.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Testing depends on timing. In the first few days of illness, serum RT-PCR can detect yellow fever virus RNA. Later, IgM antibody testing becomes more useful, but flavivirus serology can cross-react after dengue, Zika, Japanese encephalitis, West Nile virus infection, or vaccination. Plaque reduction neutralization testing may be needed to confirm the diagnosis. Suspected cases should be coordinated with public health authorities, both for diagnostic access and for surveillance.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Treatment is supportive. Patients with suspected yellow fever and laboratory evidence of hepatic involvement should be monitored closely for hypoglycemia, worsening hepatitis, coagulopathy, bleeding, renal injury, encephalopathy, and shock. Aspirin and NSAIDs should be avoided because of bleeding risk. Prevention remains the key intervention: yellow fever vaccination is highly effective for appropriate travelers and residents of at-risk areas.
  </p>
</div>

<h2 className="text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900 mb-6">
  Pathogen Notes
</h2>

<div className="mx-auto max-w-5xl">
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Yellow fever virus is an enveloped, positive-sense, single-stranded RNA virus in the genus <em>Flavivirus</em>, the same genus that includes dengue, Zika, West Nile, and Japanese encephalitis viruses. That family relationship explains why the clinical syndromes can overlap and why antibody tests can cross-react. The virus is not spread person to person by routine contact; transmission requires an infected mosquito vector.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Transmission occurs in three overlapping cycles. In the sylvatic, or jungle, cycle, mosquitoes transmit virus between nonhuman primates and can infect humans who enter forested or rural areas. This is the major concern for many travelers in endemic parts of South America. In the intermediate, or savannah, cycle, mosquitoes transmit virus between monkeys and humans in areas where people live or work near forest edges. In the urban cycle, <em>Aedes aegypti</em> transmits virus from viremic humans to other humans and can drive explosive outbreaks in cities with low vaccination coverage.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    The reservoir and amplifying hosts depend on the cycle. Nonhuman primates are the main reservoir/amplifying hosts in sylvatic transmission, while humans become the key amplifying host in urban transmission. In South America, sylvatic vectors include <em>Haemagogus</em> and <em>Sabethes</em> mosquitoes; <em>Aedes aegypti</em> is the classic urban vector and is a useful visual clue because it also transmits dengue, Zika, and chikungunya.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    The vaccine is a live-attenuated 17D yellow fever vaccine and is one of the most effective vaccines used in travel medicine. A single dose provides long-lasting protection for most immunocompetent people and is recommended for many travelers to areas with yellow fever risk. It is also required for entry into some countries, depending on itinerary. Because it is live, it is contraindicated or requires careful risk-benefit review in selected patients, including some immunocompromised people, infants younger than 6 months, and people with severe allergy to vaccine components.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Two vaccine safety points matter clinically. First, yellow fever vaccine-associated neurologic disease is rare but can occur, especially in very young infants and older adults. Second, yellow fever vaccine-associated viscerotropic disease is very rare but severe and resembles wild-type yellow fever with multiorgan involvement. These risks are why clinicians should match vaccine decisions to the actual itinerary, outbreak risk, immune status, age, and contraindications rather than treating yellow fever vaccine as a routine add-on for every traveler.
  </p>
</div>
