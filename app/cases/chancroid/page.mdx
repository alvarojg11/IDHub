<h2 className="text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900 mb-6">
  Rationale
</h2>

<div className="mx-auto max-w-5xl">
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Chancroid is a painful, ulcerative sexually transmitted infection caused by{" "}
    <em>Haemophilus ducreyi</em>, a small gram-negative coccobacillus with a characteristic
    "school of fish" arrangement on Gram stain. The organism requires specific growth factors
    (hemin, glutamine, cystine) making culture technically demanding and insensitive; nucleic
    acid amplification tests (NAAT) are more sensitive but not widely available in routine
    clinical settings. In practice, diagnosis is often clinical.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    The classic presentation is a painful, non-indurated genital ulcer with irregular,
    undermined edges, a grayish-yellow purulent base that bleeds easily on contact, and
    regional suppurative lymphadenopathy progressing to bubo formation in approximately
    30% of patients. Pain is a key differentiating feature: it sharply contrasts with
    the painless, clean, indurated ulcer of primary syphilis and the vesicular-then-shallow
    erosive pattern of genital herpes. The term "soft chancre" has been used historically
    to distinguish the non-indurated ulcer of chancroid from the indurated "hard chancre"
    of syphilis.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Chancroid was the leading cause of genital ulceration in sub-Saharan Africa and parts
    of Asia through the 1980s. Its prevalence declined dramatically after widespread
    fluoroquinolone use for syndromic STI management and improved STI control during the
    HIV era; herpes simplex virus now causes the majority of genital ulcers worldwide.
    Chancroid remains endemic in parts of sub-Saharan Africa, South and Southeast Asia,
    and Latin America, and should still be considered in travelers or migrants from these
    regions. Importantly, <em>H. ducreyi</em> has more recently been identified as a cause
    of non-venereal chronic cutaneous leg ulcers in children in Papua New Guinea, the South
    Pacific islands, and Ghana — a new epidemiologic presentation distinct from the classical
    STI.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    The interaction between chancroid and HIV is clinically significant. <em>H. ducreyi</em>{" "}
    ulcers recruit and activate CD4+ T lymphocytes and macrophages — precisely the cells
    HIV targets — markedly increasing both the risk of HIV acquisition (through portal-of-entry
    disruption) and HIV transmission (through elevated viral shedding in genital secretions).
    HIV testing is therefore mandatory in any patient with chancroid.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Regarding treatment, <em>H. ducreyi</em> acquires resistance plasmids readily. Resistance
    to tetracyclines, ampicillin, sulfonamides, trimethoprim, kanamycin, and chloramphenicol
    is described. Recommended regimens from the 2021 CDC STI Treatment Guidelines include
    azithromycin 1 g orally as a single dose or ceftriaxone 250 mg IM as a single dose.
    Ciprofloxacin 500 mg orally twice daily for 3 days is an effective alternative but
    is contraindicated in pregnancy. Fluctuant buboes should be incised and drained or
    needle-aspirated to prevent spontaneous rupture and sinus tract formation. Patients
    co-infected with HIV may have slower healing and higher failure rates; close clinical
    follow-up is essential.
  </p>
</div>

<h2 className="text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900 mb-6">
  Teaching points
</h2>

<div className="mx-auto max-w-5xl">
  <ul className="list-disc pl-6 text-lg leading-7 text-gray-800 space-y-2">
    <li>
      Chancroid presents as a painful, non-indurated ("soft") genital ulcer with undermined
      edges, a purulent base, and suppurative inguinal lymphadenopathy — pain is the key
      feature distinguishing it from the painless, indurated chancre of primary syphilis.
    </li>
    <li>
      Approximately 30% of patients develop inguinal bubo formation; fluctuant buboes
      should be drained to prevent spontaneous rupture.
    </li>
    <li>
      Diagnosis is primarily clinical; culture is insensitive due to fastidious growth
      requirements, and NAAT is not widely available. The "school of fish" arrangement
      on Gram stain is a classic but inconsistently present clue.
    </li>
    <li>
      Azithromycin 1 g orally as a single dose and ceftriaxone 250 mg IM as a single
      dose are the preferred first-line regimens (2021 CDC STI Guidelines).
    </li>
    <li>
      <em>H. ducreyi</em> acquires resistance plasmids readily — sulfonamides,
      trimethoprim, tetracyclines, and ampicillin are no longer reliable options.
    </li>
    <li>
      Chancroid markedly amplifies HIV transmission in both directions; always test for
      HIV in any patient with a genital ulcer due to chancroid.
    </li>
    <li>
      <em>H. ducreyi</em> is now recognized as a cause of non-venereal chronic cutaneous
      ulcers in children in Papua New Guinea, South Pacific islands, and Ghana — a
      distinct and emerging epidemiologic presentation.
    </li>
  </ul>
