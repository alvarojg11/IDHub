<h2 className="text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900 mb-6">
  Rationale
</h2>

<div className="mx-auto max-w-5xl">
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Human African trypanosomiasis (HAT, sleeping sickness) is caused by
    the protozoan parasite <em>Trypanosoma brucei</em>, transmitted through
    the bite of infected tsetse flies (<em>Glossina</em> species) in
    sub-Saharan Africa. Two subspecies cause human disease:
    <em>T. brucei gambiense</em> (West and Central Africa, including the
    DRC, which bears over 70% of the global burden) and <em>T. brucei
    rhodesiense</em> (East Africa). T.b. gambiense causes a chronic,
    insidious disease unfolding over months to years, while T.b. rhodesiense
    causes a more acute, rapidly fatal illness over weeks. The DRC declared
    HAT a public health emergency in the 1990s, when an estimated 300,000
    people were infected annually; intensive control efforts have reduced
    this to fewer than 1,000 reported cases per year, but the disease
    remains a risk for aid workers and travelers with rural exposure.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    The disease proceeds in two stages. Stage 1 (hemolymphatic) begins
    weeks after the tsetse bite: the inoculation site may develop a
    trypanosomal chancre, a self-resolving painful or pruritic nodule
    representing a local inflammatory response to the parasite. Systemic
    invasion produces intermittent fever, headache, fatigue, weight loss,
    and lymphadenopathy. The posterior cervical lymphadenopathy
    (Winterbottom's sign) is a classic and relatively specific finding
    for T.b. gambiense. Markedly elevated polyclonal IgM is a
    characteristic laboratory finding, reflecting the parasite's continuous
    antigenic variation. Stage 2 (meningoencephalitic) occurs when
    trypanosomes cross the blood-brain barrier, producing neuropsychiatric
    symptoms including personality change, behavioral disturbance,
    hallucinations, and the pathognomonic disruption of the circadian
    rhythm: daytime somnolence with nocturnal insomnia, the origin of the
    disease's common name.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Staging by lumbar puncture is mandatory before treatment. Stage 2 is
    defined by CSF WBC greater than 5 cells per mcL or the presence of
    trypanosomes in the CSF regardless of cell count. This distinction
    is not academic: stage 1 drugs (pentamidine for T.b. gambiense,
    suramin for T.b. rhodesiense) do not penetrate the CNS and will fail
    to treat established brain infection. Fexinidazole, approved in 2018,
    is now the WHO-recommended first-line for both stages of T.b. gambiense,
    provided the patient can swallow. Its oral formulation represents a
    transformative advance for a disease endemic in settings with limited
    IV capacity. NECT (eflornithine plus nifurtimox) remains available
    for severe or refractory stage 2 cases. Melarsoprol, the historic
    stage 2 drug, is no longer recommended for T.b. gambiense given its
    5 to 10% rate of fatal reactive encephalopathy and documented
    resistance, though it remains the only option for stage 2
    T.b. rhodesiense.
  </p>
</div>

    <li>
      HAT progresses through two stages: hemolymphatic (stage 1) and
      meningoencephalitic (stage 2). The pathognomonic feature of stage 2
      is sleep cycle inversion (daytime somnolence, nighttime insomnia).
      Posterior cervical lymphadenopathy (Winterbottom's sign) and a
      history of a self-resolving skin nodule at the bite site
      (trypanosomal chancre) are classic clues.
    </li>
    <li>
      Lumbar puncture is mandatory before treatment. Stage 2 is defined
      by CSF WBC greater than 5 cells per mcL or trypanosomes in the CSF.
      Treating stage 2 with a stage 1 drug (pentamidine) is lethal: it
      clears blood parasitemia but leaves CNS disease untreated.
    </li>
    <li>
      Fexinidazole (oral, 10-day course) is the WHO-recommended first-line
      for both stage 1 and stage 2 T.b. gambiense in patients able to
      swallow. It replaced IV-dependent NECT as the preferred regimen
      after the 2018 AMBITION trial. NECT is reserved for severe stage 2
      or fexinidazole failure.
    </li>
    <li>
      Melarsoprol is no longer first-line for T.b. gambiense due to a
      5 to 10% rate of fatal reactive encephalopathy and widespread
      resistance. It remains the only treatment for stage 2
      T.b. rhodesiense (East African sleeping sickness).
    </li>
    <li>
      Know your geography: T.b. gambiense (West and Central Africa,
      chronic course) versus T.b. rhodesiense (East Africa, acute course).
