<h2 className="text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900 mb-6">
  Rationale
</h2>

<div className="mx-auto max-w-5xl">
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Bubonic plague is the most likely diagnosis because the syndrome localizes to a regional lymph node draining a likely flea bite. The abrupt fever, severe constitutional symptoms, lower-extremity papule, and intensely tender ipsilateral inguinal bubo are the clinical pattern. The Sierra Nevada camping exposure matters because plague persists in enzootic cycles among wild rodents and fleas in parts of the western United States, including California.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    <em>Yersinia pestis</em> is a gram-negative coccobacillus in the order Enterobacterales. It is maintained in nature through rodent-flea cycles. Ground squirrels, prairie dogs, chipmunks, wood rats, and other wild rodents can participate depending on the region, while humans are incidental hosts. Infection usually follows the bite of an infected flea or direct handling of infected animals. After inoculation, organisms drain to regional lymph nodes, evade innate immune responses, and produce necrotizing hemorrhagic lymphadenitis: the bubo.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Diagnosis requires both microbiology and communication. The laboratory should be notified before handling specimens when plague is suspected. Bubo aspirate is the highest-yield specimen in bubonic disease, and blood cultures should be obtained because bacteremia may occur. Stains may show small gram-negative coccobacilli with bipolar staining on Wayson, Giemsa, or Wright stain, but culture and public health laboratory testing such as PCR or direct fluorescent antibody testing are used for confirmation. Serology is more useful later and should not be the only acute diagnostic test.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Treatment should begin immediately when plague is suspected. First-line therapy includes fluoroquinolones such as ciprofloxacin or levofloxacin and aminoglycosides such as gentamicin; streptomycin is the historical standard but is often difficult to obtain. Doxycycline is an alternative in selected patients and is also used for post-exposure prophylaxis. Bubonic plague itself is not typically transmitted person to person, but pneumonic plague can spread by respiratory droplets and requires droplet precautions and urgent prophylaxis of close contacts.
  </p>
</div>
