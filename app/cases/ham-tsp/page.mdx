    shows a mild lymphocytic pleocytosis with modestly elevated protein. Neurology asks

<h2 className="text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900 mb-6">
  Rationale
</h2>

<div className="mx-auto max-w-5xl">
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    HTLV-1-associated myelopathy/tropical spastic paraparesis (HAM/TSP) is a chronic
    inflammatory myelopathy caused by HTLV-1 infection. The classic presentation is an
    insidious, nonremitting spastic paraparesis with lower-extremity weakness, urinary
    dysfunction, and mild sensory complaints. This patient fits that pattern closely:
    she has slowly progressive bilateral leg spasticity and weakness, hyperreflexia with
    Babinski signs, reduced vibration sense, urinary incontinence, and CSF findings
    compatible with an inflammatory myelopathy.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    The diagnosis is often missed because the syndrome overlaps with other chronic
    myelopathies such as cervical spondylotic myelopathy or primary progressive multiple
    sclerosis. In this case, the absence of a compressive spinal lesion, the symmetric
    lower-extremity-predominant course, and the epidemiologic clue of origin from Guyana,
    where HTLV-1 seroprevalence is higher than in the general U.S. population, should push
    HAM/TSP higher on the differential. The lumbar puncture also helps exclude competing
    diagnoses while supporting an inflammatory process. A comprehensive workup for chronic
    infectious myelopathy can reasonably include HIV, syphilis serologies, and HTLV-1
    testing from serum and CSF. Positive HTLV-1 testing then strongly supports the diagnosis.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Important risk factors for developing HAM/TSP among HTLV-1 carriers include longer
    duration of infection and higher proviral load. Female sex and older age at onset are
    also associated with disease in epidemiologic cohorts. Some patients have rapid
    progression, while others decline slowly over many years; cerebrospinal fluid markers
    such as CXCL10 and neopterin may help stratify disease activity when available.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    There is no proven curative therapy. Management is therefore focused on maintaining
    mobility and quality of life through physical therapy, occupational therapy, assistive
    devices, and treatment of bladder dysfunction, pain, and spasticity. For progressive
    inflammatory disease, expert reviews support corticosteroid-based strategies such as
    pulsed methylprednisolone and, in selected patients, low-dose oral prednisolone
    maintenance. Antiretroviral therapy has not shown consistent benefit, and newer
    immunomodulatory options such as mogamulizumab remain promising but are not yet routine
    standard care.
  </p>
</div>

<h2 className="text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900 mb-6">
  Teaching points
</h2>

<div className="mx-auto max-w-5xl">
  <ul className="list-disc pl-6 text-lg leading-7 text-gray-800 space-y-2">
    <li>
      HAM/TSP should be considered in chronic, nonremitting spastic paraparesis with early
      bladder symptoms, especially in patients from HTLV-1 endemic regions such as the
      Caribbean, parts of South America, southwestern Japan, and West Africa.
    </li>
    <li>
      MRI may be unrevealing or show nonspecific changes; the absence of a structural lesion
      is an important clue when the examination strongly suggests myelopathy.
    </li>
    <li>
      Higher HTLV-1 proviral load is associated with increased risk of HAM/TSP, and disease
      course can range from very slow progression to rapidly disabling disease.
    </li>
    <li>
      The differential diagnosis includes compressive myelopathy, multiple sclerosis, vitamin
      B12 deficiency, and hereditary spastic paraplegia.
    </li>
    <li>
      No curative therapy exists. Rehabilitation and symptom-directed care are foundational,
      while corticosteroids may be considered for progressive inflammatory disease.
    </li>
    <li>
      Patients diagnosed with HTLV-1 infection should also be assessed for other associated
      conditions and counseled about transmission risk.
    </li>
  </ul>
