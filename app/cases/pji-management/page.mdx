<h2 className="text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900 mb-6">
  Rationale
</h2>

<div className="mx-auto max-w-5xl">
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Prosthetic joint infection (PJI) is classified by timing and mechanism of acquisition,
    which directly determines surgical strategy. Early PJI occurs within 3 months of
    implantation and is typically caused by organisms introduced at the time of surgery.
    Delayed PJI occurs between 3 and 12 months and often reflects low-virulence organisms
    such as coagulase-negative staphylococci or Cutibacterium acnes with an indolent
    course. Late PJI, as in this case, occurs more than 12 months after implantation
    and is usually the result of hematogenous seeding from a distant bacteremic source —
    here, the dental extraction.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    The critical surgical decision in acute PJI is whether DAIR — debridement,
    antibiotics, and implant retention — is appropriate. IDSA guidelines support
    DAIR when all of the following criteria are met: (1) symptom duration less than
    3 weeks; (2) a well-fixed, stable implant without radiographic loosening; (3) no
    sinus tract; (4) acceptable overlying soft tissue for closure; and (5) an organism
    susceptible to agents available for oral step-down therapy. This patient meets all
    criteria. DAIR must be performed as an open procedure — arthroscopic debridement
    is inadequate — and must include exchange of all modular components, particularly
    the polyethylene tibial insert, which harbors biofilm and cannot be adequately
    decontaminated in situ.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Viridans group streptococci — including <em>S. mitis</em>, <em>S. sanguinis</em>,
    and <em>S. oralis</em> — are normal oral flora that can cause transient bacteremia
    during dental procedures, particularly extractions. They are recognized but
    uncommon causes of hematogenous PJI. The association with dental work makes
    the history of a recent extraction highly relevant and underscores why the American
    Heart Association recommends pre-procedural antibiotic prophylaxis for patients
    with prosthetic cardiac valves — though prophylaxis is not currently recommended
    for prosthetic joint recipients before dental work in most guidelines.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    For streptococcal PJI, beta-lactams are highly effective and rifampin plays no
    role. This is a critical distinction from staphylococcal PJI, where rifampin is
    the cornerstone of therapy due to its unique activity against biofilm-embedded,
    metabolically quiescent staphylococci. Rifampin does not have an established
    benefit for streptococcal biofilm in PJI, and adding it would expose the patient
    to drug interactions (rifampin is a potent CYP3A4 inducer) without therapeutic
    gain. For penicillin-susceptible streptococci, high-dose penicillin G or
    ceftriaxone is the preferred IV agent; amoxicillin is the oral equivalent with
    approximately 90% bioavailability.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    The OVIVA trial (Li et al., NEJM 2019) was a landmark randomized controlled trial
    enrolling 1,054 patients with bone and joint infections — including PJI — who
    were randomized at approximately day 7 to continue IV antibiotics or transition
    to oral therapy. Oral antibiotics were non-inferior to IV for treatment failure
    at 1 year (14.6% vs 13.2%), with significantly fewer IV line complications in
    the oral group. OVIVA enrolled a heterogeneous population including patients
    with streptococcal, staphylococcal, and gram-negative PJI, provided an oral
    agent with good activity was available. For streptococcal PJI, high-dose
    amoxicillin satisfies this criterion. The implication is that prolonged IV therapy
    in stable patients with susceptible organisms offers no advantage over early
    oral step-down and exposes patients to unnecessary line-related risks.
  </p>
</div>

  Teaching points

<div className="mx-auto max-w-5xl">

    <li>
      Acute hematogenous PJI (symptom onset &lt;3 weeks, stable implant, no sinus
      tract, susceptible organism) is managed with DAIR — open debridement with
      exchange of all modular components — not prosthesis removal.
    </li>
    <li>
      DAIR must be performed as an open procedure; arthroscopic washout is
      inadequate for PJI and associated with higher failure rates.
    </li>
    <li>
      Biofilm is the central pathophysiologic challenge in PJI: it protects organisms
      from host immunity and most antibiotics by maintaining them in a metabolically
      quiescent state.
    </li>
    <li>
      For streptococcal PJI, rifampin is not indicated — its role in PJI is
      organism-specific, targeting biofilm-embedded staphylococci. Applying rifampin
      to streptococcal infections is a common conceptual error.
    </li>
    <li>
      Penicillin-susceptible viridans streptococcal PJI is treated with a
      beta-lactam (penicillin G or ceftriaxone IV; amoxicillin orally) — no
      combination partner is required.
    </li>
    <li>
      The OVIVA trial demonstrated that early transition to oral antibiotics is
      non-inferior to continued IV therapy for bone and joint infections including
      PJI, provided an oral agent with adequate activity is available.
    </li>
    <li>
      High-dose oral amoxicillin (~90% bioavailability) is appropriate step-down
      therapy for penicillin-susceptible streptococcal PJI following an initial
      IV phase and clinical stabilization.
    </li>
    <li>
      Total antibiotic duration per IDSA guidelines: 3 months for total knee PJI,
      6 months for total hip PJI treated with DAIR.
    </li>
