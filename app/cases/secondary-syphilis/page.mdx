<h2 className="text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900 mb-6">
  Rationale
</h2>

<div className="mx-auto max-w-5xl">
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Syphilis is caused by <em>Treponema pallidum</em> subspecies
    <em>pallidum</em>, a thin, motile spirochete that cannot be cultured on
    artificial media. Transmission occurs through direct contact with an
    infectious lesion during sexual activity. After an incubation period of
    nine to 90 days (average three weeks), primary syphilis presents as a
    painless, indurated genital ulcer (chancre) at the site of inoculation,
    accompanied by regional lymphadenopathy. The chancre heals spontaneously
    in two to six weeks, often without the patient seeking care, as occurred
    in this case.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Secondary syphilis develops four to ten weeks after the primary chancre as
    spirochetes disseminate hematogenously. The hallmark is a diffuse
    maculopapular rash that classically involves the palms and soles, a
    feature present in fewer than five percent of other dermatologic
    conditions. The lesions are typically non-pruritic, copper-colored, and
    may be subtle in patients with darker skin tones. Other manifestations
    include condylomata lata (moist, flat, warty perianal or genital lesions),
    mucous patches in the oropharynx, patchy alopecia ("moth-eaten" alopecia),
    and constitutional symptoms including fever, malaise, pharyngitis, and
    generalized lymphadenopathy. Secondary syphilis is the stage of highest
    infectivity. If untreated, symptoms resolve spontaneously and the patient
    enters the latent phase.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Syphilis is called "the great imitator" because
    its manifestations across all stages can mimic a wide variety of
    conditions, including viral exanthems, drug reactions, psoriasis,
    pityriasis rosea, lichen planus, and Rocky Mountain spotted fever. A high
    index of suspicion and a low threshold for serologic testing are essential,
    particularly in populations at risk. The palmar and plantar rash is the
    most distinctive feature of secondary syphilis and should prompt immediate
    serologic evaluation.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Serologic diagnosis relies on the combination of a non-treponemal test
    (RPR or VDRL) with a treponemal-specific confirmatory test (FTA-ABS,
    TP-PA). Non-treponemal titers correlate with disease activity and are the
    standard tool for monitoring treatment response. A fourfold decline in
    titer (two dilutions, e.g., from 1:32 to 1:8) at six to twelve months
    after treatment indicates successful therapy. Failure to achieve this
    decline warrants repeat evaluation for re-infection or treatment failure
    and consideration of lumbar puncture. All patients diagnosed with syphilis
    should be tested for HIV; co-infection is common and may alter serologic
    responses and disease progression.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    The Jarisch-Herxheimer reaction occurs in up to 50 percent of patients
    with secondary syphilis within the first 24 hours of penicillin therapy,
    caused by the sudden release of T. pallidum lipoproteins and the
    subsequent cytokine surge. It manifests as fever, chills, myalgias,
    headache, and transient worsening of the rash. It is self-limited and
    managed with antipyretics. Importantly, it is not an allergic reaction to
    penicillin and should not prompt discontinuation of therapy. In pregnant
    patients, the Jarisch-Herxheimer reaction can precipitate preterm labor
    and requires close monitoring.
  </p>
</div>
