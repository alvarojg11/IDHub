<h2 className="text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900 mb-6">
  Rationale
</h2>

<div className="mx-auto max-w-5xl">
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Tuberculous meningitis (TBM) in the setting of advanced HIV co-infection is one of
    the most complex and clinically demanding scenarios in infectious diseases. It requires
    simultaneous mastery of two treatment paradigms — anti-TB therapy and ART — and a
    nuanced understanding of how their interaction, particularly through immune
    reconstitution inflammatory syndrome (IRIS), can be fatal.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    <strong>Anti-TB therapy and adjunctive dexamethasone.</strong> Standard treatment for
    TBM follows the same four-drug RIPE backbone (isoniazid, rifampin, pyrazinamide,
    ethambutol) used for pulmonary TB, with an intensive phase of two months followed by
    a continuation phase of seven to ten months. The critical addition in CNS disease is
    adjunctive dexamethasone. The landmark Thwaites et al. trial demonstrated that
    dexamethasone reduces mortality and severe disability in TBM, and this benefit
    extends to HIV-infected patients. Dexamethasone is typically given at 0.4 mg/kg/day
    for the first week, then tapered over approximately six weeks.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    <strong>ART timing: the CNS exception.</strong> The timing of ART initiation in
    HIV-TB co-infection is one of the most evidence-driven and counterintuitive decisions
    in ID. For pulmonary TB and most extrapulmonary forms, three pivotal trials — ACTG
    5221 (STRIDE), SAPiT, and CAMELIA — established that early ART at approximately two
    weeks significantly reduces mortality, particularly at very low CD4 counts. However,
    this paradigm does not apply to TB meningitis. The Torok et al. randomized trial from
    Vietnam demonstrated that immediate ART in TBM did not reduce mortality and was
    associated with a higher rate of severe adverse events, likely driven by paradoxical
    CNS IRIS. This finding was reinforced by a subgroup analysis of ACTG 5349. The
    current OI guidelines from the Department of Health and Human Services (DHHS)
    recommend deferring ART for 4 to 8 weeks in TBM. The CNS compartment is uniquely
    vulnerable to IRIS: the blood-brain barrier concentrates inflammatory responses,
    paradoxical worsening can manifest as cerebral edema, herniation, or new tuberculomas,
    and mortality from CNS IRIS is substantially higher than from systemic IRIS.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    <strong>Drug interactions: rifampin and ART.</strong> Rifampin is a potent inducer of
    cytochrome P450 enzymes (primarily CYP3A4) and uridine diphosphate
    glucuronosyltransferases (UGT1A1), which substantially reduces plasma levels of most
    ART agents. Boosted protease inhibitors are essentially contraindicated. Among
    integrase inhibitors, bictegravir levels are severely reduced and dose adjustment is
    not feasible; raltegravir requires dose doubling to 800 mg twice daily and is now
    less preferred. Dolutegravir 50 mg twice daily effectively overcomes rifampin
    induction and is the preferred INSTI, supported by pharmacokinetic studies and the
    INSPIRING trial. Efavirenz-based therapy does not require dose adjustment with
    rifampin but carries a higher pill burden, CNS side effects, and inferior
    tolerability compared to dolutegravir. Doravirine is another NNRTI that is
    contraindicated with rifampin.
  </p>
</div>

<h2 className="text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900 mb-6">
  Teaching points
</h2>

<div className="mx-auto max-w-5xl">
  <ul className="list-disc pl-6 text-lg leading-7 text-gray-800 space-y-2">
    <li>
      TB meningitis is treated with standard four-drug RIPE therapy; adjunctive
      dexamethasone reduces mortality and severe disability and should be initiated
      concurrently regardless of HIV status.
    </li>
    <li>
      Unlike pulmonary TB (where early ART at ~2 weeks reduces mortality in advanced
      HIV), TB meningitis warrants deferral of ART for 4 to 8 weeks due to the risk
      of fatal paradoxical CNS IRIS.
    </li>
    <li>
      This ART deferral recommendation is supported by the Torok et al. trial and ACTG
      5349 subanalysis, and is reflected in DHHS OI guidelines — a critical exception
      to the general principle of "treat HIV early."
    </li>
    <li>
      Paradoxical CNS IRIS can manifest as cerebral edema, new tuberculomas, cranial
      nerve palsies, or herniation; it carries substantially higher mortality than
      systemic IRIS.
    </li>
    <li>
      Rifampin is a potent CYP3A4/UGT1A1 inducer. Boosted protease inhibitors and
      bictegravir are contraindicated. Dolutegravir 50 mg twice daily is the preferred
      integrase inhibitor with rifampin-based TB therapy.
    </li>
    <li>
      CSF GeneXpert MTB/RIF is the fastest diagnostic tool for TB meningitis and
      simultaneously detects rifampin resistance, guiding early therapeutic decisions.
    </li>
    <li>
      Oral candidiasis, very low CD4 (&lt;50), and constitutional symptoms in an
      undiagnosed patient from an endemic region should prompt immediate HIV and TB
      co-evaluation.
    </li>
  </ul>
</div>
