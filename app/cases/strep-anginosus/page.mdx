<h2 className="text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900 mb-6">
  Rationale
</h2>

<div className="mx-auto max-w-5xl">
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    The <em>Streptococcus anginosus</em> group (SAG), comprising <em>S. anginosus</em>,
    <em> S. intermedius</em>, and <em>S. constellatus</em>, occupies a distinct
    ecological and clinical niche among the streptococci. Unlike other viridans
    streptococci, which are primarily associated with endocarditis and bacteremia,
    SAG is uniquely pyogenic: its members produce hyaluronidase and other virulence
    factors that promote tissue invasion and suppurative collections at virtually
    any anatomic site. Pyogenic liver abscess, brain abscess, lung abscess,
    peritonsillar and retropharyngeal abscess, and intraabdominal collections are
    all classic SAG presentations. The clinician who isolates a viridans streptococcus
    from a blood culture must recognize when the organism is SAG rather than
    a non-pyogenic viridans species, as this distinction fundamentally changes
    the clinical urgency.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    The laboratory signature of SAG is highly specific: pinpoint colonies (less than
    0.5 mm at 24 hours) with variable hemolysis (alpha, beta, or gamma depending
    on species and strain) and a distinctive butterscotch or caramel odor produced
    by diacetyl. This odor is detectable when the agar plate is opened and is
    virtually pathognomonic among streptococci. Variable Lancefield grouping (A, C,
    F, G, or non-groupable) can complicate automated identification, but the
    characteristic morphology and odor combined with the clinical context of
    an abscess should reliably direct the diagnosis.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    SAG bacteremia with a hepatic abscess carries an important non-infectious implication:
    a strong and well-established association with colorectal neoplasia. The GI tract,
    particularly the colon, is the presumed portal of entry, bacterial translocation
    through a disrupted colonic mucosa (as seen with adenoma, carcinoma, or inflammatory
    bowel disease) seeds the portal circulation and leads to hepatic seeding. Multiple
    case series report colorectal adenoma or carcinoma in 20 to 30% of patients with
    SAG bacteremia. Colonoscopy is recommended in all patients with SAG bacteremia,
    regardless of age or prior screening history.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Treatment requires both antibiotic therapy and source control. SAG is uniformly
    susceptible to penicillin; beta-lactams are the agents of choice and should be
    de-escalated to penicillin G or ampicillin once susceptibility is confirmed.
    Percutaneous catheter drainage under imaging guidance is the standard approach
    for accessible abscesses greater than 3 to 4 cm; surgical drainage is reserved
    for inaccessible collections or drainage failures. Duration of therapy is
    typically 4 to 6 weeks total, with transition to oral amoxicillin after an
    initial IV course of 2 to 4 weeks, guided by clinical and imaging response.
  </p>
</div>
