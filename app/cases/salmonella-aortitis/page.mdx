<h2 className="text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900 mb-6">
  Rationale
</h2>

<div className="mx-auto max-w-5xl">
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Salmonella aortitis and infected aortic aneurysm represent the most
    feared complication of non-typhoidal Salmonella bacteremia. Non-typhoidal
    Salmonella serovars, most commonly Typhimurium and Enteritidis, are
    responsible for the majority of bacterial aortitis cases in Western
    case series, accounting for 30 to 40% of reported cases. The classic
    epidemiologic profile is an older man (median age 60 to 70 years)
    with pre-existing aortic disease (aneurysm, atherosclerosis, or prior
    aortic surgery) who experiences a gastroenteritis episode two to six
    weeks prior to the vascular presentation. The GI illness is often mild
    or self-limited and may not be volunteered without direct questioning.
    Poultry and eggs remain the most common vehicles of non-typhoidal
    Salmonella transmission in the United States.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    The pathophysiology is rooted in Salmonella's identity as a facultative
    intracellular pathogen. During bacteremia, Salmonella invades and
    survives within macrophages using the Salmonella pathogenicity island
    2 (SPI-2) type III secretion system, which remodels the
    Salmonella-containing vacuole to evade lysosomal killing. Atherosclerotic
    plaques and aneurysmal aortic walls are densely infiltrated with
    macrophages (foam cells), activated endothelium, and intraluminal
    thrombus, providing a uniquely permissive niche for intracellular
    Salmonella replication. This intracellular sanctuary explains both
    the organism's predilection for diseased vascular tissue and the
    failure of antibiotic monotherapy: systemic drug concentrations do not
    reliably eradicate bacteria sequestered within macrophages embedded in
    infected aortic tissue.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    The diagnosis should be suspected in any patient with known aortic
    disease who presents with fever and back or abdominal pain, even without
    a recalled GI illness. CT angiography is the imaging modality of choice:
    findings include periaortic fat stranding, soft tissue thickening around
    the aorta, rapid aneurysm enlargement compared to prior imaging, and
    periaortic or intramural gas. Periaortic gas is nearly pathognomonic of
    bacterial aortitis and demands urgent surgical evaluation. Blood cultures
    are positive in the majority of cases and should be drawn before
    antibiotics. Serologic testing for Salmonella is unreliable in this
    setting.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Management is bimodal: surgical source control and prolonged antibiotic
    therapy. Surgery involves debridement of all grossly infected aortic
    tissue and vascular reconstruction. Options include in-situ repair
    (rifampicin-soaked prosthetic graft or autologous femoral vein) or
    extra-anatomic bypass. Endovascular aneurysm repair (EVAR) may serve
    as a bridge to open repair in unstable patients but is not a definitive
    solution given the persistent infected tissue. Antibiotic therapy should
    be directed by susceptibilities: fluoroquinolones (ciprofloxacin) and
    third-generation cephalosporins (ceftriaxone) are the preferred agents,
    with good tissue penetration and reliable activity against susceptible
    strains. Duration is at minimum 6 weeks, and many centers recommend
    indefinite oral suppression given the relapse risk after discontinuation,
    particularly in patients who received endovascular rather than open
    repair.
  </p>
</div>

      Non-typhoidal Salmonella is the leading cause of bacterial aortitis.
      The classic triad: older patient with aortic aneurysm or atherosclerosis,
      a preceding gastroenteritis episode (often undercooked poultry, 2 to 6
      weeks prior), and gram-negative bacteremia with back pain and fever.
    </li>
    <li>
      Salmonella's vascular predilection is driven by its biology as a
      facultative intracellular pathogen: it survives within macrophages
      enriched in atherosclerotic plaques and aneurysmal aortic walls,
      creating an intracellular reservoir that antibiotics cannot reliably
      reach.
    </li>
    <li>
      Periaortic gas on CT is nearly pathognomonic of an infected aortic
      aneurysm and mandates urgent surgical evaluation. Do not mistake
      back pain in a patient with a known aneurysm for a musculoskeletal
      complaint without first ruling out vascular infection.
    </li>
    <li>
      Antibiotic monotherapy carries mortality exceeding 80%. Management
      requires urgent surgical debridement with vascular reconstruction
      plus prolonged antibiotics (ciprofloxacin or ceftriaxone, at least
      6 weeks; consider indefinite oral suppression given the high relapse
      rate after stopping).
    </li>
    <li>
      Always ask about a preceding GI illness in febrile patients with
      aortic disease. Patients often do not volunteer a self-limited
      diarrheal episode weeks earlier, and failure to elicit this history
      delays the diagnosis.
    </li>
  </ul>
