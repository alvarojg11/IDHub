<h2 className="text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900 mb-6">
  Rationale
</h2>

<div className="mx-auto max-w-5xl">
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Hypervirulent <em>Klebsiella pneumoniae</em> (hvKP) is a distinct pathotype characterized
    by hypermucoviscosity, specific capsular serotypes (predominantly K1 and K2), and a
    constellation of virulence genes (including <em>rmpA</em>, <em>rmpA2</em>, and the
    aerobactin siderophore locus <em>iucA</em>) that confer enhanced resistance to
    phagocytosis, complement-mediated killing, and intracellular destruction. Unlike classical
    <em>K. pneumoniae</em>, which predominantly causes healthcare-associated infections in
    immunocompromised or debilitated hosts, hvKP causes community-acquired infections in
    otherwise healthy individuals, with diabetes mellitus as the dominant host risk factor.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    The Klebsiella liver abscess syndrome, defined as primary pyogenic liver abscess caused
    by hvKP with metastatic spread to distant sites, was first described in Taiwan in the
    1980s and has since been recognized globally. The primary hepatic abscess is typically
    solitary, large (often 5 to 10 cm), and located in the right lobe; unlike classical
    pyogenic liver abscesses, it arises without antecedent biliary disease, gastrointestinal
    malignancy, or hepatobiliary manipulation. Hematogenous seeding from the liver produces
    metastatic foci in the eye (endophthalmitis), central nervous system (meningitis, brain
    abscess), lung (pulmonary abscess), and soft tissues (necrotizing fasciitis).
    Endophthalmitis is the most feared metastatic complication and occurs in 3 to 10% of cases.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    The string test is the bedside phenotypic marker of hypermucoviscosity. A positive result,
    defined as a viscous string exceeding 5 mm when a colony is lifted from the agar surface
    with an inoculation loop, reflects the overproduction of capsular polysaccharide driven
    by <em>rmpA</em> and <em>rmpA2</em>, which upregulate capsule biosynthesis genes. The
    string test can be performed within minutes at the laboratory bench without specialized
    equipment, making it a practical screening tool. Confirmatory characterization relies on
    K-antigen serotyping or molecular detection of virulence genes by PCR.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    A critical and often underappreciated feature of hvKP is its paradoxical antimicrobial
    susceptibility profile. While classical <em>K. pneumoniae</em> is increasingly associated
    with ESBL and carbapenemase production, hvKP is typically susceptible to third-generation
    cephalosporins, carbapenems, and fluoroquinolones. Ceftriaxone is appropriate definitive
    therapy in most cases. The threat posed by hvKP is its virulence, not its resistance,
    though convergent strains harboring both hypervirulence and acquired resistance genes are
    an emerging concern.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Endogenous endophthalmitis caused by hvKP carries one of the worst visual prognoses
    of any infectious ophthalmologic emergency. The blood-retinal barrier limits systemic
    antibiotic penetration into the vitreous, making intravitreal delivery essential.
    Intravitreal vancomycin (1 mg/0.1 mL) and ceftazidime (2.25 mg/0.1 mL) provide
    broad-spectrum coverage while awaiting culture results. Pars plana vitrectomy reduces
    bacterial load, allows direct microbiologic sampling, and improves drug penetration,
    but even with combined intravitreal therapy and vitrectomy, the majority of patients
    with hvKP endophthalmitis retain only light perception or no light perception in the
    affected eye. Source control of the primary liver abscess, through percutaneous or
    surgical drainage, is equally essential for clearing bacteremia and preventing further
    metastatic seeding.
  </p>
</div>

  Teaching points

    <li>
      Hypervirulent <em>K. pneumoniae</em> (hvKP) causes a distinct syndrome of
      <strong>community-acquired pyogenic liver abscess</strong> (typically solitary,
      large, and right-lobed) with metastatic spread to the eye, CNS, and lung. Diabetes
      mellitus is the dominant host risk factor.
    </li>
    <li>
      <strong>Endophthalmitis is the most feared metastatic complication</strong> of
      hvKP bacteremia. Any diabetic patient with <em>K. pneumoniae</em> bacteremia and
      fever should prompt an urgent search for a liver abscess, and any visual complaint
      in this setting demands immediate ophthalmologic evaluation.
    </li>
    <li>
      The <strong>string test</strong> (a viscous string greater than 5 mm when a colony
      is lifted with an inoculation loop) is the bedside phenotypic marker of
      hypermucoviscosity and the hallmark of hvKP. It is fast, inexpensive, and requires
      no specialized equipment.
    </li>
    <li>
      hvKP is <strong>paradoxically drug-susceptible</strong>: unlike classical
      <em>K. pneumoniae</em> (which is frequently ESBL or carbapenem-resistant),
      hvKP is typically susceptible to ceftriaxone and other standard agents. Its
      danger is virulence, not resistance.
    </li>
    <li>
      Endogenous endophthalmitis requires <strong>intravitreal antibiotics</strong>
      (vancomycin plus ceftazidime) because systemic drugs cannot achieve therapeutic
      vitreous concentrations across the blood-retinal barrier. Topical antibiotics
      alone are inadequate.
    </li>
    <li>
      <strong>Visual prognosis is poor</strong> despite aggressive treatment. The
      majority of patients with hvKP endophthalmitis lose all useful vision in the
      affected eye, reinforcing the importance of recognizing this syndrome before
      ocular seeding occurs.
    </li>
    <li>
      Source control of the liver abscess via percutaneous or surgical drainage is
      essential alongside antibiotics. Bacteremia will not reliably clear without
      draining the primary focus.
    </li>
