<h2 className="text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900 mb-6">
  Rationale
</h2>

<div className="mx-auto max-w-5xl">
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Primary cutaneous nocardiosis usually follows direct inoculation after trauma
    and can present as localized disease, mycetoma, or a lymphocutaneous pattern
    with nodules spreading proximally along lymphatic channels (a sporotrichoid
    pattern). This appearance often leads clinicians to initially consider
    sporotrichosis, a routine bacterial skin and soft tissue infection, or nontuberculous mycobacterial
    infection.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    On Gram stain, <em>Nocardia</em> classically appears as branching, beaded,
    filamentous Gram-positive rods. On modified acid-fast staining, organisms are
    often weakly acid-fast, which helps distinguish nocardiosis from several other
    filamentous bacteria. In culture, <em>Nocardia</em> may require several days to
    grow and often forms dry, chalky, white-to-pigmented colonies, so clinicians
    should communicate suspicion early and ensure the laboratory holds cultures long
    enough for recovery and speciation.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Patients with nocardiosis should be evaluated for deeper or disseminated
    infection (especially pulmonary involvement) and for immunocompromising
    conditions, but primary cutaneous disease can occur in immunocompetent hosts
    after inoculation exposures, sometimes without a clearly recalled injury.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Treatment should be individualized to the syndrome, species, and susceptibility
    profile. TMP-SMX is commonly used as initial therapy for suspected cutaneous
    nocardiosis, while alternatives such as minocycline may be appropriate for some
    isolates depending on susceptibility testing and patient factors. Treatment
    duration is typically longer than for routine skin and soft tissue infections.
  </p>
</div>

<h2 className="text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900 mb-6">
  Teaching points
</h2>

<div className="mx-auto max-w-5xl">
  <ul className="list-disc pl-6 text-lg leading-7 text-gray-800 space-y-2">
    <li>
      A sporotrichoid pattern after inoculation (often minor or unrecognized trauma)
      is a syndrome, not a diagnosis; the differential includes <em>Sporothrix</em>,
      <em>Nocardia</em>, and nontuberculous mycobacteria.
    </li>
    <li>
      Branching Gram-positive, weakly acid-fast filaments strongly suggest
      <em>Nocardia</em>.
    </li>
    <li>
      <em>N. brasiliensis</em> is a major cause of primary cutaneous nocardiosis.
    </li>
    <li>
      Primary (cutaneous) nocardiosis can occur in immunocompetent hosts.
    </li>
    <li>
      Reassess the diagnosis when a presumed skin and soft tissue infection worsens
      despite empiric therapy.
    </li>
    <li>
      Confirm species and susceptibility when possible, and tailor anti-nocardial
      therapy accordingly.
    </li>
  </ul>
</div>
