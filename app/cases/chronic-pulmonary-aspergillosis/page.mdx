<h2 className="text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900 mb-6">
  Rationale
</h2>

<div className="mx-auto max-w-5xl">
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Chronic pulmonary aspergillosis (CPA) is a distinct syndrome from invasive
    pulmonary aspergillosis, and confusing the two leads to the wrong
    diagnostics and the wrong treatment. CPA arises in patients with
    pre-existing structural lung damage but without the profound
    immunosuppression that characterizes invasive disease. The single most
    common substrate is a residual tuberculous cavity; an estimated 1.2 million
    people worldwide develop CPA as a sequela of pulmonary tuberculosis, with
    the highest burden in TB-endemic regions such as Peru. Other substrates
    include chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, non-CF bronchiectasis,
    sarcoidosis, prior pneumothorax or pneumonectomy, and ankylosing
    spondylitis. The pathogenesis is one of colonization followed by local
    tissue invasion: inhaled <em>Aspergillus</em> conidia germinate in a
    pre-existing cavity, proliferate into a tangled mycelial mass (the fungal
    ball or aspergilloma), and progressively erode the cavity wall, generating
    new cavities, surrounding inflammation, and the systemic symptoms of chronic
    infection. Crucially, this is not an angioinvasive process in the typical
    CPA patient, which is why the clinical course is indolent and the serum
    biomarker profile differs from invasive disease.
  </p>
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    CPA encompasses several overlapping entities that the WHO 2024 guide and the
    ERS/ATS consensus define along a spectrum. A <em>simple pulmonary
    aspergilloma</em> is a single fungal ball in a single, stable cavity in an
    otherwise asymptomatic patient, with no systemic symptoms and no
    radiographic progression &mdash; it may simply be observed. <em>Chronic
    cavitary pulmonary aspergillosis (CCPA)</em>, the diagnosis here, is
    defined by one or more cavities (with or without fungal balls), radiographic
    progression or new cavity formation, systemic symptoms such as weight loss
    and night sweats, and a positive <em>Aspergillus</em>-specific IgG, present
    for at least three months. Untreated CCPA can progress to <em>chronic
    fibrosing pulmonary aspergillosis (CFPA)</em>, in which extensive
    replacement of lung parenchyma by fibrosis causes major irreversible loss of
    pulmonary function. <em>Subacute invasive pulmonary aspergillosis</em>
    (formerly chronic necrotizing) sits closer to invasive disease, progressing
    over one to three months with more tissue destruction and some degree of
    angioinvasion, and is managed more aggressively. Recognizing where a patient
    sits on this spectrum determines how urgently and aggressively to treat.
  </p>
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    The diagnostic approach to CPA rests on three pillars: characteristic
    imaging, a compatible host, and immunologic or microbiologic evidence of
    <em> Aspergillus</em>. On contrast chest CT, the hallmarks are an enlarging
    or new cavity containing a dependent rounded soft-tissue mass surrounded by
    a crescent of air &mdash; the air-crescent sign (also called Monad's sign)
    &mdash; with pericavitary ground-glass infiltrate, cavity-wall thickening,
    and adjacent pleural thickening. Because the fungal ball is gravity-dependent
    and mobile, prone imaging may demonstrate a shift in its position, a useful
    confirmatory maneuver. The immunologic cornerstone is
    <em>Aspergillus</em>-specific IgG (historically measured as immunodiffusion
    precipitins), which has roughly 90% sensitivity and specificity for CPA and
    is incorporated into the diagnostic criteria. This is the mirror image of
    invasive aspergillosis, where serum galactomannan &mdash; not IgG &mdash; is
    the key biomarker; in CPA, serum galactomannan is usually negative because
    there is no meaningful angioinvasion to release antigen into the
    bloodstream. Sputum culture frequently grows <em>A. fumigatus</em> and is
    valuable for speciation and susceptibility testing, but it cannot diagnose
    CPA in isolation because airway colonization is common. Beta-D-glucan is
    nonspecific and unhelpful for confirming the diagnosis. Mycobacterial
    cultures should always be sent and followed, because active tuberculosis or
    nontuberculous mycobacterial disease can coexist with CPA.
  </p>
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    The treatment of CPA is nuanced, and the choice among the available options
    depends on the subtype, the extent of disease, the severity of hemoptysis,
    and the patient's pulmonary reserve and ability to tolerate prolonged
    therapy. The foundation for symptomatic CCPA is a prolonged course of an
    oral mold-active triazole. Both <em>itraconazole</em> and
    <em>voriconazole</em> are guideline-recommended first-line agents (ERS/ATS
    consensus, IDSA 2016, and WHO 2024 guide); azoles are the only class of oral
    drugs with reliable activity against <em>Aspergillus</em>. Itraconazole is
    the most affordable and widely available option, which is why the WHO guide
    &mdash; written for TB-endemic, resource-limited settings &mdash; emphasizes
    it; the oral solution is preferred over capsules because of superior and
    more predictable bioavailability. Voriconazole achieves higher and more
    consistent lung tissue concentrations and is generally preferred for the
    more aggressive subacute invasive form or for severe disease. Both drugs
    require therapeutic drug monitoring: itraconazole because of erratic
    absorption and a wide concentration range, and voriconazole because of
    CYP2C19 polymorphism that produces enormous inter-patient variability.
    Isavuconazole, with more predictable pharmacokinetics and fewer drug
    interactions, and posaconazole are effective second-line or salvage options
    for intolerance, failure, or azole resistance. Fluconazole has no role
    because <em>Aspergillus</em> is intrinsically resistant to it.
  </p>
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    The duration of therapy is a frequent source of error. CPA is not a
    two-week course. The minimum recommended duration is six months, and most
    patients with CCPA require twelve months or longer to achieve durable
    clinical and radiographic stability; some need long-term suppressive
    therapy. Response is monitored with serial assessment of symptoms, chest
    CT, and the Aspergillus-specific IgG titer, which should fall with
    successful treatment. Stopping therapy too early &mdash; or choosing a drug
    with inadequate activity &mdash; predictably leads to relapse. If oral
    triazoles fail, are not tolerated, or meet azole resistance, intravenous
    liposomal amphotericin B or an echinocandin can be used as salvage, but
    these are inferior for long-term management because of toxicity (amphotericin
    nephrotoxicity) and only fungistatic activity against
    <em>Aspergillus</em> (echinocandins); they are reserved for subacute
    invasive disease or refractory CPA, not for first-line use.
  </p>
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Hemoptysis is the most feared complication of CPA and merits its own
    management ladder. The source of bleeding is not the pulmonary arterial
    circulation but the hypertrophied systemic bronchial arteries that
    neo-vascularize the cavity wall under the angiogenic drive of chronic
    <em>Aspergillus</em> inflammation. Mild blood-streaked sputum can be
    managed by initiating or optimizing antifungal therapy, which reduces
    recurrence by controlling the underlying inflammation. Moderate, recurrent,
    or life-threatening hemoptysis warrants <em>bronchial artery
    embolization</em>, an interventional radiology procedure that selectively
    occludes the feeding bronchial arteries; it is highly effective as an
    acute measure but is a bridge rather than a cure, because revascularization
    and rebleeding are common without definitive control of the infection.
    <em>Surgical resection</em> is the definitive treatment for hemoptysis when
    disease is localized &mdash; typically a single aspergilloma or a resectable
    cavity in a patient with adequate pulmonary reserve, assessed by predicted
    postoperative lung function. Surgery is also curative for localized
    resectable CCPA, offering the best chance of eradication in the right
    candidate. It is contraindicated in extensive bilateral disease or in
    patients with such limited reserve that resection would leave them
    ventilator-dependent; in those patients, lifelong medical management is the
    only option. The correct sequencing for this patient &mdash; medical
    therapy plus bronchial artery embolization for the active bleeding, with
    surgery held in reserve for localized or refractory disease &mdash;
    reflects this hierarchy.
  </p>
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Two additional considerations shape modern CPA management. First,
    azole-resistant <em>Aspergillus fumigatus</em> is an emerging global threat,
    driven both by environmental exposure to agricultural azole fungicides
    (the TR34/L98H mechanism) and by selection pressure during prolonged
    patient therapy. Because resistance predicts treatment failure, isolates
    from CPA patients should undergo antifungal susceptibility testing, and a
    rising IgG titer or progressive imaging despite adequate drug levels should
    prompt resistance testing and a switch to an alternative agent or class.
    Second, deciding <em>not</em> to treat is occasionally the right answer: an
    asymptomatic patient with a single, stable simple aspergilloma and no
    systemic symptoms or radiographic progression can be observed, since oral
    triazoles penetrate the cavity and fungal ball poorly and offer little
    benefit when there is no progressive disease to suppress. The art of CPA
    management lies in correctly classifying where a patient sits on the
    spectrum and tailoring the intensity and modality of therapy accordingly.
  </p>
</div>
