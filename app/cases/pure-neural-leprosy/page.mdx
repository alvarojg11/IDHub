<h2 className="text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900 mb-6">
  Rationale
</h2>

<div className="mx-auto max-w-5xl">
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Mycobacterium leprae is an obligate intracellular acid-fast bacillus with a unique tropism for Schwann cells of peripheral nerves. The organism binds to the G-domain of laminin-2 in the basal lamina of Schwann cells via its surface lectin phenolic glycolipid-1, gaining entry through the alpha-dystroglycan receptor complex and the ErbB2 receptor tyrosine kinase. Once inside, it subverts the host cell by promoting demyelination, inhibiting apoptosis, and reprogramming Schwann cells into a stem cell-like phenotype that facilitates bacillary survival and dissemination along the nerve. The clinical result is a granulomatous neuropathy that preferentially affects nerves in cooler superficial anatomical locations: the ulnar nerve at the olecranon groove, the common peroneal nerve at the fibular head, the median nerve at the carpal tunnel, the posterior tibial nerve at the medial malleolus, and the great auricular nerve.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Pure neural leprosy accounts for approximately 2 to 10 percent of all new leprosy cases depending on the geographic region, with the highest prevalence reported from India, Brazil, Indonesia, and Nepal. It is defined by the absence of skin lesions at presentation and throughout the course of disease, with clinical involvement limited to peripheral nerves. The incubation period of leprosy averages 3 to 5 years but can extend beyond a decade, which explains why patients may present years after leaving an endemic area. The mononeuritis multiplex pattern is the most common nerve involvement pattern in PNL, occurring in approximately half of cases, followed by mononeuropathy and asymmetric polyneuropathy. The most frequently affected nerves are the common peroneal, producing foot drop, and the ulnar, producing claw hand deformity. Up to 20 percent of PNL patients develop skin lesions on follow-up, at which point they are reclassified on the Ridley-Jopling spectrum.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    The diagnostic challenge in PNL is that the standard tools of leprosy diagnosis — slit-skin smear and skin biopsy — are negative because the bacilli are confined to nerve tissue. The bedside clues that should raise suspicion for PNL in any patient presenting with neuropathy are: asymmetric nerve involvement in a mononeuritis multiplex distribution, palpably thickened and tender peripheral nerves at characteristic anatomical sites, a patient originating from or having lived in a leprosy-endemic country, and a normal CSF protein that argues against CIDP. High-resolution nerve ultrasound has emerged as a powerful noninvasive diagnostic tool that can demonstrate the characteristic asymmetric segmental nerve enlargement with loss of the normal fascicular honeycomb pattern, altered echotexture, and increased intraneural vascularity on Doppler. The sonographic pattern in leprosy differs from CIDP, which produces uniform symmetric enlargement, and from compression neuropathy, which produces focal enlargement limited to entrapment sites. In the 2025 study by Roy et al., ultrasound-guided fine needle aspiration cytology of enlarged nerves achieved diagnostic confirmation in 82.8 percent of PNL cases. However, nerve biopsy remains the gold standard, demonstrating epithelioid granulomas within the endoneurium and perineurium with perineurial thickening and, in approximately 75 percent of cases, acid-fast bacilli on Fite-Faraco stain.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Treatment requires both antimicrobial therapy and anti-inflammatory management. Multibacillary multidrug therapy with rifampin, dapsone, and clofazimine for 12 months is the standard, though some experts extend treatment to 24 months for extensive nerve involvement. Corticosteroids are essential adjunctive therapy for neuritis, starting at prednisone 40 to 60 mg daily with a structured taper over 12 to 24 weeks. The Jardim et al. prospective case series demonstrated that combined MDT and corticosteroids prevented nerve damage progression in PNL, whereas antimicrobial therapy alone was insufficient. The window for preventing irreversible axonal loss is narrow: motor deficits such as foot drop and claw hand become permanent once Wallerian degeneration is complete. Disability prevention — including ankle-foot orthoses for foot drop, hand therapy for ulnar neuropathy, and education on protective footwear to prevent plantar trophic ulcers — must begin concurrently with antimicrobial and anti-inflammatory therapy. Leprosy reactions, particularly type 1 reversal reactions, can occur during or after MDT and may cause acute worsening of neuritis, requiring immediate corticosteroid escalation and serial nerve function monitoring.
  </p>
</div>
