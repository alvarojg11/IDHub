<h2 className="text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900 mb-6">
  Rationale
</h2>

<div className="mx-auto max-w-5xl">
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    The combination of fever, jaundice, renal failure, and conjunctival suffusion is classic for leptospirosis (Weil's disease). Exposure to floodwater and animal reservoirs (dogs, rodents) provides the epidemiologic link. The presence of thrombocytopenia, elevated creatine kinase, and meningeal signs further supports systemic leptospirosis.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Key diagnostic tests include PCR (detectable in blood during first week, urine during second week) for early diagnosis and microscopic agglutination test (MAT) for confirmatory serology. Culture is slow and insensitive for acute management. Early antibiotic therapy with intravenous penicillin G (or ceftriaxone as an alternative) reduces mortality and shortens illness duration. Antibiotic therapy may trigger a Jarisch-Herxheimer reaction, characterized by fever and hypotension, which should be managed supportively. Supportive care includes hemodialysis for renal failure and monitoring for pulmonary hemorrhage syndrome, which carries high mortality, can progress to ARDS, and may require intensive care support.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Leptospirosis is a zoonosis with global distribution, especially in tropical regions. Rodents are the primary reservoir, but dogs, livestock, and other mammals can shed Leptospira in urine. Humans acquire infection through contact with contaminated water or soil. Prevention includes rodent control, animal vaccination, and use of protective equipment in high-risk occupations.
  </p>
</div>

<h2 className="text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900 mb-6">
  Teaching Points
</h2>

<div className="mx-auto max-w-5xl">
  <ul className="list-disc pl-6 text-lg leading-7 text-gray-800 space-y-2">
    <li>
      Conjunctival suffusion is a classic physical exam finding in leptospirosis, present in up to 50% of cases. Its presence with hepatorenal dysfunction strongly suggests leptospirosis over other febrile illnesses.
    </li>
    <li>
      Leptospirosis causes a spectrum of disease from mild self-limited fever to severe Weil's disease characterized by jaundice, renal failure, and hemorrhage. Severe disease carries mortality up to 10% without appropriate treatment.
    </li>
    <li>
      Diagnosis relies on PCR (positive in blood during first week, urine during second week) for early detection and microscopic agglutination test (MAT) for confirmatory serology. Culture is slow and insensitive for acute diagnosis.
    </li>
    <li>
      Pulmonary hemorrhage syndrome occurs in 5-10% of severe cases and is the most common cause of death, with mortality up to 50%. It presents with hemoptysis, hypoxemia, and diffuse alveolar hemorrhage that can progress to acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), requiring intensive care support.
    </li>
    <li>
      Exposure to contaminated water or animal urine is the key risk factor. Occupational groups include farmers, sewer workers, and veterinarians. Prevention focuses on rodent control, protective equipment, and chemoprophylaxis with doxycycline for high-risk exposures.
    </li>
  </ul>
