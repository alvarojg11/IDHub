<h2 className="text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900 mb-6">
  Rationale
</h2>

<div className="mx-auto max-w-5xl">
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    African tick-bite fever is a spotted fever group rickettsiosis caused by
    <em> Rickettsia africae</em>, transmitted primarily by <em>Amblyomma hebraeum</em>
    and <em>Amblyomma variegatum</em> ticks in sub-Saharan Africa and parts of the
    Caribbean. Among travelers returning from southern Africa, it is one of the most
    common causes of febrile illness after malaria. The disease usually follows rural
    exposure rather than urban travel: game parks, brush, cattle fields, and safari
    walking routes are classic settings.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    The bedside clue is the eschar, often accompanied by regional lymphadenopathy,
    headache, myalgias, and fever. Rash may be faint or absent, which is one reason
    the diagnosis is missed. Multiple eschars are especially suggestive of
    <em> R. africae</em> because <em>Amblyomma</em> ticks frequently bite more than once;
    this feature helps separate African tick-bite fever from Mediterranean spotted
    fever caused by <em>Rickettsia conorii</em>, which more often produces a solitary
    tache noire and a more florid rash.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Diagnosis in the first week is a practical problem because standard serology often
    lags behind the illness. Indirect immunofluorescence may be negative early and
    only become useful on convalescent testing. When an eschar is present, PCR of an
    eschar swab or biopsy offers the best early yield because it samples the site of
    direct inoculation. Traveler surveillance data show that acute serology can be
    nonreactive even when eschar PCR is already positive, which is exactly why the
    lesion itself should not be ignored.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Treatment is straightforward: doxycycline should be started as soon as the disease
    is suspected. Most patients defervesce rapidly, often within 24 to 48 hours. The
    illness is usually self-limited and less severe than some other rickettsioses, but
    that relative mildness is not a reason to wait. In returning travelers, the right
    move is often to treat presumptively while confirming the diagnosis with an eschar
    specimen rather than waiting for the antibodies to catch up.
  </p>
</div>
