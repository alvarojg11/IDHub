<h2 className="text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900 mb-6">
  Rationale
</h2>

<div className="mx-auto max-w-5xl">
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Toxoplasma gondii is an obligate intracellular apicomplexan parasite with a complex life cycle that completes its sexual phase only in felids. Human infection occurs through two principal routes: ingestion of tissue cysts containing bradyzoites in undercooked or raw meat (particularly lamb, pork, and wild game), and ingestion of oocysts shed in cat feces that have sporulated in the environment. This patient had both exposures: regular consumption of raw beef tartare and two indoor-outdoor cats capable of hunting and shedding oocysts. Less commonly, transmission occurs through contaminated water, unwashed produce, organ transplantation, or congenital routes. Global seroprevalence ranges from approximately 10% in the United States to over 60% in parts of Latin America, sub-Saharan Africa, and Southeast Asia, reflecting dietary habits, climate (oocyst survival is favored by warm humid conditions), and cat population density.
  </p>
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    In immunocompetent adults, acute acquired toxoplasmosis most commonly presents as a mononucleosis-like illness. Cervical lymphadenopathy — particularly of the posterior cervical chain — is the hallmark, present in over 80% of symptomatic patients. Nodes are typically enlarged (1 to 3 cm), firm, mobile, and non-tender, distinguishing them from the tender, fluctuant nodes of bacterial lymphadenitis or the matted, fixed nodes of tuberculous or malignant disease. Systemic symptoms include low-grade fever, fatigue, myalgias, and headache. Mild transaminitis occurs in approximately 25% of patients. Crucially, pharyngitis, tonsillar exudate, splenomegaly, and atypical lymphocytes — all features of EBV infectious mononucleosis — are typically absent in toxoplasmosis, providing important clinical discrimination. Most patients recover spontaneously within one to two months, though lymphadenopathy may persist for up to six months in a minority.
  </p>
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    The differential diagnosis of heterophile-negative mononucleosis-like illness with cervical lymphadenopathy includes CMV infection, acute HIV seroconversion, cat-scratch disease (Bartonella henselae), toxoplasmosis, and lymphoma. CMV more often produces generalized lymphadenopathy and atypical lymphocytes on smear. Acute HIV seroconversion can cause fever and lymphadenopathy but is typically accompanied by a rash, mucocutaneous ulcers, and a more acute onset within two to four weeks of exposure. Cat-scratch disease usually presents with unilateral tender lymphadenopathy and a visible inoculation papule at the site of a cat scratch. Hodgkin lymphoma causes painless lymphadenopathy but is not associated with the fever pattern, transaminitis, or positive Toxoplasma serology seen here. The combination of positive Toxoplasma IgM and IgG, bilateral non-tender posterior cervical lymphadenopathy, raw meat and cat exposure, and negative EBV, CMV, and HIV testing makes acute acquired toxoplasmosis the leading diagnosis.
  </p>
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Serologic diagnosis of acute toxoplasmosis relies on IgM and IgG antibody detection, but interpretation requires care. Toxoplasma IgG appears within one to two weeks of infection, peaks at one to two months, and persists for life — its presence confirms exposure but does not distinguish acute from remote infection. IgM appears within the first week and can persist for months to years in up to 25% of patients, making a positive IgM result suggestive but not definitive for acute infection. False-positive IgM results also occur with many commercial ELISA kits due to cross-reactivity or rheumatoid factor interference. IgG avidity testing resolves this ambiguity: during the early immune response, IgG antibodies have low binding affinity for Toxoplasma antigens. Over the subsequent three to five months, affinity maturation produces high-avidity IgG. A low-avidity result on the current specimen confirms recent acquisition, while a high-avidity result excludes infection within the past three to five months. This test can be performed on the initial serum sample without requiring serial draws.
  </p>
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    The management of acute acquired toxoplasmosis in immunocompetent patients is predominantly supportive. The disease is self-limited, and specific anti-toxoplasma therapy is not indicated for uncomplicated cases. Treatment with pyrimethamine combined with sulfadiazine and leucovorin — the standard regimen for cerebral toxoplasmosis, ocular toxoplasmosis, and disseminated disease — is reserved for patients with severe or persistent symptoms (typically beyond four to six weeks), organ involvement (chorioretinitis, myocarditis, pneumonitis), or immunocompromise. The rationale for restraint is that the drug regimen carries significant toxicity: pyrimethamine causes dose-dependent bone marrow suppression requiring twice-weekly complete blood count monitoring, and sulfadiazine carries risks of hypersensitivity, crystalluria, and acute interstitial nephritis. In an immunocompetent host whose immune system is effectively controlling the infection, the risk-benefit calculus favors observation. Patients should be counseled about expected disease course, advised to report new visual changes (chorioretinitis may develop in up to 2% of acute cases), and scheduled for follow-up in two to four weeks. Persistent or worsening lymphadenopathy beyond four to six weeks should prompt excisional biopsy to exclude lymphoma.
  </p>
