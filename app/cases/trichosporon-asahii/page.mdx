<h2 className="text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900 mb-6">
  Rationale
</h2>

<div className="mx-auto max-w-5xl">
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    <em>Trichosporon asahii</em> is a basidiomycetous yeast found in soil, water,
    and as a commensal on human skin and the gastrointestinal tract. It is the
    most clinically relevant species within the genus and the principal cause of
    disseminated trichosporonosis, a life-threatening infection that occurs almost
    exclusively in profoundly immunocompromised patients: recipients of allogeneic
    hematopoietic stem cell transplants, patients receiving induction chemotherapy
    for hematologic malignancy, and solid organ transplant recipients. Prolonged
    neutropenia is the dominant risk factor; mortality in disseminated disease
    remains high, often exceeding 60% in published series.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    The clinical presentation of disseminated trichosporonosis typically includes
    persistent fever despite broad-spectrum antibacterial and antifungal coverage,
    fungemia, and characteristic skin lesions. The skin lesions are a critical
    clinical clue: they appear as erythematous papules or nodules, often with
    central umbilication, pale centers, or necrosis, and are distributed on the
    trunk and extremities. These lesions reflect hematogenous seeding and provide
    the most accessible tissue for early diagnosis. Biopsy reveals hyaline septate
    hyphae, pseudohyphae, and the pathognomonic barrel-shaped arthroconidia formed
    by fragmentation of hyphae at septa. Blastoconidia may also be present.
    The organism grows readily on standard mycology media including Sabouraud
    dextrose agar, producing white to cream-colored, dry, cerebriform colonies.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    The false-positive cryptococcal antigen is the key diagnostic pitfall in this
    case. <em>T. asahii</em> produces glucuronoxylomannan (GXM)-like polysaccharides
    in its cell wall that are antigenically cross-reactive with the capsular GXM
    of <em>Cryptococcus neoformans</em> and <em>C. gattii</em>. Both the traditional
    latex agglutination and the newer lateral flow assay formats detect these
    cross-reactive antigens, producing false-positive CrAg results. The titer is
    usually low (1:2 to 1:16), as in this case, but titer alone cannot reliably
    exclude cross-reactivity: tissue diagnosis is essential. Clinicians who
    encounter a low-titer positive CrAg in a neutropenic BMT recipient should
    consider Trichosporon prominently in the differential.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Antifungal therapy for <em>T. asahii</em> requires understanding two critical
    resistance patterns. First, echinocandins (micafungin, caspofungin,
    anidulafungin) have no meaningful activity against Trichosporon: the organism
    either lacks the target enzyme (1&#8594;3)-&#946;-D-glucan synthase or
    possesses structural adaptations that render the echinocandin class ineffective.
    This is the most consequential resistance pattern to recognize, as echinocandins
    are commonly used empirically in febrile neutropenia. Second, amphotericin B
    has variable and often suboptimal activity, with elevated MICs and poor
    clinical outcomes despite in vitro susceptibility in some isolates.
    Voriconazole, a second-generation triazole, demonstrates the lowest MICs
    against <em>T. asahii</em> of all available agents and is the treatment of
    choice based on in vitro data and clinical case series. Therapeutic drug
    monitoring with voriconazole trough targets of 1 to 5.5 mg/L is recommended.
    Recovery of neutrophil count remains the most important prognostic variable:
    immune reconstitution is essential for clearance of infection.
  </p>
</div>
