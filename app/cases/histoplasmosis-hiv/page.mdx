<h2 className="text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900 mb-6">
  Rationale
</h2>

<div className="mx-auto max-w-5xl">
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    <em>Histoplasma capsulatum</em> is a thermally dimorphic fungus endemic
    to the Mississippi and Ohio River valleys in the United States and
    throughout Central and South America, particularly Honduras, Guatemala,
    Mexico, and Brazil. In soil, it grows as a mold producing infectious
    microconidia; at body temperature, it converts to the yeast phase.
    Infection follows inhalation of microconidia from contaminated soil,
    often associated with disturbance of bird or bat guano (construction,
    spelunking, demolition of old buildings). Most immunocompetent individuals
    develop asymptomatic or self-limited pulmonary infection; progressive
    disseminated histoplasmosis occurs almost exclusively in patients with
    impaired cellular immunity, particularly advanced HIV with CD4 counts
    below 150 cells/mcL.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    In patients with advanced AIDS, <em>H. capsulatum</em> disseminates
    hematogenously to the mononuclear phagocyte system: bone marrow, liver,
    spleen, lymph nodes, and adrenal glands. The clinical presentation is
    often subacute and nonspecific, mimicking other AIDS-defining illnesses
    including disseminated Mycobacterium avium complex, disseminated
    cytomegalovirus, or lymphoma. The triad of prolonged fever, weight loss,
    and pancytopenia with hepatosplenomegaly in a patient from an endemic
    region with a very low CD4 count should immediately trigger a urine
    Histoplasma antigen. Laboratory clues include markedly elevated ferritin
    (often greater than 10,000 ng/mL), elevated LDH, elevated alkaline
    phosphatase, and cytopenias reflecting bone marrow infiltration.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    The urine Histoplasma antigen assay has sensitivity exceeding 90 percent
    in disseminated AIDS-related histoplasmosis and can return results within
    hours, enabling diagnosis and treatment initiation far earlier than
    cultures (which require two to four weeks). Serum antigen has slightly
    lower sensitivity than urine; sending both maximizes detection. The
    Histoplasma antigen cross-reacts with other endemic fungi (Blastomyces,
    Coccidioides, Paracoccidioides, Talaromyces), so clinical and geographic
    context must inform interpretation. Serial antigen levels are useful for
    monitoring treatment response and detecting relapse.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    For moderate-to-severe disseminated histoplasmosis, liposomal amphotericin
    B 3 mg/kg/day for at least two weeks is the induction regimen of choice,
    followed by oral itraconazole 200 mg twice daily for a minimum of 12
    months. Itraconazole levels should be checked after two weeks of therapy
    to confirm therapeutic concentrations (random level greater than 1-3
    mcg/mL). Fluconazole should not be substituted for itraconazole. ART
    should be initiated within two weeks or less of starting antifungal therapy to
    accelerate immune recovery; immune reconstitution inflammatory syndrome
    (IRIS) can occur but is manageable and does not outweigh the benefit of
    early ART initiation in patients with very low CD4 counts. Maintenance
    itraconazole can be discontinued after 12 months if the CD4 count exceeds
    150 cells/mcL on suppressive ART and the Histoplasma urine antigen is
    undetectable.
  </p>
</div>

      hepatosplenomegaly from an endemic region (Ohio/Mississippi Valley,
      Central and South America).
    </li>
    <li>
      Markedly elevated ferritin (often greater than 10,000 ng/mL) and LDH
      in this context are strong clues. Send urine Histoplasma antigen early:
      sensitivity exceeds 90 percent in disseminated AIDS-related disease and
      results return in hours.
    </li>
    <li>
      On bone marrow aspirate, <em>H. capsulatum</em> appears as small
      (2 to 4 micrometer) intracellular yeast within macrophages, surrounded
      by a clear halo. The key mimic is <em>Leishmania donovani</em>:
      distinguish by the kinetoplast (visible in Leishmania, absent in
      Histoplasma) and geography.
    </li>

      Treat moderate-to-severe disease with liposomal amphotericin B

      and should not be used.
