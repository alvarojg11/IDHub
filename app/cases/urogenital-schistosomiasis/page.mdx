</h2>

<div className="mx-auto max-w-5xl">
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    A 39-year-old man from western Mozambique presents with 5 days of progressive
    shortness of breath. He reports marked orthopnea and paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea.
    Over the same interval, he has developed continuous bilateral flank pain and persistent
    nausea. He denies cough, fever, and chest pain.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    He was told he had arterial hypertension 2 years earlier and was prescribed medication,
    but he never took it and had no further evaluation. His past medical and family history
    are otherwise unremarkable. A recent HIV test was negative. He works as a fisherman and
    has frequent freshwater exposure in endemic areas.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    On examination, he is pale and in respiratory distress but does not appear chronically ill.
    His blood pressure is 200/130 mmHg, pulse 66/min, temperature 36.8 C, and respiratory rate
    32/min. The apex beat is mildly displaced. Heart sounds are regular without an obvious murmur.
    Jugular venous pressure is not elevated. Fine bibasilar crackles are present. The abdomen is
    soft and non-tender, but there is bilateral renal angle tenderness, and both kidneys are
    ballotable. There is no peripheral edema.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Initial laboratory testing shows WBC 3.8 x 10^9/L, hemoglobin 6.0 g/dL, MCV 92 fL,
    platelets 187 x 10^9/L, creatinine 12.1 mg/dL, BUN 250 mg/dL, and potassium 7.2 mmol/L.
    Chest radiography shows an enlarged cardiac silhouette with lower-zone hazy opacification
    consistent with pulmonary edema. CT abdomen and pelvis demonstrated massive right-sided hydroureteronephrosis
    and moderate left-sided hydronephrosis.
  </p>
</div>

<h2 className="text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900 mb-6">
  Rationale
</h2>

<div className="mx-auto max-w-5xl">
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    This patient presents with a combined cardiorenal syndrome: acute decompensated
    left-sided heart failure, severe kidney failure, hyperkalemia, and profound anemia.
    The striking clues are the bilateral flank pain, enlarged ballotable kidneys, and
    markedly elevated creatinine. Those findings argue against uncomplicated hypertensive
    nephrosclerosis, which would more typically produce small chronically scarred kidneys.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    In a patient with heavy freshwater exposure in an endemic region of southern Africa,
    chronic genitourinary schistosomiasis due to <em>Schistosoma haematobium</em> should be
    high on the differential. Chronic egg deposition in the bladder and distal ureters can
    lead to granulomatous inflammation, fibrosis, obstructive uropathy, hydronephrosis,
    recurrent infection, and eventually end-stage kidney disease.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    The CT abdomen and pelvis has already established advanced obstructive uropathy, with
    severe right-sided hydroureteronephrosis and contralateral hydronephrosis. Once that is
    known, the most useful next investigation is cystoscopy, because it can evaluate the
    bladder and ureteric orifice region directly and help identify the lesion responsible
    for distal obstruction.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    In chronic <em>Schistosoma haematobium</em> infection, cystoscopy may show characteristic
    hyperemia and so-called sandy patches caused by chronic mucosal granulomatous inflammation.
    Tissue biopsy is ideal when available because it can confirm ova in tissue and help exclude
    bladder malignancy. Urine microscopy may be negative in advanced disease because egg
    excretion often declines once fibrosis is established.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Management is urgent and initially supportive: hyperkalemia treatment, relief of urinary
    obstruction, and dialysis if available. Praziquantel may still be given to eradicate
    viable adult worms, but in advanced obstructive disease it does not reverse established
    hydronephrosis or renal failure by itself.
  </p>
</div>

<h2 className="text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900 mb-6">
  Teaching points
</h2>

<div className="mx-auto max-w-5xl">
  <ul className="list-disc pl-6 text-lg leading-7 text-gray-800 space-y-2">
    <li>
      Chronic <em>Schistosoma haematobium</em> infection can present late with obstructive uropathy,
      hydronephrosis, and end-stage kidney disease.
    </li>
    <li>
      Enlarged ballotable kidneys in severe renal failure should push the differential toward
      obstruction rather than hypertensive nephrosclerosis alone.
    </li>
    <li>
      In endemic settings, urine ova testing can be negative in advanced chronic disease and
      does not exclude urogenital schistosomiasis.
    </li>
    <li>
      After cross-sectional imaging has shown hydronephrosis, cystoscopy is often the most useful next
      step to identify the distal lesion and support the diagnosis.
    </li>
    <li>
      Sandy patches on cystoscopy are highly suggestive of chronic genitourinary schistosomiasis.
    </li>
    <li>
      Praziquantel may clear active infection, but advanced hydronephrosis often requires decompression
      and renal replacement support when available.
    </li>
  </ul>
</div>
