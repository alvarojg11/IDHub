<h2 className="text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900 mb-6">
  Rationale
</h2>

<div className="mx-auto max-w-5xl">
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Babesiosis is a tick-borne hemolytic disease caused by intraerythrocytic protozoa of
    the genus <em>Babesia</em>. In the United States, <em>Babesia microti</em> is the
    dominant species, transmitted by <em>Ixodes scapularis</em> — the same black-legged
    tick that transmits Lyme disease and human granulocytic anaplasmosis. Endemic areas
    include coastal New England (particularly Nantucket, Martha's Vineyard, Cape Cod, and
    Block Island), the upper Midwest, and parts of the mid-Atlantic. Co-infection with
    <em>Borrelia burgdorferi</em> or <em>Anaplasma phagocytophilum</em> occurs in up to
    10–20% of babesiosis cases and should always be considered.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    The central diagnostic challenge in this case is the peripheral blood smear. Both
    <em>Babesia microti</em> and <em>Plasmodium falciparum</em> can produce small
    intraerythrocytic ring forms that are morphologically similar. The features that
    favor <em>Babesia</em> over <em>Plasmodium</em> are: (1) extracellular ring forms —
    <em>Plasmodium</em> spp. complete their intraerythrocytic cycle before lysing the host
    cell, while <em>Babesia</em> merozoites can be seen free in plasma; (2) the Maltese
    cross or tetrad form — a four-cell budding structure that is pathognomonic for
    <em>Babesia</em>; (3) absence of hemozoin (malaria pigment); and (4) absence of
    banana-shaped gametocytes characteristic of <em>P. falciparum</em>. Malaria antigen
    testing and PCR should nevertheless be sent, as co-infection with <em>Plasmodium</em>
    is possible in a traveler with this exposure history.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Asplenia is the most important host risk factor for severe babesiosis. The spleen
    plays a critical role in clearing parasitized and ring-form erythrocytes; without
    it, parasitemia escalates rapidly and severe hemolytic anemia, respiratory failure,
    and death can follow within days. Other high-risk groups include patients over 50,
    those with B-cell lymphoma, HIV infection with low CD4 counts, and recipients of
    rituximab or other B-cell depleting therapies.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Treatment is with atovaquone plus azithromycin as the preferred first-line regimen
    for all disease severities. For hospitalized patients, azithromycin can be
    administered intravenously. Clindamycin plus quinine — historically considered
    the regimen of choice for severe disease — is now reserved as an alternative when
    first-line therapy fails, given its higher rate of adverse effects. Treatment
    duration is 7–10 days in immunocompetent hosts; in immunocompromised patients,
    treatment should continue for at least 6 consecutive weeks and for at least 2 weeks
    after parasites are no longer detectable on smear, as relapse risk is high.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Exchange transfusion is indicated for parasitemia greater than 10%, or for
    moderate-to-high parasitemia accompanied by severe hemolytic anemia, pulmonary
    compromise, renal failure, or hepatic dysfunction. It rapidly reduces the burden
    of parasitized erythrocytes and removes toxic byproducts of hemolysis. At 8%
    parasitemia without clear evidence of end-organ failure, exchange transfusion
    is not yet mandated but should be arranged on standby given this patient's
    asplenia and trajectory.
  </p>
</div>

  Teaching points
</h2>

<div className="mx-auto max-w-5xl">
  <ul className="list-disc pl-6 text-lg leading-7 text-gray-800 space-y-2">
    <li>
      <em>Babesia microti</em> is transmitted by <em>Ixodes scapularis</em> in the
      northeastern and upper midwestern United States; co-infection with Lyme disease
      or anaplasmosis occurs in 10–20% of cases and should always be considered.
    </li>
    <li>
      Key smear features distinguishing <em>Babesia</em> from <em>Plasmodium falciparum</em>:
      extracellular ring forms, Maltese cross tetrad (pathognomonic), absence of hemozoin,
      and absence of banana-shaped gametocytes.
    </li>
    <li>
      Asplenia is the strongest risk factor for severe babesiosis — it removes the
      primary clearance mechanism for parasitized erythrocytes and allows parasitemia
      to escalate rapidly.
    </li>
    <li>
      Atovaquone plus azithromycin is the preferred first-line regimen for all
      severities of babesiosis, including hospitalized and high-risk patients.
      Clindamycin plus quinine is an alternative reserved for treatment failure.
    </li>
    <li>
      Exchange transfusion is indicated for parasitemia &gt;10%, or for moderate-to-high
      parasitemia with severe hemolysis, pulmonary compromise, renal failure, or
      hepatic dysfunction.
    </li>
    <li>
      In immunocompromised hosts, treatment must continue for at least 6 weeks and
      for 2 weeks after the smear clears — relapse is common with shorter courses.
    </li>
    <li>
      Always send malaria antigen testing and PCR when the smear shows intraerythrocytic
      ring forms — co-infection with <em>Plasmodium</em> is possible in returning
      travelers, and the two infections can be morphologically indistinguishable.
    </li>
  </ul>
