<h2 className="text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900 mb-6">
  Rationale
</h2>

<div className="mx-auto max-w-5xl">
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    <em>Entamoeba histolytica</em> is an intestinal protozoan parasite
    transmitted via the fecal-oral route, most often through contaminated
    water or food in regions with inadequate sanitation. It is estimated to
    infect 40 to 50 million people worldwide and is responsible for
    approximately 100,000 deaths annually, making it the third leading
    parasitic cause of death globally after malaria and schistosomiasis.
    In the United States, infection is almost exclusively seen in immigrants
    from endemic regions (Latin America, South Asia, Sub-Saharan Africa)
    and travelers. The infection cycle involves ingestion of
    environmentally resistant cysts, which excyst in the small intestine
    and colonize the colon as trophozoites.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Most individuals (approximately 90%) who ingest <em>E. histolytica</em>
    remain asymptomatic carriers. Invasive disease occurs when trophozoites
    penetrate the intestinal mucosal barrier, producing amoebic colitis
    (characterized by bloody diarrhea, tenesmus, and abdominal cramps) or
    extraintestinal spread. Liver abscess is the most common extraintestinal
    complication, arising when trophozoites access the portal circulation
    from the right colon and seed the liver. The right hepatic lobe is
    preferentially affected in over 75% of cases, reflecting portal flow
    patterns. A key clinical teaching point is that concurrent intestinal
    symptoms are present in fewer than one-third of patients with amoebic
    liver abscess at the time of presentation, so the absence of diarrhea
    must never be used to exclude the diagnosis.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    The pathognomonic microscopic finding is erythrophagocytosis: the
    ingestion of host erythrocytes by trophozoites, visible as red blood
    cells within the cytoplasm of the parasite. This feature distinguishes
    <em>E. histolytica</em> from <em>E. dispar</em>, a morphologically
    identical species that colonizes the intestinal lumen without invasion
    and is non-pathogenic. Standard ova and parasite microscopy cannot
    reliably distinguish the two species, which is why antigen-based or
    serologic testing is preferred for diagnosis. Serum IgG serology is
    greater than 95% sensitive for amoebic liver abscess and is the
    cornerstone of confirmatory diagnosis. CT imaging classically shows a
    hypodense, rim-enhancing lesion that is most often solitary and in the
    right lobe. The aspirate, when obtained, is chocolate-brown or
    anchovy-paste colored, odorless, and sterile on bacterial culture.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Treatment requires a two-phase strategy. Metronidazole 750 mg orally
    three times daily for 10 days (or tinidazole 2 g once daily for 5
    days) eradicates the tissue-invasive trophozoites. However,
    metronidazole has poor luminal activity and does not reliably clear
    intestinal cysts. Failure to follow with a luminal agent risks
    relapse, re-invasion, and ongoing transmission. Paromomycin 500 mg
    orally three times daily for 7 days is the preferred luminal agent in
    the United States. Drainage is not routinely needed for uncomplicated
    amoebic liver abscess and most patients defervesce within 72 to 96
    hours of starting metronidazole. Indications for aspiration include
    large abscesses at risk of rupture, left lobe involvement, and
    failure to respond to medical therapy.
  </p>
</div>
