<h2 className="text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900 mb-6">
  Rationale
</h2>

<div className="mx-auto max-w-5xl">
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Mycoplasma genitalium is the smallest known self-replicating free-living bacterium, measuring only 200 to 300 nanometers in diameter. As a member of the class Mollicutes, it lacks a cell wall, which renders all beta-lactam antibiotics intrinsically ineffective. Its reductive evolution has produced a minimal genome of approximately 580 kilobase pairs that encodes fewer than 500 proteins, including a specialized tip organelle (the terminal attachment organelle) that mediates adherence to urethral epithelial cells and microcolony formation. These biological features explain both the organism's tissue tropism for the urogenital epithelium and its intrinsic resistance to cell-wall-active agents.
  </p>
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Epidemiologically, M. genitalium is now firmly established as a cause of non-gonococcal urethritis in men, accounting for 15 to 25% of cases. It is also associated with cervicitis, pelvic inflammatory disease, and possibly tubal factor infertility in women. Critically, it is the most common identifiable etiology of persistent or recurrent urethritis after standard empiric therapy for gonorrhea and chlamydia. The standard ceftriaxone-plus-doxycycline regimen covers N. gonorrhoeae and C. trachomatis but leaves M. genitalium largely untreated: ceftriaxone has no activity against organisms without cell walls, and doxycycline monotherapy achieves only 30 to 40% microbiologic cure for M. genitalium. This therapeutic gap is why the vignette patient's symptoms persist.
  </p>
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    The diagnostic gold standard is nucleic acid amplification testing. The Hologic Aptima assay, the only FDA-cleared test for M. genitalium, uses transcription-mediated amplification to target 16S ribosomal RNA amplicons. TMA is an isothermal amplification method that avoids the thermal cycling required by PCR. The use of ribosomal RNA (present at 10<sup>3</sup> to 10<sup>4</sup> copies per organism) rather than genomic DNA (1 copy per organism) confers high analytical sensitivity, which is important because organism burdens in clinical specimens can be as low as 10 to 1000 copies per swab. Culture, by contrast, requires weeks of incubation on specialized SP4 agar supplemented with cholesterol, produces barely visible granular colonies, and is available only in reference or research laboratories.
  </p>
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Treatment is complicated by the rapid emergence of antimicrobial resistance. Macrolide resistance in M. genitalium, driven primarily by point mutations in the 23S rRNA gene (especially A2058G and A2059G in Escherichia coli numbering), now exceeds 50% in many regions of the United States. This renders single-dose azithromycin regimens — once the standard of care — no longer reliably effective. The 2021 CDC STI Treatment Guidelines therefore recommend a sequential two-stage approach when resistance testing is unavailable: doxycycline 100 mg twice daily for 7 days to reduce bacterial load, followed by moxifloxacin 400 mg daily for 7 days. This regimen achieves cure rates exceeding 90%. When molecular macrolide resistance testing is available and confirms susceptibility, the extended azithromycin regimen (500 mg on day 1, then 250 mg daily on days 2 through 5) can be used instead of moxifloxacin after doxycycline pretreatment.
  </p>
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Test of cure (TOC) with repeat NAAT is recommended 3 to 4 weeks after completion of therapy, and all sexual partners within the preceding 60 days should be referred for evaluation and treatment. Sexual activity should be avoided until treatment is complete and symptoms have resolved.
  </p>
</div>
