    Six weeks before the current visit he developed progressive cough with purulent sputum, low-grade fevers, night sweats, and a 4 kg weight loss. The primary team empirically started azithromycin for presumed bacterial bronchitis; there was no improvement. Three weeks later he noticed a firm, tender erythematous nodule on his right forearm. Two additional smaller nodules appeared over the following two weeks. He denies hemoptysis, dyspnea at rest, or joint pain.
  </p>

No lymphadenopathy is palpated. The remainder of the examination is unremarkable.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">

<h2 className="text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900 mb-6">
  Rationale
</h2>

<div className="mx-auto max-w-5xl">
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    <em>Mycobacterium abscessus</em> complex (MABC) is the most virulent and treatment-resistant of the clinically important rapidly growing mycobacteria (RGM). It is defined by colonial growth within 7 days on solid media. In this case, a CF patient with known pretransplant MABC colonization develops disseminated disease under myeloablative immunosuppression: pulmonary infiltrates and cavitation in the transplanted lung combined with subcutaneous nodules on the forearm. Bacteremic dissemination confirms bloodstream seeding. The empiric azithromycin course was ineffective, foreshadowing the macrolide resistance finding.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    MABC comprises three subspecies with meaningfully different drug susceptibility profiles. <em>M. abscessus</em> subsp. <em>abscessus</em> carries a functional <em>erm(41)</em> gene and is the most treatment-resistant; macrolides cannot be relied upon as active agents. <em>M. abscessus</em> subsp. <em>massiliense</em> carries a truncated, non-functional <em>erm(41)</em> and is truly macrolide susceptible, allowing the continuation regimen to include an active macrolide and yielding substantially better treatment outcomes. <em>M. abscessus</em> subsp. <em>bolletii</em> has variable <em>erm(41)</em> activity and intermediate behavior. Subspecies identification by molecular methods (<em>hsp65</em> sequencing or whole genome sequencing) is therefore mandatory before finalizing any treatment plan. Among the other clinically relevant RGM, <em>M. chelonae</em> is a close relative with overlapping skin and soft tissue tropism but generally more drug susceptibility and no <em>erm(41)</em>; tobramycin is often active. <em>M. fortuitum</em>, the most common RGM overall, causes post-traumatic and post-procedural skin and soft tissue infections and is usually susceptible to fluoroquinolones, TMP-SMX, and doxycycline. <em>M. mucogenicum</em> is associated with catheter-related bloodstream infections rather than pulmonary or disseminated disease.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    The <em>erm(41)</em> mechanism deserves emphasis because it creates a diagnostic trap. Standard Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) susceptibility testing for M. abscessus reads azithromycin susceptibility at day 3, before erm(41) expression is fully induced. The organism appears susceptible. Extended 14-day incubation reveals the inducible phenotype with a markedly elevated MIC. The 2020 ATS, ERS, ESCMID, and IDSA guidelines for nontuberculous mycobacterial pulmonary disease explicitly recommend 14-day macrolide susceptibility testing for all MABC isolates and caution against interpreting day-3 results as reliable for clinical decision-making. A second and more severe form of macrolide resistance, constitutive resistance from acquired <em>rrl</em> 23S rRNA gene mutations, emerges after macrolide monotherapy or inadequate therapy and confers high-level resistance with no therapeutic role for macrolides.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Treatment of M. abscessus complex follows a two-phase framework supported by observational data and expert guidelines rather than randomized trials. The intensive phase, lasting 4 to 8 weeks, combines IV amikacin (15 mg/kg daily with therapeutic drug monitoring targeting peak levels of 35 to 45 mcg/mL), IV cefoxitin (up to 12 g/day) or imipenem-cilastatin, and IV tigecycline when tolerated. The continuation phase for subsp. abscessus with macrolide resistance centers on inhaled liposomal amikacin (ARIKAYCE), which delivers high local drug concentrations in the airways, combined with oral clofazimine and linezolid. Prolonged linezolid therapy requires vigilant monitoring for myelosuppression and neuropathy. Surgical resection of localized pulmonary cavities, when feasible, reduces bacterial burden and has been associated with improved outcomes in selected patients. Phage therapy has been reported in compassionate use cases of disseminated M. abscessus after lung transplantation for CF, but it remains experimental and is not a standard option.
  </p>
</div>
