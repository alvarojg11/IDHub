<div className="mx-auto max-w-5xl">
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    A 60-year-old man with type 2 diabetes mellitus and obesity presents to dermatology
    for evaluation of a flesh-colored pedunculated lesion on his right proximal thigh.
    During the examination, the clinician also notices bilateral groin plaques that the
    patient had not considered important.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    The plaques are well-demarcated and brown-red with fine peripheral scale and central
    hypopigmentation along both inguinal folds. They are minimally symptomatic, with only
    occasional mild pruritus. He has no fever, no satellite pustules, and no involvement
    of the scrotum.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    He reports chronic sweating and spends long hours working outdoors. He has tried an
    over-the-counter antifungal powder intermittently without clear improvement. The rest
    of the skin examination is unremarkable.
  </p>
</div>

<h2 className="text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900 mb-6">
  Rationale
</h2>

<div className="mx-auto max-w-5xl">
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    This is a classic intertriginous rash in a high-risk host. Erythrasma is a superficial
    bacterial infection of the stratum corneum caused most often by <em>Corynebacterium
    minutissimum</em>. It favors warm, moist skin folds, especially the inguinal, axillary,
    and interdigital regions.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Risk factors include diabetes, obesity, advancing age, hyperhidrosis, and chronic skin
    occlusion. The lesions are often asymptomatic or only mildly pruritic, making them easy
    to overlook unless a deliberate skin-fold examination is performed.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    The highest-yield bedside diagnostic clue is bright coral-pink fluorescence on Wood lamp
    examination. This fluorescence results from porphyrins, especially coproporphyrin III,
    produced by the organism. In contrast, candidiasis and most dermatophyte infections are
    diagnosed by potassium hydroxide preparation rather than this characteristic Wood lamp finding.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    The differential diagnosis in the groin includes candidal intertrigo, tinea cruris,
    pityriasis versicolor, and terra firma-forme dermatosis. The pattern here, coupled with
    the expected Wood lamp finding, makes erythrasma the best fit.
  </p>
</div>
