<h2 className="text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900 mb-6">
  Rationale
</h2>

<div className="mx-auto max-w-5xl">
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Coagulase-negative staphylococci, especially Staphylococcus epidermidis, are a leading cause of prosthetic valve endocarditis and should be treated as real pathogens when persistent across multiple separate blood culture sets. These organisms are common skin contaminants but frequently cause device-related infections due to their ability to form biofilms on prosthetic materials.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Diagnosis requires careful clinical judgment. The combination of timing (≥60 days after valve surgery), persistent bacteremia from multiple sets, and new clinical findings (murmur, conduction abnormality) strongly supports true infection rather than contamination. The negative coagulase test distinguishes coagulase-negative staphylococci from S. aureus.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Imaging is critical for evaluation. While transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) is often the initial test, it has limited sensitivity for prosthetic valve complications due to acoustic shadowing. Transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) provides superior visualization and is the imaging standard for prosthetic valve endocarditis, detecting vegetations, abscesses, pseudoaneurysms, and dehiscence with high sensitivity.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Surgical management follows AHA guidelines. Class I indications for surgery in prosthetic valve endocarditis include: perivalvular abscess, persistent infection despite appropriate antibiotic therapy, heart failure, and severe valve dysfunction. The new first-degree AV block in this case suggests perivalvular extension, which along with the prosthetic valve infection creates a strong case for surgical evaluation.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Treatment requires combination therapy for at least 6 weeks. The backbone includes an antistaphylococcal agent (vancomycin for resistant isolates, nafcillin only if MSSA) plus rifampin for biofilm penetration. Rifampin is started only after bacteremia clears (typically 3 to 5 days) to avoid selecting resistance. Aminoglycoside addition is guideline-variable: 2015 AHA suggests gentamicin for 2 weeks, but recent reviews no longer routinely recommend it due to unclear benefit and nephrotoxicity.
  </p>
</div>
