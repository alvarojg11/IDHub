<div className="mx-auto max-w-5xl">
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    A 24-year-old previously healthy man presents with 9 days of high fevers, profound
    fatigue, headache, diffuse myalgias, and progressive dyspnea on exertion. He reports
    dark urine for 3 days and near-syncope when climbing stairs. He returned 3 weeks ago
    from a rural valley in the Peruvian Andes, where he stayed in adobe housing without
    window screens and reported frequent nighttime insect bites. He did not take malaria
    prophylaxis and has no known chronic immunocompromising condition.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Physical exam shows an ill-appearing, markedly pale patient with mild scleral icterus.
    Vital signs are temperature 39.4 C, blood pressure 96/58 mmHg, heart rate 126/min,
    respiratory rate 24/min, and oxygen saturation 95% on room air. He has conjunctival
    pallor, a flow systolic murmur, and mild right upper quadrant tenderness. There is no
    nuchal rigidity, no focal neurologic deficit, and no rash at initial presentation.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Initial laboratories show severe anemia (hemoglobin 6.1 g/dL), thrombocytopenia,
    indirect hyperbilirubinemia, elevated LDH, and low haptoglobin, consistent with active
    hemolysis. A peripheral blood smear demonstrates pleomorphic bacillary forms associated
    with erythrocytes, and early blood cultures are unrevealing.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    He is treated and initially improves clinically, but several weeks later develops crops
    of friable red-violaceous papules and nodules on the extremities that bleed with minor
    trauma, consistent with the eruptive phase of disease.
  </p>
</div>

<h2 className="text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900 mb-6">
  Rationale
</h2>

<div className="mx-auto max-w-5xl">
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Carrion's disease is caused by <em>Bartonella bacilliformis</em>, a fastidious
    gram-negative bacillus transmitted by sand flies (genus <em>Lutzomyia</em>).
    Endemic transmission occurs in Andean valleys of Peru, Ecuador, and Colombia,
    generally at mid-altitude elevations.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Transmission is classically stercorarian: infected sand flies feed, then
    Bartonella-containing vector feces are inoculated into nonintact skin at or near
    the bite site (often through scratching). Humans are the main reservoir for
    <em> B. bacilliformis</em>; bacteremic individuals (symptomatic or asymptomatic)
    infect new sand flies, which then transmit infection to other humans. Unlike many
    other zoonotic Bartonella species, a consistent nonhuman vertebrate reservoir has
    not been established for this organism.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    The illness is classically biphasic. The acute bacteremic phase (Oroya fever)
    presents with high fever, profound hemolytic anemia, and potentially high fatality
    without timely treatment. Survivors can later develop the eruptive phase
    (verruga peruana), characterized by vascular papules or nodules that may bleed.
    Although this later phase is often less immediately life-threatening than acute
    Oroya fever, it is still active infection and is generally treated rather than
    observed alone, especially when lesions are multiple, friable, or bleeding.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Diagnosis in the acute phase can be supported by peripheral blood smear in endemic
    settings, while PCR can improve detection when smear is negative. Clinical history
    (Andean exposure, vector risk, and biphasic syndrome) is critical because culture
    is difficult and often delayed.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    For severe acute disease, references describe prompt systemic treatment, commonly
    ciprofloxacin-based regimens (often 14 days), with ceftriaxone added when illness
    is severe or persistent. Because fluoroquinolone resistance has been reported,
    close clinical follow-up and awareness of alternative regimens are important.
    For verruga peruana, azithromycin is commonly used (often 7 to 14 days), with
    rifampin as an alternative in selected cases, depending on context and local practice.
  </p>
</div>

  Teaching points

  <ul className="list-disc pl-6 text-lg leading-7 text-gray-800 space-y-2">
    <li>
      Think of Carrion's disease in patients with hemolytic fever after travel to endemic Andean regions.
    </li>
    <li>
      Oroya fever and verruga peruana represent different phases of the same infection.
    </li>
    <li>
      Verruga peruana usually still warrants antimicrobial treatment, not observation alone, especially if lesions are extensive or bleeding.
    </li>
    <li>
      Severe acute disease requires urgent systemic treatment due to significant mortality if untreated.
    </li>
    <li>
      Blood smear can be useful in acute disease; PCR improves detection when smear is negative.
    </li>
    <li>
      Reported fluoroquinolone resistance means follow-up and regimen reassessment are essential.
