  Rationale

<div className="mx-auto max-w-5xl">
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    <em>Enterobius vermicularis</em> (pinworm, threadworm, or oxyuris) is the most common helminth infection in the United States and other developed countries, with an estimated global prevalence of over 1 billion infections. It is particularly prevalent among school-aged children, their household contacts, and institutionalized populations. The adult worms inhabit the cecum, appendix, and adjacent portions of the ascending colon and ileum, where they feed on intestinal contents and superficial mucosal secretions.
  </p>
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    The life cycle of <em>E. vermicularis</em> is relatively simple but contains a critical feature that drives both its extraordinary transmissibility and its tendency to cause nocturnal perianal pruritus. After ingestion of embryonated eggs via the fecal-oral route, the larvae hatch in the duodenum, molt twice as they migrate through the small intestine, and mature into adults in the cecum and appendix. Gravid females, laden with up to 10,000 to 15,000 eggs, migrate out of the rectum at night — typically 1 to 2 hours after the host falls asleep — and crawl onto the perianal and perineal skin, where they deposit their eggs. This nocturnal migration is the direct cause of perianal pruritus, which is the hallmark symptom of enterobiasis and the reason the patient's daughter has been scratching at night.
  </p>
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    The eggs are not passed in stool but remain on the perianal skin, where they embryonate and become infectious within 4 to 6 hours. This extremely rapid maturation is another key feature that distinguishes <em>Enterobius</em> from other intestinal nematodes, most of which require days to weeks for eggs to become infectious. The rapid embryonation means that hand-to-mouth transmission can occur within hours of egg deposition, facilitating efficient person-to-person spread, particularly among children who scratch the perianal area and then touch shared objects, food, or other children.
  </p>
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    The eggs are characteristically elongated-oval (50 to 60 by 20 to 30 micrometers), flattened on one side (giving them a D-shaped or bean-shaped appearance), and contain a partially developed larva visible within the thin, double-layered shell. On histopathology, adult worms in cross-section show a thin eosinophilic cuticle with two prominent lateral alae — wing-like extensions of the cuticle on either side of the body that are pathognomonic for <em>E. vermicularis</em> among human intestinal nematodes. The lateral alae are visible at low to medium power on H&E staining and serve as the key morphologic identifier.
  </p>
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    The association between <em>E. vermicularis</em> and appendicitis has been debated in the surgical literature for over a century. Large retrospective studies of appendectomy specimens have found <em>Enterobius</em> in 0.2 to 8.7 percent of appendices, with variable rates of concomitant acute inflammation. The relationship is likely multifactorial: the worms can obstruct the appendiceal lumen, their presence triggers a local eosinophilic inflammatory response, and their cuticular antigens may act as luminal irritants that provoke mucosal ulceration and secondary bacterial invasion. Whether the parasite is a true cause of appendicitis or an innocent bystander found incidentally in the appendix remains controversial, but cases with dense eosinophilic infiltrate centered on the parasite — as in this patient — are generally accepted as causally related.
  </p>
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    The marked tissue eosinophilia seen in this case reflects the Th2-dominant immune response that characterizes helminth infection. CD4+ T helper 2 cells, activated by helminth antigens presented in the context of the GATA-3 transcription factor program, produce IL-4, IL-5, and IL-13. IL-5 is the master regulator of eosinophil biology: it drives eosinophilopoiesis in the bone marrow, promotes eosinophil release into the circulation, and acts synergistically with the chemokine eotaxin (CCL11) to recruit eosinophils to tissues where helminth antigens are present. IL-5 also prolongs eosinophil survival by inhibiting apoptosis. The eosinophil is the principal effector cell in anti-helminth immunity: its granules contain major basic protein (MBP), eosinophil cationic protein (ECP), eosinophil peroxidase (EPO), and eosinophil-derived neurotoxin (EDN), all of which can damage helminth surfaces when the eosinophil degranulates in direct contact with the parasite.
  </p>
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    The diagnostic gold standard for enterobiasis is the perianal cellophane tape preparation (Graham test), not stool ova and parasite examination. This is because the gravid female deposits eggs on the perianal skin, not in the intestinal lumen, and the eggs are therefore found on the skin surface rather than in the stool. A single tape test has approximately 50 percent sensitivity, but this rises to approximately 90 percent with three consecutive morning specimens. The test must be performed in the morning before bathing or toileting, as these activities remove eggs from the perianal area.
  </p>
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Treatment requires mebendazole 100 mg as a single oral dose, repeated at 2 weeks, administered to the patient and all household contacts simultaneously. The repeat dose is necessary because of retroinfection: eggs deposited on the perianal skin before treatment can hatch, and the larvae migrate back through the anus into the rectum, maturing into adult worms that are not killed by the initial dose. Mebendazole works by inhibiting nematode beta-tubulin polymerization, blocking microtubule-dependent glucose uptake and leading to parasite death. It is poorly absorbed from the gastrointestinal tract, which is advantageous for a luminal parasite like <em>Enterobius</em> — the drug remains in the intestinal lumen at high concentrations where the worms reside.
  </p>

      Lateral alae on cross-section are pathognomonic for <em>E. vermicularis</em> among intestinal nematodes — no other common human helminth has this feature.
    </li>
    <li>
      The Th2/IL-5 axis (not IgE/mast cells) is the primary driver of tissue eosinophilia in helminth infection — IL-5 drives eosinophilopoiesis, and eotaxin (CCL11) recruits eosinophils to the tissue site.
    </li>
    <li>
      The Scotch tape test (not stool O&P) is the diagnostic standard because gravid females deposit eggs on the perianal skin at night — eggs are rarely passed in stool.
    </li>
    <li>
      The second dose at 2 weeks targets retroinfection: eggs on the perianal skin hatch, and larvae migrate back into the rectum to complete maturation.
    </li>
    <li>
      ALL household contacts must be treated simultaneously because of extreme contagiousness, rapid egg maturation (4-6 hours), and frequent asymptomatic adult carriage.
    </li>
  </ul>
</div>
