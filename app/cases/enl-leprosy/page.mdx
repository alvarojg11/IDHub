<div className="mx-auto max-w-5xl">
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    A 36-year-old man from the Peruvian Amazon presents for evaluation of a new febrile illness several months after completing treatment for multibacillary Hansen disease. His original diagnosis had evolved over more than a year: he developed numerous annular and infiltrated plaques on the trunk and extremities, diffuse thickening of the earlobes and eyebrows, palpably thickened ulnar and posterior tibial nerves, and glove-and-stocking hypoesthesia with intermittent burning pain in both feet. A slit-skin smear showed a bacteriological index of 4.5, and skin biopsy demonstrated a diffuse dermal infiltrate of foamy macrophages packed with acid-fast bacilli. He completed 12 months of multidrug therapy with monthly rifampin, monthly clofazimine, daily clofazimine, and daily dapsone under supervision. During treatment, the infiltrated plaques flattened progressively and his sensory symptoms stabilized.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    At the end of treatment, a repeat slit-skin smear returned with a bacteriological index of 3 and a very low morphological index. The treating team counseled him that bacillary antigen persists and clears slowly in highly bacilliferous disease, and that a positive smear without rising index or solid-staining bacilli does not constitute relapse. He was discharged from supervised treatment feeling clinically improved, with residual hyperpigmented plaques but without new lesions.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Several months later, following a gastrointestinal illness and a period of emotional stress, he develops abrupt onset fever to 38.8°C, diffuse myalgias, bilateral ankle edema, testicular pain diagnosed clinically as epididymo-orchitis, and new crops of exquisitely tender erythematous nodules on the face, extensor surfaces of the arms, and trunk. Several nodules become pustular and ulcerate. He reports worsening tenderness over both ulnar nerves but denies new weakness in the hands, foot drop, or new areas of numbness. The old plaques remain flat rather than newly infiltrated. Motor testing of the small muscles of the hands and feet is preserved bilaterally.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Laboratory studies show neutrophilic leukocytosis, mild normocytic anemia, and elevated C-reactive protein. Creatinine is normal, urinalysis is bland, complement levels are normal, and ANCA is negative. Repeat slit-skin smear shows a bacteriological index of 3, unchanged from the end of treatment, with a persistently low morphological index. He also reports a history of intermittent abdominal cramping and loose stools over the past year. Prednisolone 60 mg/day is started. After 48 hours, fever and nerve pain improve substantially, but he develops refractory hyperglycemia requiring an insulin infusion. Thalidomide becomes available.
  </p>

<h2 className="text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900 mb-6">
  Rationale
</h2>

<div className="mx-auto max-w-5xl">
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    ENL is not a failure of multidrug therapy. It is an inflammatory complication driven by antigen persistence and dysregulated host immunity in multibacillary leprosy, particularly at the lepromatous and borderline lepromatous poles of the Ridley-Jopling spectrum. The pathophysiology involves immune complex deposition, neutrophil recruitment, complement activation, and cytokine release, producing painful dermal nodules with systemic features including fever, neuritis, edema, orchitis, lymphadenitis, arthritis, and nephritis. ENL can occur before, during, and after MDT precisely because effective antimicrobial therapy reduces the viable bacterial load without immediately eliminating the reservoir of bacillary antigen, which continues to drive immune activation in a previously sensitized host.
  </p>

  <figure className="my-8">
    <div className="mx-auto max-w-3xl rounded-lg border border-gray-300 bg-white p-2">
      <Image
        src="/cases/enl-leprosy/ridley-jopling.jpg"
        alt="Ridley-Jopling leprosy spectrum from tuberculoid to lepromatous poles showing clinical subtypes"
        width={708}
        height={385}
        className="rounded-lg mx-auto w-full object-contain"
      />
    </div>
    <figcaption className="mt-3 text-center text-sm text-gray-600">
      The Ridley-Jopling spectrum: from tuberculoid (high cell-mediated immunity, few lesions, low bacillary load) to lepromatous (low cell-mediated immunity, diffuse infiltration, high bacillary load). Borderline lepromatous disease lies near the lepromatous pole and carries the highest risk for type 2 reactions. Image adapted from Dubey et al., Frontiers in Pharmacology (2024), CC BY 4.0.
    </figcaption>
  </figure>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Distinguishing ENL from relapse matters because the treatment pathways diverge completely. ENL requires immunomodulation; relapse requires extended antimicrobial therapy and resistance testing. The diagnostic criteria for relapse include new or expanding leprosy skin lesions, a rising bacteriological index, solid-staining bacilli or a positive morphological index indicating viable replicating organisms, and in the modern era, molecular detection of mutations in rpoB (rifampin resistance), folP1 (dapsone resistance), or gyrA (fluoroquinolone resistance). Persistent smear positivity with a low morphological index and flat, non-expanding old plaques represents antigen persistence, not relapse. The strong distractors in this case, including ANCA vasculitis, panniculitic lymphoma, and Lucio phenomenon, are excluded by the negative ANCA, the leprosy-specific features (orchitis, neuritis, borderline lepromatous classification, high initial BI), and the pustular-to-ulcerative nodular morphology characteristic of severe ENL rather than necrotizing vasculopathy.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Severe ENL with active neuritis is a disability emergency. Untreated or inadequately treated neuritis in ENL progresses to irreversible axonal loss, producing claw hand, foot drop, and anesthetic extremities that are the most disabling long-term consequences of leprosy. The preserved motor function at presentation represents a critical treatment window: the priority is protecting the ulnar nerves over the next days to weeks. Corticosteroids remain the most reliably rapid therapy for acute neuritis, and their use is appropriate as a bridge; the problem arises when steroid-related toxicity makes prolonged use untenable. Thalidomide is highly effective for the systemic and cutaneous features of ENL and reduces steroid requirements, but its effect is not immediate and it requires at least several days to titrate. Before use, baseline neurological assessment and counseling on teratogenicity, thrombosis, and peripheral neuropathy are required. The structured bridge, from steroids to thalidomide, with daily nerve-function monitoring as the taper guide, is the evidence-based approach.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    The Strongyloides risk in this case is not incidental. High-dose corticosteroids in a patient from an endemic region with abdominal symptoms can precipitate hyperinfection, in which the autoinfection cycle accelerates massively and larvae disseminate to the lungs, central nervous system, and other organs, carrying enteric bacteria into the bloodstream and carrying mortality rates exceeding 60% in untreated disseminated disease. Screening or empiric treatment with ivermectin before or concurrent with prolonged high-dose steroids is an established harm-reduction strategy. For chronic Strongyloides infection, ivermectin 200 mcg/kg/day orally for 2 days is the standard regimen; hyperinfection or disseminated disease requires daily treatment until stool and sputum samples are negative for at least 2 consecutive weeks, combined with reduction of immunosuppression when feasible.
  </p>
</div>

  Teaching Points
