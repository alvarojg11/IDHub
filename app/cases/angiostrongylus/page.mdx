<h2 className="text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900 mb-6">
  Rationale
</h2>

<div className="mx-auto max-w-5xl">
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    <em>Angiostrongylus cantonensis</em>, the rat lungworm, is the most common cause
    of eosinophilic meningitis worldwide. Adult worms reside in the pulmonary arteries
    of rats; larvae are shed in rat feces and ingested by intermediate hosts — primarily
    snails and slugs. Humans are accidental dead-end hosts who acquire the infection
    by eating raw or undercooked snails, slugs, freshwater shrimp, crabs, or frogs, or
    by consuming produce contaminated with slug slime or larval-containing water. Slug
    slime on unwashed lettuce and garden herbs is an underappreciated and common
    transmission route that explains cases in patients who have no recollection of
    eating mollusks. The disease is endemic in Hawaii, the Pacific Islands, Southeast
    Asia, parts of the Caribbean, and increasingly in other tropical regions.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    After ingestion, third-stage larvae penetrate the gut wall, enter the circulation,
    and migrate to the central nervous system. In the human CNS — an accidental host
    — larvae cannot complete their development to adult worms. The inflammatory
    response to larvae and their antigens drives the clinical syndrome. The defining
    laboratory finding is eosinophilic pleocytosis in the CSF: more than 10 eosinophils
    per mm³ or more than 10% eosinophils constitutes eosinophilic meningitis by
    standard definition. Peripheral eosinophilia is present in the majority of cases.
    The clinical picture of subacute severe headache, neck stiffness, and migratory
    paresthesias of the trunk and extremities — caused by larval migration along
    peripheral nerves — in a patient with appropriate geographic exposure and CSF
    eosinophilia is essentially diagnostic.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    The most important treatment principle — and the most common source of clinical
    error — is that anthelminthic therapy is contraindicated. When albendazole or
    mebendazole kill larvae within the subarachnoid space, the resulting antigen
    release provokes an acute inflammatory cascade that dramatically worsens
    meningitis and can precipitate life-threatening intracranial hypertension. Because
    larvae in accidental human hosts die spontaneously over days to weeks, the disease
    is self-limited and anthelminthics offer no benefit while incurring substantial
    risk. A randomized controlled trial by Chotmongkol et al. demonstrated that
    prednisolone (60 mg/day tapered over 2 weeks) significantly reduces headache
    duration and severity compared to placebo, and this is the cornerstone of
    pharmacologic management. Serial therapeutic lumbar punctures are effective for
    symptomatic relief of elevated intracranial pressure.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    The differential diagnosis of eosinophilic meningitis is an important framework
    for any ID clinician. The two leading helminthic causes are
    <em>Angiostrongylus cantonensis</em> (contaminated produce or mollusks, Pacific/
    Southeast Asia) and <em>Gnathostoma spinigerum</em> (raw fish or meat, Southeast
    Asia) — distinguished primarily by geography and exposure history.
    <em>Baylisascaris procyonis</em> (raccoon exposure, children) causes the most
    severe helminthic eosinophilic meningoencephalitis in North America. Non-parasitic
    causes of CSF eosinophilia include coccidioidal meningitis, neurosarcoidosis,
    hypereosinophilic syndrome, and intrathecal drug administration. Among these,
    Coccidioides is the most important to exclude given its overlap with travel to
    endemic areas in the western hemisphere.
  </p>
</div>
