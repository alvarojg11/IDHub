<h2 className="text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900 mb-6">
  Rationale
</h2>

<div className="mx-auto max-w-5xl">
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Oropouche virus is an orthobunyavirus that has emerged from relative obscurity to
    become one of the most important causes of non-malarial febrile illness in the
    Amazon basin. Clinically it behaves like a master imitator. Patients often present
    with abrupt high fever, severe headache, retroorbital pain, myalgias, arthralgias,
    and sometimes a transient maculopapular rash. In the first pass, many of these
    patients are labeled as possible dengue, chikungunya, or Zika, which is exactly
    why Oropouche should enter the differential once the usual tests turn negative.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    The epidemiology is the memorable part. Unlike the better-known tropical
    arboviruses transmitted by mosquitoes, Oropouche virus is classically spread in
    urban cycles by biting midges, especially <em>Culicoides paraensis</em>. These
    insects are tiny enough that travelers may remember swarms, itching, or papules
    without clearly recalling being bitten. That detail matters because it helps the
    clinician move away from the default mosquito framework and toward a virus many
    otherwise would not consider.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Diagnosis depends on timing. During the acute phase, RT-PCR from serum is the most
    useful test when available, whereas serology is often retrospective and
    cross-reactivity with other arboviruses can complicate interpretation. In recent
    outbreaks, genomic surveillance has also clarified that Oropouche virus is not a
    static curiosity but a re-emerging pathogen with reassortant lineages capable of
    rapid spread across the Americas. For the bedside clinician, though, the key point
    is simpler: if a traveler returns from the Amazon with a dengue-like illness and
    negative dengue testing, keep going.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Treatment is supportive because no proven antiviral therapy exists. Most cases are
    self-limited, but the syndrome is still worth recognizing accurately for several
    reasons: it prevents unnecessary antimicrobial therapy, sharpens counseling about
    vector avoidance, and contributes to outbreak awareness and reporting. Oropouche
    fever is one of those infections that becomes easier to diagnose the moment it is
    remembered.
  </p>
</div>
