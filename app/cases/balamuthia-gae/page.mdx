<h2 className="text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900 mb-6">
  Rationale
</h2>

<div className="mx-auto max-w-5xl">
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    <em>Balamuthia mandrillaris</em> is a free-living ameba first isolated from the brain
    of a mandrill baboon in 1986 and now recognized as an important, if rare, cause of
    granulomatous amebic encephalitis (GAE) in humans. Unlike <em>Naegleria fowleri</em>,
    which causes a fulminant primary amebic meningoencephalitis, Balamuthia GAE follows
    a subacute to chronic course spanning weeks to months. Mortality exceeds 90% in
    untreated or late-diagnosed cases.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    A key epidemiologic distinction is that <em>Balamuthia</em> can cause disease in
    both immunocompromised and immunocompetent hosts — unlike <em>Acanthamoeba</em> GAE,
    which almost exclusively affects immunocompromised individuals. In the United States
    and Japan, the most common presentation is subacute meningoencephalitis without a
    preceding skin lesion. In South America and China, however, patients frequently
    develop cutaneous disease first — typically an asymptomatic or mildly erythematous,
    indurated plaque on the central face or extremities — which may precede CNS
    involvement by weeks to months. This preceding skin lesion is a critical and
    often-missed diagnostic clue.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    An important clinical trap is the administration of corticosteroids to the skin
    lesion before the diagnosis is established. Topical and especially systemic
    corticosteroids can cause rapid expansion of cutaneous Balamuthia lesions and
    may accelerate CNS dissemination. In this case, the topical corticosteroid
    prescribed for presumed rosacea likely contributed to plaque expansion.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Neuroimaging in Balamuthia GAE typically reveals multiple ring-enhancing lesions
    with surrounding edema, often in the posterior fossa or cerebral hemispheres, that
    can closely mimic toxoplasma encephalitis, primary CNS lymphoma, or metastatic
    disease. CSF analysis usually shows a lymphocytic pleocytosis with elevated protein
    and normal or mildly depressed glucose; however, CSF may be normal early in the
    disease. Definitive diagnosis requires brain biopsy with histopathologic examination
    and specific immunostaining, as <em>Balamuthia</em> trophozoites are
    morphologically indistinguishable from <em>Acanthamoeba</em> spp. on H&amp;E alone.
    The CDC Division of Parasitic Diseases can confirm the diagnosis and facilitate
    access to treatment.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    No randomized controlled trials exist for Balamuthia GAE, and treatment is guided
    entirely by case reports and retrospective series. Miltefosine, an alkylphosphocholine
    with potent in vitro amebicidal activity, has emerged as the most important agent
    in successful treatment regimens. Most survivors received combination therapy
    incorporating miltefosine alongside flucytosine, azoles (fluconazole, voriconazole,
    or itraconazole), pentamidine, or sulfadiazine. Surgical debulking of accessible
    lesions has been a component of successful outcomes in several published cases.
    Early diagnosis — before significant neurologic deterioration — appears to be the
    strongest predictor of survival.
  </p>
</div>

<h2 className="text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900 mb-6">
  Teaching points
</h2>

<div className="mx-auto max-w-5xl">
  <ul className="list-disc pl-6 text-lg leading-7 text-gray-800 space-y-2">
    <li>
      <em>Balamuthia mandrillaris</em> GAE is a subacute to chronic granulomatous
      encephalitis that — unlike Acanthamoeba GAE — can affect both immunocompromised
      and immunocompetent hosts, including children.
    </li>
    <li>
      A preceding indolent, non-ulcerated cutaneous plaque on the central face or
      extremities is a key diagnostic clue in Latin American and Asian patients;
      this skin lesion typically heralds eventual CNS involvement if untreated.
    </li>
    <li>
      Corticosteroids (topical or systemic) can cause rapid expansion of cutaneous
      Balamuthia lesions and should be avoided once the diagnosis is suspected.
    </li>
    <li>
      On H&amp;E staining, <em>Balamuthia</em> trophozoites cannot be reliably
      distinguished from <em>Acanthamoeba</em> spp.; specific immunostaining or
      PCR is required to confirm the species.
    </li>
    <li>
      Treatment requires combination antimicrobial therapy; miltefosine is now
      considered a cornerstone agent, typically combined with flucytosine, an azole,
      and/or pentamidine. Surgical debulking of accessible lesions may improve outcomes.
    </li>
    <li>
      The CDC Emergency Operations Center can assist with diagnosis confirmation and
      access to miltefosine in suspected cases.
    </li>
    <li>
      Mortality exceeds 90% without early recognition and treatment; prognosis is
      directly tied to the speed of diagnosis.
    </li>
  </ul>
</div>
