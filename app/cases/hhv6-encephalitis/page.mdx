<h2 className="text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900 mb-6">
  Rationale
</h2>

<div className="mx-auto max-w-5xl">
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Human herpesvirus 6B (HHV-6B) is a betaherpesvirus that infects nearly all
    humans in early childhood, establishing latency predominantly in CD4-positive
    T lymphocytes, monocytes, and neural progenitor cells. Like all herpesviruses,
    HHV-6B can reactivate under conditions of immunosuppression. In the setting of
    allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation, HHV-6B reactivation occurs
    in 30 to 70% of recipients, typically between day 20 and day 60 post-transplant:
    the window of maximal immunosuppression during immune reconstitution. Encephalitis
    develops in a subset, with limbic structures (hippocampus, amygdala,
    parahippocampal gyrus) being preferentially targeted because of the reservoir
    of latent virus in neural progenitor cells.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    The clinical syndrome of HHV-6 limbic encephalitis is distinctive. The hallmark
    is subacute anterograde amnesia: patients lose the ability to form new memories
    while remote memory is initially preserved, producing the disorientation and
    repeated questioning observed here. Seizures are common and often have temporal
    lobe semiology. SIADH, caused by hypothalamic involvement, is a characteristic
    associated finding that should raise suspicion for HHV-6 in the post-transplant
    patient with unexplained hyponatremia and encephalopathy. MRI reveals bilateral,
    often asymmetric, T2/FLAIR hyperintensity in the mesial temporal lobes without
    significant gadolinium enhancement: a pattern indistinguishable radiographically
    from HSV encephalitis and autoimmune limbic encephalitis, underscoring the
    importance of CSF PCR for diagnosis.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    The ciHHV-6 pitfall is one of the most important diagnostic considerations in
    HHV-6 encephalitis. Approximately 1% of the general population harbors HHV-6
    integrated into the telomeric region of a germline chromosome. Every nucleated
    cell in these individuals contains one integrated HHV-6 genome, producing whole
    blood viral loads typically exceeding 5 to 10 million copies/mL: a level that
    would indicate life-threatening viral encephalitis if it reflected true
    replication. CSF HHV-6 PCR resolves this ambiguity: cerebrospinal fluid contains
    few nucleated cells, so ciHHV-6 carriers have absent or very low HHV-6 DNA in
    CSF, while patients with true HHV-6 encephalitis have detectable and often high
    viral loads reflecting active CNS replication. In equivocal cases, detection of
    HHV-6 in hair follicles (which contain nucleated cells from a single individual)
    can confirm ciHHV-6 when viral load equals whole-blood levels.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Antiviral therapy with ganciclovir or foscarnet is the standard of care. Neither
    acyclovir nor valacyclovir has meaningful activity against HHV-6: the virus
    lacks a thymidine kinase homolog capable of activating acyclovir to its
    triphosphate form. Ganciclovir is phosphorylated by the HHV-6 UL97 homolog
    (a viral phosphotransferase) and then by cellular kinases to its active
    triphosphate, which inhibits the viral DNA polymerase. Foscarnet acts directly
    on the viral DNA polymerase without requiring prior phosphorylation, making it
    active regardless of viral kinase status. Myelosuppression from ganciclovir
    is the principal concern in post-BMT patients, and complete blood count must be
    monitored closely. Foscarnet requires careful attention to renal function and
    electrolytes, including magnesium, phosphate, and calcium. Reduction of
    immunosuppression, when feasible given the GvHD risk, is an essential adjunct
    to antiviral therapy.
  </p>
</div>
