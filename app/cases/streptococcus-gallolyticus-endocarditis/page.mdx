<h2 className="text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900 mb-6">
  Rationale
</h2>

<div className="mx-auto max-w-5xl">
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    The <em>Streptococcus bovis</em>/<em>Streptococcus equinus</em> group has
    undergone substantial taxonomic revision over the past two decades.
    The landmark reclassification by Schlegel and colleagues in 2003 renamed the
    former <em>S. bovis</em> biotype I as <em>Streptococcus gallolyticus</em>
    subsp. <em>gallolyticus</em>, while biotype II was split into
    <em> S. infantarius</em> subsp. <em>infantarius</em> (formerly II/1) and
    <em> S. gallolyticus</em> subsp. <em>pasteurianus</em> and
    <em> S. infantarius</em> subsp. <em>coli</em> (formerly II/2). The group
    now includes <em>S. gallolyticus</em> (with subspecies
    <em>gallolyticus</em>, <em>macedonicus</em>, and <em>pasteurianus</em>),
    <em>S. infantarius</em>, <em>S. alactolyticus</em>,
    <em>S. equinus</em>, and <em>S. lutetiensis</em>. This taxonomic
    restructuring was not merely academic: the clinical significance of these
    organisms varies by subspecies, and <em>S. gallolyticus</em> subsp.
    <em>gallolyticus</em> carries the strongest association with colorectal
    neoplasia by a wide margin.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    The microbiologic identification of the <em>S. bovis</em>/<em>equinus</em>
    group rests on a distinctive biochemical profile that distinguishes it from
    other streptococci and enterococci. The organism is alpha-hemolytic on sheep
    blood agar (though some strains may be gamma-hemolytic), catalase-negative,
    bile esculin-positive, and PYR-negative. Bile esculin positivity — the
    ability to grow in the presence of 40% bile and hydrolyze esculin to
    esculetin — is the hallmark feature that separates the <em>S. bovis</em>
    group from viridans streptococci, which are bile esculin-negative. The
    PYR-negative result further distinguishes the <em>S. bovis</em> group from
    <em>Enterococcus</em> species, which are both bile esculin-positive and
    PYR-positive. Modern identification relies on MALDI-TOF mass spectrometry
    or molecular methods, which can provide subspecies-level identification —
    clinically important because the colorectal neoplasia risk varies
    significantly by subspecies.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    The association between <em>S. gallolyticus</em> subsp.
    <em>gallolyticus</em> bacteremia and colorectal neoplasia was first
    recognized in the 1950s and has since been confirmed in dozens of studies
    across multiple continents. The prevalence of colorectal adenoma or
    carcinoma in patients with <em>S. gallolyticus</em> subsp.
    <em>gallolyticus</em> bacteremia ranges from 47 to 71%, depending on the
    study population and the thoroughness of colonic evaluation. The mechanism
    underlying this association is not carcinogenic but adhesive and ecological:
    <em>S. gallolyticus</em> subsp. <em>gallolyticus</em> expresses
    specialized pilus proteins — particularly the Pil1 pilus composed of
    Gallo2179 and Gallo2178 subunits, along with Pil2 and Pil3 variants — that
    mediate specific adhesion to extracellular matrix proteins and tumor-associated
    glycans present on the surface of adenomatous and carcinomatous colonic
    tissue. These pili recognize mucins and cell surface antigens that are
    overexpressed during neoplastic transformation. The tumor provides a
    privileged niche: the disrupted epithelial barrier facilitates bacterial
    invasion and translocation into the portal and systemic circulation. This is
    a species-specific interaction — other members of the <em>S. bovis</em>
    group that lack these pilus variants do not show the same strength of CRC
    association.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Endocarditis is the most common invasive manifestation of <em>S.
    gallolyticus</em> infection, though bacteremia without endocarditis, liver
    abscess, spinal epidural abscess, and meningitis have all been reported. The
    organism exhibits a tropism for damaged or prosthetic cardiac valves that is
    shared with other streptococcal species. Treatment follows standard
    guidelines for streptococcal endocarditis: intravenous penicillin G or
    ceftriaxone for 4 to 6 weeks, with ceftriaxone often preferred for its
    convenient once-daily dosing. The organism is almost universally susceptible
    to beta-lactams, and resistance is rare. Vancomycin is reserved for patients
    with severe beta-lactam allergy. The AHA/IDSA guidelines for infective
    endocarditis management apply directly, and the addition of an aminoglycoside
    for synergy is optional for fully susceptible streptococci with low MICs.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    The management imperative that extends beyond antibiotics and valve treatment
    is colonoscopy. Every patient with <em>S. gallolyticus</em> subsp.
    <em>gallolyticus</em> bacteremia — whether or not endocarditis is present,
    whether or not gastrointestinal symptoms are reported, and regardless of
    prior screening history — should undergo colonoscopic evaluation. The
    colonoscopy should be performed during the index admission or shortly after
    discharge, not deferred to a later outpatient follow-up where it risks being
    forgotten. FIT testing is insufficient, as it misses a substantial fraction
    of adenomas and early carcinomas. The blood culture in this context is not
    merely a microbiologic result — it is a sentinel event that should trigger
    a focused search for colorectal neoplasia with the same urgency that a
    positive screening mammogram triggers a diagnostic workup for breast cancer.
  </p>
</div>
