<h2 className="text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900 mb-6">
  Rationale
</h2>

<div className="mx-auto max-w-5xl">
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Toxocara visceral larva migrans is the correct diagnosis, but arriving there requires working through a genuinely competitive differential built by the travel history. Bolivia introduces Fasciola hepatica as a compelling alternative: the altiplano has some of the world's highest human fasciolasis rates, and early-phase fasciolasis produces poorly defined hepatic parenchymal lesions with eosinophilia that closely resemble VLM on CT. The distinction rests on the absence of aquatic plant consumption, a normal alkaline phosphatase (established fasciolasis causes biliary disease and ALP elevation), and three negative stool O&P samples (patent fasciolasis sheds eggs). Vietnam creates concern for Clonorchis sinensis, but clonorchiasis requires raw freshwater fish ingestion and produces biliary rather than parenchymal lesions. Strongyloides is less likely given negative serology and the absence of immunosuppression. Tropical pulmonary eosinophilia overlaps on the pulmonary and eosinophilic axes but does not cause focal hepatic lesions. Having worked through the travel exposures, the dog rescue shelter emerges as the operative history: Toxocara-contaminated soil is the source, stool O&P is always negative, and Toxocara IgG ELISA is the diagnostic test.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    <em>Toxocara canis</em> is the roundworm of domestic dogs; <em>T. cati</em> from cats causes an identical syndrome. Dogs, particularly puppies, shed enormous numbers of eggs in their feces. Eggs require 2 to 4 weeks of environmental embryonation to become infective; once embryonated they can survive in moist soil for months to years. Humans ingest infective eggs through contact with contaminated soil (pica, unwashed hands, unwashed vegetables), or, rarely, through consumption of raw meat from paratenic hosts (rabbits, chickens). After hatching in the human small intestine, second-stage larvae penetrate the intestinal wall, enter the portal circulation, and reach the liver within hours. In the definitive canine host, larvae continue their migration through the lungs, ascend the trachea, are swallowed, and mature to adult worms in the small intestine where they shed eggs. In humans, this developmental progression cannot occur. The larvae wander through hepatic parenchyma, lungs, skeletal muscle, and occasionally the central nervous system and eye, provoking a Th2-skewed eosinophilic granulomatous response at every site. The clinical result is visceral larva migrans: eosinophilia, elevated IgE, hepatic lesions, pulmonary infiltrates, and urticaria reflecting the systemic Th2 milieu.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Diagnosis of VLM relies on serology because direct detection of larvae in stool is impossible: humans never shed eggs. Toxocara IgG ELISA, using larval excretory-secretory antigens, has a sensitivity of approximately 78% and specificity of 90% for VLM. Peripheral eosinophilia above 1,500/uL and markedly elevated serum IgE support the diagnosis. CT findings of multiple small poorly defined hypodense parenchymal hepatic lesions are characteristic. Tissue biopsy showing an eosinophilic granuloma containing a larva is definitive but is rarely necessary.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Albendazole 400 mg twice daily for 5 days is the standard first-line treatment, inhibiting larval tubulin polymerization and impairing motility and glucose uptake. Corticosteroids are co-administered in symptomatic or severe disease to blunt the inflammatory reaction to dying larvae, which can transiently worsen symptoms. Mebendazole is an alternative but achieves lower systemic concentrations for tissue-invasive larvae. Ocular larva migrans, a distinct syndrome in which a single larva reaches the retina and causes a focal granuloma with the risk of permanent vision loss, may require periocular steroid injection; the role of albendazole in established ocular disease is uncertain, and treatment should be coordinated with ophthalmology.
  </p>
</div>
