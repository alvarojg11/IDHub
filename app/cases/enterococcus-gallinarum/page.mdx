<h2 className="text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900 mb-6">
  Rationale
</h2>

<div className="mx-auto max-w-5xl">
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    <em>Enterococcus gallinarum</em> is a motile enterococcal species that carries the VanC
    resistance gene cluster as a constitutive, chromosomally encoded trait. Unlike
    <em>E. faecalis</em> and <em>E. faecium</em> — which are the dominant clinical
    enterococcal pathogens — <em>E. gallinarum</em>, <em>E. casseliflavus</em>, and
    <em>E. flavescens</em> are intrinsically vancomycin-resistant at the species level.
    This resistance is not acquired through antibiotic selective pressure or horizontal
    gene transfer, and it carries fundamentally different clinical and epidemiological
    implications than the acquired VanA and VanB resistance mechanisms responsible for
    the global VRE problem.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    The molecular basis of VanC resistance lies in the substitution of the terminal
    D-alanine in peptidoglycan precursors with D-serine, producing D-Ala-D-Ser dipeptides
    that bind vancomycin with approximately 100-fold lower affinity than the standard
    D-Ala-D-Ala target. This results in low-level vancomycin resistance (MIC typically
    2–32 &#956;g/mL) without cross-resistance to teicoplanin, another glycopeptide that
    binds a different site on the acyl-D-Ala-D-Ala substrate. Teicoplanin susceptibility
    is therefore a useful phenotypic discriminator: VanA strains are resistant to both
    vancomycin and teicoplanin, VanB strains are vancomycin-resistant but teicoplanin-
    susceptible (as is VanC), but species identification by MALDI-TOF or biochemical
    testing is the definitive method to distinguish VanB from VanC when both are
    teicoplanin-susceptible.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    The clinical significance of <em>E. gallinarum</em> bacteremia is increasingly
    recognized in immunocompromised patients, those with structural gastrointestinal
    disease, and patients with cirrhosis. The dysfunctional gut barrier and altered
    mucosal immunity of advanced liver disease create conditions for enterococcal
    translocation across the intestinal mucosa into the portal and systemic circulation.
    Liver disease is among the most consistently identified risk factors for
    <em>E. gallinarum</em> bacteremia. Identifying and treating an occult source —
    such as biliary disease, primary peritonitis, or gastrointestinal mucosal injury —
    is as important as selecting the correct antibiotic.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    A critical pitfall in the management of <em>E. gallinarum</em> is over-escalation of
    antibiotics triggered by the automated "VRE" flag in susceptibility reporting systems.
    Because <em>E. gallinarum</em> is far less common than <em>E. faecalis</em> or
    <em>E. faecium</em> in clinical microbiology laboratories, many automated reporting
    systems are not configured to distinguish VanC from acquired vancomycin resistance,
    leading to inappropriate initiation of linezolid or daptomycin when ampicillin
    would be curative. Recognition of this reporting artifact, and the habit of always
    confirming species identity alongside susceptibility results, is an essential
    clinical skill.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    All enterococcal bacteremias warrant echocardiography to evaluate for endocarditis,
    given the predilection of <em>Enterococcus</em> species for damaged or prosthetic
    cardiac valves. In patients with cirrhosis, underlying valvulopathy may be present
    and repeat blood cultures should confirm clearance. Uncomplicated <em>E. gallinarum</em>
    bacteremia without endocarditis is treated with ampicillin for 14 days; confirmed
    endocarditis requires 4 to 6 weeks of combination therapy, typically ampicillin plus
    ceftriaxone or an aminoglycoside depending on gentamicin susceptibility.
  </p>
</div>

    <li>
      <em>Enterococcus gallinarum</em> carries <strong>intrinsic VanC-type vancomycin
      resistance</strong> — constitutive, chromosomally encoded, and non-transferable.
      It is a species-level trait, not an acquired resistance driven by antibiotic
      pressure.
    </li>
    <li>
      VanC resistance produces <strong>low-level vancomycin resistance</strong>
      (MIC 2–32 &#956;g/mL) with preserved teicoplanin susceptibility. VanA produces
      high-level resistance to both vancomycin and teicoplanin; VanB produces variable
      vancomycin resistance with teicoplanin susceptibility — similar to VanC
      phenotypically, but occurring in <em>E. faecalis/faecium</em> and
      epidemiologically significant.
    </li>
    <li>
      The automated laboratory flag <strong>"VRE" does not apply to <em>E. gallinarum</em></strong>:
      always confirm species identity alongside susceptibility results. MALDI-TOF is
      the standard method for species identification and reliably distinguishes
      <em>E. gallinarum</em> from <em>E. faecalis</em> and <em>E. faecium</em>.
    </li>
    <li>
      <em>E. gallinarum</em> is nearly universally <strong>ampicillin-susceptible</strong>.
      Ampicillin is the drug of choice — there is no indication to escalate to
      linezolid or daptomycin when the isolate is ampicillin-susceptible.
    </li>
    <li>
      <strong>No VRE contact precautions are required</strong> for VanC-type enterococci.
      The VanC gene is non-transferable and carries no epidemiological risk of
      institutional spread. Implementing VRE isolation for these patients wastes
      resources and harms patients through unnecessary isolation.
    </li>
    <li>
      Cirrhosis is a recognized risk factor for <em>E. gallinarum</em> bacteremia
      through gut barrier dysfunction and enterococcal translocation. Always evaluate
      for an occult source in the GI or hepatobiliary tract.
