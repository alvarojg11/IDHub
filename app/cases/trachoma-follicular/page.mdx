<h2 className="text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900 mb-6">
  Rationale
</h2>

<div className="mx-auto max-w-5xl">
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Trachoma is caused by ocular infection with <em>Chlamydia trachomatis</em>
    serovars A, B, Ba, and C, obligate intracellular bacteria that infect
    conjunctival epithelial cells. It is the world's leading infectious cause
    of blindness, responsible for visual impairment in approximately 1.9 million
    people and active infection in an estimated 125 million worldwide. It is
    hyperendemic in sub-Saharan Africa, South Asia, and parts of the Middle
    East and Latin America, concentrated in communities with poverty, crowding,
    inadequate water access, and poor sanitation. Ethiopia bears one of the
    highest national burdens globally. Transmission occurs by direct contact
    with ocular or nasal discharge from infected individuals, via fomites, and
    through the eye-seeking fly <em>Musca sorbens</em>, which is attracted to
    ocular discharge in young children.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    The WHO simplified grading system classifies trachoma into five signs:
    trachomatous follicular inflammation (TF, five or more follicles on the
    upper tarsal conjunctiva), trachomatous intense inflammation (TI, intense
    inflammatory thickening obscuring more than half the deep tarsal vessels),
    trachomatous conjunctival scarring (TS, fibrous white bands or sheets on
    the tarsal conjunctiva), trachomatous trichiasis (TT, one or more lashes
    touching the globe), and corneal opacity (CO, easily visible corneal
    opacity over the pupil). TF and TI represent active, potentially
    reversible infection; TS, TT, and CO represent the sequelae of repeated
    infection. Children aged 1 to 9 are the primary infected reservoir;
    women bear a disproportionate burden of trichiasis and blindness, likely
    because of prolonged contact with infected children.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    The WHO SAFE strategy provides a comprehensive framework for trachoma
    elimination: Surgery for trichiasis (bilamellar tarsal rotation to
    correct in-turned lashes before corneal damage occurs), Antibiotics
    via community-wide MDA (azithromycin single oral dose annually until
    TF prevalence in children 1 to 9 falls below 5%), Facial cleanliness
    (reducing transmission via flies and contact), and Environmental
    improvement (water, sanitation, and hygiene). Azithromycin MDA reduces
    community infection prevalence, slows progression to scarring, and when
    sustained over several rounds, can eliminate trachoma as a public health
    problem. Countries including Morocco, Oman, and Ghana have achieved
    WHO validation of trachoma elimination through sustained SAFE
    implementation.
  </p>
</div>
