<h2 className="text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900 mb-6">
  Rationale
</h2>

<div className="mx-auto max-w-5xl">
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Interferon-gamma release assays (IGRAs) such as the QuantiFERON-TB Gold
    Plus measure the IFN-gamma response of CD4 T lymphocytes to
    Mycobacterium tuberculosis-specific antigens (ESAT-6 and CFP-10) as
    an indirect marker of TB infection. The assay requires two functional
    elements: antigen-specific T cells sensitized by prior mycobacterial
    exposure, and a competent lymphocyte pool capable of producing IFN-gamma
    when stimulated. The mitogen control tube confirms the latter by
    non-specifically activating T cells with a polyclonal mitogen. In
    advanced HIV, both elements may be compromised. Low CD4 counts reduce
    the absolute number of functional T cells capable of generating an
    IFN-gamma signal, and even if TB-specific sensitized cells exist, the
    mitogen control may fail because the total functional lymphocyte pool is
    depleted. An indeterminate result therefore does not indicate absence of
    TB infection; rather, it indicates that the immune response is too
    impaired to be reliably interrogated. The pre-test probability of LTBI
    in this patient is high: he shares a small apartment with a smear-positive
    index case and has been exposed continuously for the weeks before his
    partner's diagnosis was established.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Managing LTBI in a person with newly diagnosed HIV requires simultaneous
    attention to ART initiation, drug interactions, and LTBI regimen
    selection. Both ART and LTBI treatment should be started in close
    temporal proximity, as advanced HIV-mediated immunosuppression increases
    the risk of progression from latent to active TB. The most clinically
    significant interaction is between rifamycins and integrase strand
    transfer inhibitors (INSTIs). Rifampin and rifapentine are potent
    inducers of CYP3A4 and UGT1A1, which are the primary metabolic pathways
    for most INSTIs. Dolutegravir is the INSTI with the most evidence
    supporting dose adjustment (50 mg twice daily) during rifamycin
    co-administration, derived from pharmacokinetic studies and clinical
    experience in TB-HIV co-treatment. Bictegravir lacks pharmacokinetic
    data supporting dose escalation and is pharmacologically contraindicated.
    Elvitegravir/cobicistat combinations are incompatible with rifamycins
    due to cobicistat's inability to maintain adequate boosting in the
    presence of potent induction. If no rifamycin-compatible INSTI can be
    used, isoniazid monotherapy for 9 months (9H) carries no significant
    ART interactions and remains a guideline-endorsed alternative.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    The landscape of LTBI treatment has shifted substantially with the
    availability of short-course rifamycin-based regimens. The BRIEF-TB
    trial established 1HP (28 days of daily rifapentine plus isoniazid) as
    non-inferior to 9H in HIV-positive patients, with a superior safety
    profile and markedly improved completion rates attributable to the
    shorter duration. For patients in whom daily adherence for 28 days is
    more achievable than 9 months, 1HP represents a practical advance.
    The 3HP regimen (once-weekly rifapentine plus isoniazid for 12 weeks)
    is another short-course alternative with strong evidence in HIV,
    requiring similar ART adjustments (DTG 50 mg BID with weekly rifapentine
    is recommended by DHHS, though some pharmacokinetic data suggest the
    interaction is milder than with daily rifapentine). Pyridoxine
    supplementation at 25 to 50 mg daily is essential with any
    isoniazid-containing regimen to prevent peripheral neuropathy, which
    is more common in HIV-positive patients due to baseline neural
    vulnerability from the virus itself and its metabolic sequelae.
  </p>
</div>
