<h2 className="text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900 mb-6">
  Rationale
</h2>

<div className="mx-auto max-w-5xl">
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Post-transplant lymphoproliferative disorder is a heterogeneous group of lymphoid proliferations
    arising as a consequence of immunosuppression after solid organ transplantation. EBV plays a
    central pathogenetic role in early-onset PTLD: calcineurin inhibitors (tacrolimus, cyclosporine)
    suppress the EBV-specific cytotoxic T-lymphocyte response that normally limits EBV-infected
    B-cell expansion. In EBV-seronegative recipients who receive an organ from an EBV-seropositive
    donor (D+/R-), primary EBV infection occurs in the setting of profound immunosuppression, and
    infected B cells can proliferate without effective immune control.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Heart and lung recipients have among the highest PTLD rates of any solid organ group (approximately
    2 to 8%), reflecting the greater depth of immunosuppression required to prevent rejection in
    thoracic transplants. The EBV D+/R- mismatch confers the highest individual risk, with PTLD
    rates up to 10 to 20 times higher than in EBV-matched transplants. Constitutional B symptoms
    (fever, night sweats, weight loss), a mass lesion with mediastinal adenopathy, and elevated LDH
    in this clinical context are a red flag for PTLD. The lung is one of the most common sites of
    extranodal PTLD involvement.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Tissue biopsy is the cornerstone of diagnosis. PTLD encompasses a histologic spectrum: from early
    lesions (plasmacytic hyperplasia, infectious mononucleosis-like) to polymorphic PTLD to monomorphic
    PTLD, which accounts for approximately 60 to 70% of cases in solid organ transplant recipients and
    most commonly presents as diffuse large B-cell lymphoma. EBER in situ hybridization is the gold
    standard for demonstrating EBV involvement within tumor cells; CD20 positivity confirms eligibility
    for rituximab. These data can only come from tissue: serum EBV PCR supports clinical suspicion but
    does not replace histologic classification or immunophenotyping.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    For CD20-positive monomorphic PTLD, the current standard first-line approach is IS reduction
    combined with rituximab. IS reduction (reducing or holding mycophenolate, decreasing tacrolimus
    by 25 to 50%) partially restores EBV-specific cytotoxic T-cell surveillance and can achieve
    complete remission in approximately 20 to 40% of patients with lower-risk disease. Rituximab
    (anti-CD20 monoclonal antibody) targets the CD20-expressing lymphoma cells directly. The pivotal
    PTLD-1 trial (Trappe et al., 2017) demonstrated that a sequential strategy of four cycles of
    rituximab followed by risk-stratified consolidation (additional rituximab for responders, or
    R-CHOP for non-responders) achieved event-free survival of 54% at three years. This sequential
    approach avoids routine upfront chemotherapy while preserving the option to escalate. R-CHOP is
    reserved for disease refractory to IS reduction plus rituximab, or for high-risk presentations
    at diagnosis.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    In cardiac transplant recipients, IS reduction must be carefully balanced against the risk of
    allograft rejection: the heart cannot be bridged or supported the way a kidney can if graft
    function is lost. Close coordination with transplant cardiology is essential, and rejection
    surveillance with endomyocardial biopsy may be warranted during the IS reduction period.
  </p>
</div>
