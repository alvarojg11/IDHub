Her Crohn disease has been difficult to control over the past year, with worsening diarrhea, abdominal cramping, weight loss, and a draining perirectal fistula. She is currently taking intermittent prednisone tapers and mesalamine. She has no diabetes, peripheral vascular disease, injection drug use, animal bites, freshwater exposure, or recent travel.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Three weeks before admission, she noticed a tender pustule on the anterior right shin that she attributed to minor trauma from bumping the leg against a coffee table. Over several days it opened into a painful ulcer with surrounding erythema. She was treated in urgent care with oral cephalexin, then trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, without improvement. She presented to the emergency department when the lesion doubled in size over 48 hours and developed a dark necrotic-appearing center. Initial concern was for a complicated skin and soft tissue infection. Vancomycin and piperacillin-tazobactam were started, and surgical debridement was performed.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    The wound worsened after the procedure. The debrided margins became more violaceous and undermined, and new satellite pustules appeared along the incision line. Over the next week she underwent two additional debridements for presumed uncontrolled infection, each followed by further enlargement of the ulcer. Blood cultures remained negative. Operative Gram stain showed many neutrophils but no organisms, and tissue cultures grew only rare coagulase-negative staphylococci from enrichment broth. Fungal and acid-fast bacilli stains were negative. CT angiography of the leg showed soft tissue edema but no gas, drainable abscess, arterial occlusion, or osteomyelitis.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    On infectious diseases consultation, she is afebrile with heart rate 94/min, blood pressure 124/76 mmHg, and oxygen saturation 99% on room air. There is no crepitus, bullous hemorrhage, anesthesia, lymphangitic streaking, or pain extending beyond the visible border. Laboratory studies show white blood cell count 12.4 x 10^3/uL, hemoglobin 10.1 g/dL, platelet count 520 x 10^3/uL, C-reactive protein 118 mg/L, erythrocyte sedimentation rate 88 mm/h, creatinine 0.7 mg/dL, and albumin 2.8 g/dL. Dermatology performs an incisional biopsy from the active ulcer edge. <h2 className="text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900 mb-6">
  Rationale
</h2>

<div className="mx-auto max-w-5xl">
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Pyoderma gangrenosum is a neutrophilic dermatosis that sits directly in the diagnostic blind spot of infectious diseases practice. It looks infected: the ulcer can be erythematous, exquisitely painful, necrotic-appearing, draining, and surrounded by edema. Yet the primary process is not microbial invasion. It is dysregulated innate inflammation with neutrophilic tissue injury, often linked to systemic inflammatory disease. Inflammatory bowel disease is one of the classic associations, along with inflammatory arthritis, hematologic disorders, and autoinflammatory syndromes. This patient's active fistulizing Crohn disease, severe intestinal inflammatory burden, pustule-to-ulcer evolution, violaceous undermined border, and sterile neutrophilic biopsy create the diagnostic pattern.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    The most important clue is pathergy, the paradoxical worsening of lesions after minor trauma. PG may begin after a bump, venipuncture, surgery, biopsy, or debridement. In this case, every attempt to surgically clean the wound made it larger and more inflamed. That trajectory is not typical of an inadequately debrided abscess or necrotizing infection. It is a warning that the host inflammatory response, not bacterial burden, is driving tissue destruction. Debridement is not absolutely forbidden in every PG case, especially if there is frank necrosis or a true superinfection, but aggressive operative debridement during active uncontrolled PG can be disastrous.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Diagnosis is clinicopathologic. The 2018 Delphi consensus criteria for ulcerative PG emphasize biopsy of the ulcer edge showing neutrophilic infiltrate as a major criterion, supported by exclusion of infection, pathergy, history of inflammatory bowel disease or inflammatory arthritis, pustule or papule that rapidly ulcerates, peripheral erythema with undermined tender border, multiple ulcers or anterior lower leg involvement, cribriform scarring, and response to immunosuppression. No single feature is perfectly specific. Histology can be supportive, but it is not a magic stain for PG. The biopsy is equally valuable because it searches for mimics: vasculitis, malignancy, deep fungal infection, mycobacterial infection, ecthyma gangrenosum, vascular ulceration, and cutaneous Crohn disease.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    The ID decision is not whether infection is ever possible. Superinfection and colonization can occur in PG ulcers. The decision is whether infection explains the entire syndrome and whether antibiotics or surgery are helping. In this patient, cultures were unrevealing, imaging showed no abscess, gas, osteomyelitis, or vascular occlusion, and the wound worsened after trauma. Rare coagulase-negative staphylococci in broth should not be allowed to anchor the team to a bacterial diagnosis. Antibiotics should be targeted to objective infection, not continued indefinitely because an inflammatory ulcer looks purulent.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Treatment requires stopping the cycle of trauma and suppressing inflammation. Severe ulcerative PG is commonly treated with systemic corticosteroids for rapid control or cyclosporine as an alternative. The STOP GAP randomized trial found no major difference in healing speed between prednisolone and cyclosporine overall, so the choice is often driven by comorbidities and toxicity. In Crohn-associated PG, anti-TNF therapy is especially attractive. Infliximab has trial and observational evidence in PG and treats fistulizing Crohn disease, making it a rational steroid-sparing therapy after infection screening and exclusion of uncontrolled wound infection. Wound care should be atraumatic, with nonadherent dressings, pain control, edema management when appropriate, and careful multidisciplinary follow-up.
  </p>
</div>

    </li>

    </li>
    <li>
      Severe PG is treated with systemic immunosuppression and atraumatic wound care. In active Crohn disease, anti-TNF therapy such as infliximab can address both the intestinal disease and the skin manifestation, often with corticosteroids used for rapid initial control.
