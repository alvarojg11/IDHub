<h2 className="text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900 mb-6">
  Rationale
</h2>

<div className="mx-auto max-w-5xl">
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Legionella pneumonia often announces itself through features that do not quite fit routine community-acquired pneumonia. Severe fever, multilobar involvement, diarrhea, confusion, hyponatremia, transaminitis, renal injury, and poor response to beta-lactam therapy should prompt concern for an intracellular atypical pathogen. The plumbing disruption is not a throwaway detail: Legionella persists in warm water systems and biofilms, and aerosolized water from showers, cooling systems, fountains, or health care plumbing can seed infection.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    The negative urine antigen is the central trap. Legionella urinary antigen testing is rapid and useful, but most commonly used assays primarily detect <em>Legionella pneumophila</em> serogroup 1. A negative result does not exclude infection from other <em>L. pneumophila</em> serogroups or other Legionella species. This matters most when the patient is severely ill, immunocompromised, linked to a possible water-system exposure, or part of a potential cluster where public health investigation may depend on identifying the organism.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    When suspicion remains high, lower respiratory tract testing should be pursued. Polymerase chain reaction can detect Legionella beyond the scope of many urine antigen assays, while culture on buffered charcoal yeast extract agar allows species and serogroup identification and supports outbreak investigation. Because Legionella is intracellular, treatment requires an agent with reliable intracellular activity. Azithromycin or a respiratory fluoroquinolone is appropriate; in this severe immunosuppressed case, levofloxacin is a strong best answer.
  </p>
</div>
