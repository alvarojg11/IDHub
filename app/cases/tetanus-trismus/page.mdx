<CaseReveal pollId="case-tetanus-trismus-q2">
<h2 className="text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900 mb-6">
  Rationale
</h2>

<div className="mx-auto max-w-5xl">
  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Tetanus is a vaccine-preventable neurologic disease caused by tetanospasmin, the
    toxin produced by <em>Clostridium tetani</em>. Spores are widespread in the environment,
    especially in soil, and can enter the body through even minor wounds or abrasions.
    Because infection does not confer immunity, patients with uncertain or incomplete
    vaccination remain at risk throughout life.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Early tetanus is a clinical diagnosis. Trismus is a classic early manifestation
    because the muscles of mastication are often affected first. Patients can have a
    normal initial examination and then deteriorate over the next several days as toxin
    already taken up into nerves continues to travel centrally. This is why treatment
    should begin on suspicion rather than waiting for generalized spasms to make the
    diagnosis obvious.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    In an older patient with jaw symptoms, giant cell arteritis remains an important
    urgent alternative diagnosis because jaw claudication can precede vision-threatening
    complications; obtaining an ESR is therefore reasonable. However, giant cell arteritis
    does not explain the broader epidemiologic context as well as tetanus in this case,
    and checking an ESR should not delay empiric treatment for suspected tetanus.
  </p>

  <p className="text-lg leading-6 text-gray-800 mb-6 text-justify">
    Immediate management includes tetanus immune globulin to neutralize circulating toxin,
    metronidazole as preferred antimicrobial therapy, careful wound search with cleaning
    and debridement when appropriate, and close monitoring in a low-stimulation environment.
    Progressive dysphagia, tachypnea, or spasms should prompt escalation of care because
    laryngeal spasm and respiratory compromise can develop rapidly. Benzodiazepines are
    first-line agents for spasm control once spasms occur, and severe cases may need airway
    intervention, tracheostomy, or mechanical ventilation. After stabilization, the patient
    should also begin a full tetanus toxoid vaccination series because the illness itself
    does not provide reliable future protection.
  </p>
</div>
